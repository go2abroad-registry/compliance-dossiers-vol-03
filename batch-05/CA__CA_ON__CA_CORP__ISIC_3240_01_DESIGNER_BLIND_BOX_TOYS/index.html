Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Federal tax administration; Ontario corporate income tax is collected by CRA under a federal-provincial tax collection agreement. Principal registration portal is CRA 'My Business Account'; corporate registry for Ontario is the Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery (Companies Directorate).

E-government filing portal

Foreign non-resident corporations must apply to CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Form RC141C (Application for a Business Number or RC141U for non-resident use) or by calling CRA International Tax Services. Once incorporated federally or provincially, the corporation receives a 9-digit Ontario Corporation Number which is linked to the federal BN during the incorporation process. The BN is the unique tax identifier for income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and import-export activities. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada may register before starting business; registration for GST/HST is mandatory when worldwide taxable supplies exceed C$30,000 and is available voluntarily for lower turnover. Supporting documents typically include certificate of incorporation, articles, appointment of agent for service, and evidence of a permanent establishment in Ontario if required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Territorial_Scope: Resident Canadian corporation taxed on worldwide income, with foreign tax credits available. Ontario corporate income tax is assessed on taxable income allocated to Ontario based on a permanent establishment and a sales/gross revenue apportionment formula. This is not a pass-through entity; however, a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may access the federal small business deduction and Ontario small business deduction if it meets status tests in section 125 of the Income Tax Act.

Available election forms

No 'check-the-box' or elective pass-through mechanism exists under Canadian law. Available statutory elections include: (i) voluntary GST/HST registration under ETA s. 221(1) for a small supplier; (ii) fiscal year-end election under section 249.1 for an offshore parent-controlled corporation; (iii) non-resident election under section 216 to file Part I tax on rental income on a net basis; (iv) partnership and agency election under section 252(1) in limited circumstances; (v) anti-hybrid rule in section 94B and 18.2/18.3 is mandatory, not elective, but corporations must file an information return if a hybrid mismatch arrangement is present; (vi) provincial filing coordination under the Ontario Corporations Information Act with annual return and Ontario allocation filings via CRA OneFile tax package.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 annual worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies (both zero-rated and taxable). When worldwide taxable supplies exceed C$30,000 in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, GST/HST registration is mandatory. Registration threshold applies to non-residents as well; importation of goods for commercial sale requires registration to avoid customs hold if the small-supplier threshold is exceeded. Voluntary registration is possible below C$30,000 and should be considered to recover input tax credits on establishment costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under ETA Schedule VI Part V for goods supplied to a non-resident when the goods are exported from Canada as part of the supply chain and proof of export is obtained. The supplier or the client must export the goods within a reasonable time after the supply, generally within 60 days for standard export documentation, and the supplier must retain evidence such as a customs bill of lading or warehouse receipt showing the goods left Canada. Services are zero-rated where they are performed wholly outside Canada or are ancillary to the export of goods; numerically, section 7(a) of Part V covers services in relation to tangible personal property that is exported, and section 23 covers services to a non-resident in respect of real property outside Canada or for enforcing or protecting a right outside Canada. For blind-box toy merchandise, zero-rating attaches only when the legal title and physical possession pass to the non-resident customer and the goods are delivered to a common carrier for immediate export or are sent through international mail/parcel service.

  • Customs Export Declaration – Canada Border Services Agency form B13A or the ACI/eManifest export report filed through a customs broker or approved portal.
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, rail, or truck document issued by the carrier indicating Canadian port of departure and foreign destination).
  • Inward Remittance Certificate – bank SWIFT or wire transfer confirmation showing foreign currency payment attributable to the shipment (not mandatory for zero-rating but commonly requested in audits).
  • Commercial invoice of the Canadian seller describing the 10-digit HS tariff classification (e.g., 9503.00.00) and unit values.
  • Packing list matching the commercial invoice and the physical goods in the container/pallet.
  • Proof of foreign import entry (destination customs declaration or goods arrival report) where available, especially for B2B high-value shipments.

Canada does not currently operate a real-time electronic invoicing or continuous transaction control clearance regime. There is no requirement to submit invoices to the CRA before issuing them, no API-based fiscal stamping, and no digital signature arrangement is mandatory. Electronic invoices are permitted and must contain prescribed statutory data (ETA s. 169, 172.1) such as vendor registration number, transaction date, description, and separate HST amount. CRA is participating in the Pan-Canadian e-invoicing stream through the Digital Interchange Association (PEPPOL), but use is optional and does not affect GST/HST liability or clearance.

CRA may re-assess an export zero-rating claim within the 4-year normal reassessment period, or 10 years for misrepresentation attributable to neglect or fraud. To defend a zero-rating claim, exporters must produce contemporaneous proof of export; CRA rejects photocopies without underlying bills of lading and often requires the shipping carrier's online flight/vessel record. If zero-rating is denied, the full 13% HST becomes payable on the invoiced amount plus interest computed as the CRA prescribed daily compound rate, and a person may be liable to a penalty of 5% of the net tax, with an additional 1% per month for a missing statement or omission. Gross negligence penalties can reach 50% of the understated net tax under ETA s. 285.1(2). Where a false claim is made deliberately, tax evasion under criminal provisions can lead to fines of up to 250% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years. CRA also has the power to claw back input tax credits for the full documentary deficiency when the exporter cannot establish that the goods left Canada. Note: No statutory FX repatriation deadline exists in Canada; this field is set to 0 as a formal placeholder. Export proceeds should be reported in Canadian dollars at the CRA posted rate, but there is no 180-day recovery requirement.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully liberalized floating exchange-rate regime under the Currency Act, with no exchange-control legislation. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are freely convertible in CAD, USD, and all major currencies; no government approval is required for foreign investment, dividend repatriation, or capital injection. Profit remittance is unrestricted, but dividends and certain payments to non-residents are subject to 25% Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, typically reduced to 5-15% under applicable tax treaties; remittances must be supported by board resolutions, beneficial-ownership attestations, and transfer-pricing documentation to withstand surplus-stripping audits.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory timeline for the surrender of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio. Export receipts may be retained in CAD or foreign currency in any Schedule I or II bank account without restriction. The mandatory conversion ratio is 0%. Documentary evidence of export transactions (sales contracts, commercial invoices, CBSA customs B3 entries, bills of lading, and related FX contracts) must be retained for six years for Income Tax Act and GST/HST audit purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority and lender of last resort; prudential regulation of banks delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing supervision conducted by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); domestic clearing and settlement oversight through Payments Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-Medium viability. Offshore digital EMIs and foreign fintech payment platforms (UK FCA EMIs, EU EMIs, US-chartered neobanks) are not authorized deposit-taking institutions in Canada and cannot directly maintain domestic CAD clearing accounts within the Payments Canada ACSS/LVTS rails. They may act only as auxiliary settlement conduits via a Canadian Schedule I partner bank. Any payment-service provider dealing with Canadian clients must register with FINTRAC as a money services business under the PCMLTFA and obtain a licence under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); unlicensed offshore EMIs create immediate onboarding, sanctions, and operational risk.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal/Ontario general corporate rate: 26.5% (15% federal Part I + 11.5% Ontario general rate).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless a Double Taxation Agreement (DTA) reduces the rate.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the corporation's statutory fiscal year-end, irrespective of the tax balance due date.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Line 100 / Part I – Net Income Report gross revenue, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, and other income from the sale of designer blind-box toys and related merchandising. Non-resident owned Canadian corporations must report Canadian-source business income carried on through a permanent establishment; income earned wholly outside Canada is generally excluded unless allocated to a Canadian PE. Schedule 1 – Net Income (Loss) for Income Tax Purposes Reconcile accounting net income to tax net income by adding back non-deductible expenses and deducting statutory allowances and capital cost allowance. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Ontario must ensure that all capital gains on taxable Canadian property are included and that Canadian branch profits are correctly reconciled. Late filing penalty is 5% of any unpaid balance plus 1% per month for up to 12 months; repeated failures trigger a penalty of 10% plus 2% per month for up to 12 months. Gross negligence penalty is 50% of the understated tax or overstated loss. Form T106 failure attracts a penalty of at least CA$1,000 and up to CA$100 per day per failure, with a maximum of approximately CA$24,000.
Schedule 400 – Ontario Corporation Tax Compute Ontario taxable income, apply the Ontario allocation formula based on permanent establishment establishment in Ontario, and calculate Ontario corporate tax before credits. A non-resident corporation with a permanent establishment in Ontario must file Schedule 400 to allocate worldwide active business income to Ontario using the two-factor formula and claim applicable Ontario tax credits. Late filing penalty is 5% of any unpaid balance plus 1% per month for up to 12 months; repeated failures trigger a penalty of 10% plus 2% per month for up to 12 months. Gross negligence penalty is 50% of the understated tax or overstated loss. Form T106 failure attracts a penalty of at least CA$1,000 and up to CA$100 per day per failure, with a maximum of approximately CA$24,000.
Form T106 – Information Return of Non-Arm's-Length Transactions with Non-Residents Disclose all reportable transactions between the Canadian corporation and each related non-resident, including licensing of toy designs, procurement, distribution, and financing charges. Applies to non-resident owned Canadian corporations when aggregate cross-border transactions with a single related non-resident exceed CA$1,000,000 in the fiscal year; attach transfer pricing schedules and supporting documentation. Late filing penalty is 5% of any unpaid balance plus 1% per month for up to 12 months; repeated failures trigger a penalty of 10% plus 2% per month for up to 12 months. Gross negligence penalty is 50% of the understated tax or overstated loss. Form T106 failure attracts a penalty of at least CA$1,000 and up to CA$100 per day per failure, with a maximum of approximately CA$24,000.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CA$1,000,000 aggregate non-arm's-length cross-border transaction value triggers Form T106 reporting; contemporaneous TP documentation (Local File / Master File equivalent) is expected for taxpayers with gross revenue of CA$10,000,000 or more or upon CRA written request; Country-by-Country Reporting applies to Canadian multinationals with consolidated group revenue of approximately CA$1.1 billion (€750 million threshold).

Economic substance scope: Canada applies an economic substance test via the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act) and the permanent establishment doctrine; a corporation must demonstrate real commercial activity, physical presence, operating substance, personnel, assets, and decision-making in Ontario to withstand CRA challenge and to claim treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Income Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction carries imprisonment up to 5 years and a fine of 100% to 200% of the evaded tax; tax evasion penalties include 50% gross negligence penalties and compound interest on reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for uncollected source deductions and GST/HST under section 227.1; criminal wrongdoing can expose directors to restitution orders, travel bans, and incarceration.
Shell Company / Artificial Entity

Statutory sanction: CRA can disregard the shell under GAAR, resassess the enterprise at the operating owner level, deny treaty-based withholding reductions, and impose interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors of a factually hollow shell may be disallowed limited liability and held personally accountable for taxes on benefits received, with possible asset seizure and court-imposed director disqualification.
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative fines up to CA$500,000 per violation for a corporation, and up to CA$2,000,000 for a repeated violation; criminal prosecution possible for knowingly failing to report.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors who authorize inadequate AML frameworks can incur personal fines up to CA$100,000 each, disqualification from board service, and in criminal cases, imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Penalty and False Documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reallocate income and apply a 10% transfer pricing penalty under section 247(3) when the net adjustment exceeds the lesser of $5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue; plus full tax and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false TP certificates or knowingly omit cross-border transactions are exposed to gross negligence penalties assessed in their own name, director liability proceedings, and corporate veil lifting in fraud cases.
Unremitted Part XIII Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: The withholding tax is payable to CRA directly by the payer company, plus 7% annual interest and late-filing penalties; CRA may also assess a corporation for the unreported amount and stop collection offsets.

Director personal liability: Directors are deemed personally liable for the unremitted Part XIII withholding amount, interest, and penalties if they knew or ought to have known of the non-remittance and did not exercise reasonable diligence.
