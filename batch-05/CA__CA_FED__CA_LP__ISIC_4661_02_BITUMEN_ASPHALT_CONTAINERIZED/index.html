Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — Federal tax and GST/HST administration; primary portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); general official portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html

E-government filing portal

A non-resident Limited Partnership (LP) carrying on business in Canada must register for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account (code 'RT') with the CRA. Registration is completed online through the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal, by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), or by completing the GST/HST Internet Registration page. The LP must provide proof of existence under foreign law, the partnership agreement, identification and Canadian contact or agent for service of the general partner, and a description of Canadian business activities. A GST/HST account is generally issued immediately upon online filing; the BN is attached to the partnership itself. The LP must also file annual partnership information returns (Form T5013, Schedule 50) for each fiscal period, even if the LP has no Canadian source income. If the LP has no Canadian establishment, the CRA may require a simplified non-resident registration procedure and a section 96 election designating a representative resident partner.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada applies statutory look-through treatment: an LP is not a separate taxpayer for Income Tax Act purposes. Each partner is taxed on the allocable share of partnership income or loss under section 96. No general check-the-box election exists in Canada; classification follows the legal form under Canadian law, modified by Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ss. 18.2, 18.4, 12.5, 96, 138.5) implementing BEPS Action 2. For cross-border financing, taxpayers may make an elective deduction-disallowance election under s. 18.4 or rely on the 'reasonable characterization' rule under s. 138.5 for foreign insurance. Foreign entity characterization follows CRA administrative policy: a foreign partnership that is a body corporate under foreign law is still treated as a partnership if it has the attributes of a Canadian partnership under s. 96(4) and 96(5). A foreign LP may, in certain dual-resident structures, file a joint election under subsection 96(5) to compute income in Canadian dollars. GST/HST elections: a non-resident registrant can elect under section 272.1 of the Excise Tax Act for simplified quarterly reporting and elect with a resident agent for CRA service (s. 307).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — A non-resident LP that carries on business in Canada is a 'small supplier' exempt from mandatory GST/HST registration only if the value of its worldwide taxable supplies (including associates) in the current calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters does not exceed CAD 30,000 (Excise Tax Act, s. 148 and s. 240). Once the CAD 30,000 threshold (all jurisdictions worldwide) is exceeded, registration becomes mandatory and the LP must charge and collect GST/HST at 5% federal rate (plus applicable HST provincial rates where the supply is made in a harmonized province: 13% ON, 15% NL/NS/PE/NB, 14% PE pre-2024 adjustments; BC PST applies separately in non-harmonized provinces). For pure offshore supplies to non-resident consignees with no Canadian nexus, no registration is required; however, the LP's containerized bitumen distribution from Canadian warehouses creates a taxable supply in Canada and triggers the threshold test.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible personal property—including drummed and bagged penetration grade bitumen/asphalt—are zero-rated (0% GST/HST) under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier ships the goods to a destination outside Canada in the course of commercial transportation (by common carrier, mail, courier, or via customs broker) and title passes to the consignee outside Canada. For a containerized wholesale supply under ISIC 4661/NAICS 424720, the CRA requires that the goods be exported within a reasonable time after sale and that the supplier maintains contemporaneous evidence that the goods physically left Canada. Offshore services related to the sale (e.g., freight, handling, logistics, and distribution services supplied to non-residents) are zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI where the services are performed for a non-resident and are not for consumption in Canada. Bitumen/asphalt goods are classified under HS heading 2713 (petroleum bitumen) or 2715 (bituminous mixtures); the zero-rating applies irrespective of HS classification, provided the property is tangible personal property. Where the foreign purchaser takes delivery in Canada and exports within 60 days, the supply may still be zero-rated under s. 1.2 of Part V if the purchaser is not registered and the goods are exported.

  • Canadian Export Declaration — Form B13A / CAED electronic export declaration filed with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) containing customs entry number, date of export, HS classification (2713/2715), quantity, weight, and value
  • Bill of Lading — original ocean/rail/truck waybill evidencing shipment from a Canadian port to a foreign port, with consignee outside Canada and title-transfer terms (FOB or CIF)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank credit advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds in the LP's account
  • Commercial Invoice (CRA-compliant) with full description of the bitumen goods, unit price, terms of sale, and statement that goods are destined outside Canada
  • Freight forwarder's certificate of export or customs broker's export confirmation dated within 60 days of supply
  • Container tracking / terminal receipts evidencing movement of the container to a foreign destination
  • T5013 partnership information return records and intercompany agreements to substantiate the LP's Canadian selling entity role

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or government pre-clearance of e-invoices. The CRA accepts commercial invoices in paper or electronic format; no Peppol clearance gate is mandatory, although participation in the Pan-Canadian Peppol BIS 3.0 e-invoicing network is voluntary and recommended. A GST/HST registrant must file returns electronically via CRA's GST/HST Internet File Transfer system (monthly, quarterly, or annual based on revenue) and must retain invoice, shipping, and customs documentation for six years. The CBSA operates the Canadian Export Declaration system (CAED / CANPGS) for electronic transmission of export declarations; failure to submit an accurate export declaration may result in administrative penalties under the Customs Act. There is no API-fiscal clearance requirement for exports; invoice data is not transmitted to a central clearance platform.

False or unsupported export zero-rating claims expose the LP to reassessment of unpaid GST/HST at 5% plus interest retroactive to the reporting period, and loss of input tax credits. Under s. 296(1) of the Excise Tax Act, the CRA may reassess beyond the normal four-year limitation period where a person has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default. Civil penalties under s. 327(1) apply for false or deceptive statements: the greater of CAD 250 and 10% of the tax that would have been evaded. Gross negligence penalties under s. 327.1 reach 50% of the tax arising from the false statement. Criminal prosecution for wilful evasion or making false declarations (s. 327(1)(a) and s. 328 ETA; s. 153 Customs Act) is punishable by fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction or five years on indictment. Where the CRA determines that goods were not actually exported or title did not pass outside Canada, the zero-rating is reversed, GST is assessed with arrears interest at the prescribed rate, and the exporter must produce shipping and remittance documentation within 30 days of demand; failure to do so triggers immediate reassessment and potential seizure of goods under the Customs Act. Canada does not impose a strict statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for GST purposes, but the 180-day benchmark is used by the CRA to test whether the sale and export cycle is genuine; a longer unremitted export period increases audit risk and may prompt a specialty audit of the export documentation bundle.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains de facto full convertibility for both current and capital account transactions, including profit remittances and repatriation of foreign capital from a Canadian limited partnership. There is no exchange-rate authorization or capital-admission approval requirement for inbound investment into a Canadian LP or for outbound remittances to non-resident partners. The only statutory hurdles are tax compliance with Part XIII withholding under the Income Tax Act (25% rate, subject to treaty reduction), and AML/sanctions screening under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and the Criminal Code. Non-resident partners should expect CRS/FATCA reporting at the intermediary bank and mandatory FINTRAC large-transaction reporting for wires exceeding CAD 10.000.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation timeline for export proceeds arising from wholesale bitumen trade. Exporters may hold foreign currency, convert freely at market rates, and maintain offshore receivable accounts. However, the exporting entity must translate foreign-currency receivables into CAD at exchange rates consistent with Income Tax Regulation 2611 for financial statements, and all wire transfers over CAD 10.000 are reportable to FINTRAC. No forced sale or Central Bank of Canada pegging applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, operating in coordination with the Minister of Finance and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Canada does not have a specialized Foreign Exchange Administration; exchange control policy is conducted under the Currency Act, the Canadian Payments Act, and Canada's Article VIII obligations of the IMF. Payments Canada operates the Lynx wholesale clearing system for large-value CAD settlements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium primary viability. Canada does not grant electronic money institution (EMI) licences under a distinct EU-style regime; non-bank digital payment providers are regulated either as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC or as payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). These fintech entities can facilitate low-value fee payments, FX conversions, and non-core operational settlements, but they cannot serve as the primary treasury rail for high-value bitumen transactions because they have no direct access to Payments Canada's Lynx clearing system and cannot provide documentary letters of credit. Legal constraints include the absence of CDIC deposit insurance and the requirement that any trust account held on behalf of a client must be segregated. For commodity trading, Tier-1 chartered banks are necessary for confirmation of LC issuance and export financing.
