Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), federal tax administration under the Income Tax Act (R.S.C. 1985, c. 1, as amended) and the Excise Tax Act (R.S.C. 1985, c. E-15) for GST/HST. Primary portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; GST/HST registration and returns are filed through CRA My Business Account or GST/HST NETFILE.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian limited partnership (LP) must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST program account. Procedure: (1) execute the partnership agreement and specify the Canadian registered office and principal place of business; (2) complete extra-provincial registration in the province of operation if the LP is formed in another province or has a non-resident general partner; (3) submit CRA Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number', or apply online via Business Registration Online (BRO), providing the LP legal name, fiscal period-end, and all partner names and residency details; (4) CRA issues the BN with a two-letter account suffix (e.g., 'RT' for GST/HST or 'RC' for payroll); (5) the LP registers for the GST/HST Account and CRA My Business Account, enabling electronic returns and export-claim filing. Non-resident limited partners may hold interests without obtaining separate Canadian tax IDs; the LP BN covers the partnership registration.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — Under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act, a limited partnership is fiscally transparent: business income, losses, and tax credits are computed at the partnership level and allocated to partners according to the partnership agreement. The LP itself is not taxable on federal income tax; it must file a T5013 Partnership Information Return if it has more than five partners, or at CRA's request. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — Under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act, a limited partnership is fiscally transparent: business income, losses, and tax credits are computed at the partnership level and allocated to partners according to the partnership agreement. The LP itself is not taxable on federal income tax; it must file a T5013 Partnership Information Return if it has more than five partners, or at CRA's request. For GST/HST purposes, the registered LP is a separate 'registrant' under the Excise Tax Act and must collect and remit GST/HST on taxable supplies made in Canada.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election; classification is statutory and factual. Available LP-related elections include: (i) joint election under ITA s. 96(3) to compute capital cost allowance at the partnership level rather than on a pro-rata per-partner basis; (ii) election under ITA s. 216 for a non-resident partner to file a net-basis Part I return on certain Canadian-source income instead of Part XIII withholding; (iii) designation of the LP as a 'Canadian partnership' under ITA s. 102(1) where all partners are Canadian-resident; (iv) GST/HST drop-shipment election under ETA s. 156 for cross-border goods flow where the LP is the non-resident vendor; and (v) mandatory hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4-18.6), effective 1 July 2020, which apply automatically and are not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 148(1). A Canadian LP with taxable supplies made in Canada (including remote retail of automotive parts to Canadian consumers) is a small supplier until its total annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in the current or immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; crossing the threshold triggers mandatory GST/HST registration. Non-resident vendors of e-commerce digital or physical goods are subject to the same CAD 30,000 threshold under the 2021 simplified non-resident registration regime.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST/HST applies under Schedule VI to the ETA, specifically: (a) tangible personal property (TPP) exported by the vendor (Part V, s. 1) — automotive replacement parts and tuning accessories shipped by common carrier from Canada to a foreign address qualify if the LP is a GST/HST registrant and retains documentary proof of export; (b) supplies made to a non-resident buyer who directs the vendor to ship the goods directly to an address outside Canada (Part V, s. 3); (c) services supplied in respect of exported TPP to a non-resident, such as warranty repairs, technical tuning consultation, or remanufacturing services (Part V, ss. 13-15); and (d) supplies that would have been zero-rated had payment been received in a foreign currency or by cross-border wire transfer, provided export documentation substantiates the physical departure. The LP must not zero-rate domestic sales of parts installed in Canada, even if the buyer is a non-resident tourist.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CBSA B13A export declaration or the Canadian Export Reporting System (G4) electronic filing, showing the LP as exporter, the 10-digit HS code (e.g., 8708.99 for automotive parts), the foreign destination, and the mode of transport; a commercial invoice is required even where the export value falls below the CAD 2,000 filing threshold.
  • Bill of Lading: original or verified electronic copy (ocean bill of lading, air waybill, or FedEx/UPS/DHL shipment waybill) issued by the carrier, showing the shipment origin in Canada, the foreign consignee address, the shipment date, and a description of the goods matching the commercial invoice.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / Export Proceeds Record: a SWIFT MT103 bank credit advice, PayPal or Stripe payout transaction record, or bank account statement evidencing receipt of foreign-currency or CAD settlement from the non-resident buyer; where available, an inward remittance certificate issued by the Canadian clearing bank identifying the export sale proceeds.

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API pre-clearance, digital stamping, or government-validated continuous transaction controls at the federal level. Electronic invoices and PDFs are accepted as source documentation under ETA s. 286(1); the Pan-Canadian E-Invoice Standard (PCeHR) is voluntary. Mandatory electronic filing applies to GST/HST registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5M, filed via GST/HST NETFILE or an approved service provider. For an auto-parts e-commerce LP using Stripe and PayPal, those payment-provider records must be retained for at least six years under ETA s. 286(1) and ITA s. 230.

CRA audits export zero-rating claims under ETA ss. 169, 297, and Schedule VI documentation rules. The normal reassessment window is four years (ETA s. 298); it extends to seven years for neglect, carelessness, willful default, or fraud. Disallowed export claims are reassessed to 5% GST or 13%/15% HST plus compound interest under ETA s. 280. Penalties include: (a) false statements or omissions in a GST/HST return — CAD 250 plus 25% of the tax sought to be evaded (ETA s. 285(1)); (b) gross negligence for income-tax filings — up to 50% of the understated tax (ITA s. 163(2)); (c) criminal evasion — fines up to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to five years (ETA s. 327; ITA s. 239); and (d) application of the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245) where the LP structures export sales through a letterbox entity. While Canada does not codify a 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, CRA auditors commonly apply a 180-day benchmark to assess whether an export transaction is genuine, and prolonged non-repatriation of proceeds may trigger a full export-claim audit with retrospective clawback of zero-rating plus interest and penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely; there are no exchange-control approvals, no limits on profit repatriation, no dividend-blocking measures, and no restrictions on inflow or outflow of capital for an e-commerce limited partnership. Non-resident limited partners may contribute capital and receive distributions freely in CAD or foreign currency. The only frictions are tax-related, not currency-related: Part XIII withholding on certain distributions to non-resident partners, potential section 216 filing obligations, and CRA reporting of foreign-source income; these do not constitute convertibility or transfer restrictions. FATCA and CRS self-certifications are required at bank onboarding but do not impede convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender rules exist in Canada. There is no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to repatriate or convert export proceeds. Receipts from cross-border online sales of automotive aftermarket parts may be retained indefinitely in foreign-currency bank accounts (USD, EUR, etc.). For income tax purposes, the CRA recommends translating foreign currency receipts using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate, but this is a reporting convention, not a conversion mandate.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as central bank and monetary authority; Department of Finance (Canada) responsible for the Currency Act and exchange-rate policy framework; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises chartered banks; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers AML/ATF and cross-border EFT reporting; Payments Canada operates the Lynx RTGS and ACSS clearing systems.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH as a secondary liquidity corridor, not a primary principal bank. Canadian-registered EMIs/payment institutions such as Wise Canada (FINTRAC-registered MSB) and Stripe Treasury can hold CAD, receive merchant settlement payouts, and execute cross-currency remittances. However, under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) transitional regime implemented through the Bank of Canada (2024-2025), payment service providers in Canada must be registered with the Bank of Canada and must safeguard end-user funds in statutory trust accounts; an offshore EMI (EU/UK-licensed) that is not FINTRAC-registered and RPAA-registered cannot serve as the sole CAD clearing rail for a Canadian LP. For USD efficiency, Wise can hold USD balances as a non-deposit investment, but such funds are not CDIC-insured and are not protected under the banking insolvency framework. Recommended architecture: RBC or TD as primary CAD/USD operational bank; Wise or Stripe Treasury as a non-primary overlay for small-value cross-currency vendor payments and e-commerce payout acceleration; PayPal Business, where used, must be linked to the LP's own verified Canadian bank account, with PayPal Canada acting only as an MSB, not a bank.

CAD-value settlements clear over Lynx (Bank of Canada RTGS); USD-value settlements clear via Fedwire or CHIPS into the Canadian bank's US-dollar correspondent. No Bank of Canada approval or exchange-control permit is required for inbound capital. Mandatory records: (i) subscription agreement and LP capital account entry; (ii) FINTRAC International Electronic Funds Transfer (IEFT) report for each transfer of CAD $10,000 or more; (iii) source-of-funds evidence for all contributors; (iv) FATCA/CRS self-certifications from each non-resident partner; (v) the injection must be booked as contribution to partnership capital, not income, and must be exempt from GST/HST under the capital-contribution rules.Clearing rails: (1) For supplier payments below CAD 1,000,000 and recurring inventory restocking, use SWIFT MT103 (wire) or Cross-Border ACH (USD via the US FedGlobal/Epn; CAD via Payments Canada ACSS/Lynx); (2) For first-time or large Asian/US/European suppliers, use a Commercial Letter of Credit (SWIFT MT700) with bills of lading, commercial invoices and inspection certificates; (3) Stripe and PayPal batched net settlement payouts are routed through their Canadian acquiring/MSB arrangements directly to the LP's CAD operating account; (4) For pure domestic CAD payments to Canadian suppliers/distributors, use Payments Canada ACSS or Lynx for high-value items; (5) USD-denominated domestic payments should be cleared through the Canadian bank's US-dollar correspondent rather than converted twice; (6) All outbound wires must be screened against the Consolidated Canadian Autonomous Sanctions List, OSFI AML directives, and OFAC (if USD-clearing via US correspondents). E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH as a secondary liquidity corridor, not a primary principal bank. Canadian-registered EMIs/payment institutions such as Wise Canada (FINTRAC-registered MSB) and Stripe Treasury can hold CAD, receive merchant settlement payouts, and execute cross-currency remittances. However, under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) transitional regime implemented through the Bank of Canada (2024-2025), payment service providers in Canada must be registered with the Bank of Canada and must safeguard end-user funds in statutory trust accounts; an offshore EMI (EU/UK-licensed) that is not FINTRAC-registered and RPAA-registered cannot serve as the sole CAD clearing rail for a Canadian LP. For USD efficiency, Wise can hold USD balances as a non-deposit investment, but such funds are not CDIC-insured and are not protected under the banking insolvency framework. Recommended architecture: RBC or TD as primary CAD/USD operational bank; Wise or Stripe Treasury as a non-primary overlay for small-value cross-currency vendor payments and e-commerce payout acceleration; PayPal Business, where used, must be linked to the LP's own verified Canadian bank account, with PayPal Canada acting only as an MSB, not a bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate rate for the taxation year (combined federal-provincial general rate range of approximately 23%–27% for corporate partners; the Limited Partnership itself is fiscally transparent and not subject to Part I corporate tax as a separate taxpayer).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding on dividends deemed paid to non-residents of Canada; treaty rates commonly reduce portfolio dividends to 15% and direct substantial-holding dividends to 5%. For a Limited Partnership, distributions of business profits are not technically dividends; they are partnership allocations subject to Part I tax on Canadian business income. Withholding at source may apply under Part XIII to specific passive distributions such as interest, royalties, or rents sourced in Canada.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File preparation is mandatory where aggregate cross-border non-arm's-length transactions (goods, services, royalties, loans) exceed CAD 1,000,000 in a fiscal period; Master File is expected where such transactions aggregate above CAD 50,000,000; Country-by-Country Report filing threshold is consolidated group revenue of CAD 750,000,000 for MNE groups.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulation statute, but the CRA applies the economic-substance doctrine, the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245), and substance-over-form principles to disregard artificial transactions and deny treaty benefits. A Limited Partnership conducting online retail must show active business substance (real inventory, decision-making, employees, bank accounts) in Canada; the absence of substance will expose non-resident partners to Part XIII withholding and s.247 transfer-pricing reallocations if profits are shifted to a low-tax jurisdiction.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Understatement of Income

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax under s.163(2), reinstatement of unreported income with interest compounding, monetary fines in criminal prosecution of up to 200% of the tax evaded, and imprisonment for wilful evasion of up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for withheld and collectible amounts, including interest and penalties. Criminal conviction of senior directors or officers triggers personal criminal fines, restitution orders, travel bans pending sentencing, and imprisonment. The corporate veil is pierced where the LP was used for fraudulent tax evasion.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement for Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA denial of treaty benefits under s.245 GAAR and the principal-purpose test, reassessment of income to the beneficial owner, Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on deemed distributions, 50% gross-negligence penalties on the unreported amounts, and nominal penalty awards in civil fraudulent-conveyance proceedings.

Director personal liability: The limited-partner shield is pierced under provincial limited-partnership legislation where the limited partner takes part in control or management of the LP. Directors of the corporate general partner may be held personally liable under knowing-assistance and fraudulent-conveyance principles; courts may impose travel bans, asset freezes, and civil restitution if treaty-abusive structures conceal assets.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation of Cross-Border Auto-Parts Purchases

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the gross adjustment under s.247(3) where adequate documentation is not produced; reassessment of Canadian taxable income, denial of deduction, interest on arrears, and additional 50% penalties for false or misleading statements in price-setting records.

Director personal liability: Directors who approved exorbitant intercompany pricing without independent benchmarking expose themselves to s.227.1 liability if employees later fail to remit assessed Part XIII withholding or Part I instalments; general-party civil claims for fraudulent diversion of profits can support personal travel bans, seizure of foreign assets, and imprisonment where criminal tax evasion is proven.
Anti-Money-Laundering Compliance Failure Using E-Commerce Payment Rails

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, up to C$500,000 for a first corporate violation and up to C$1,000,000 for subsequent violations; criminal conviction can impose imprisonment up to 5 years and forfeiture of the laundered property.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly direct or authorize money-laundering through the LP face criminal liability, including civil restitution to victims, personal asset forfeiture, travel bans to prevent flight, and prison sentences. Courts may set aside the LP's liability shield to recover proceeds where limited partners actively operate the retail payment stream.
