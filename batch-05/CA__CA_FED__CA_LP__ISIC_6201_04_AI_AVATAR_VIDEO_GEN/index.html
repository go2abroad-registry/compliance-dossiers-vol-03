Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/business-registration-online.html)

E-government filing portal

Foreign non-resident entities establishing a Canadian limited partnership must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) with the CRA, either online or by mail. The BN is a unique 9-digit identifier plus a 2-letter program account suffix. For GST/HST purposes, the partnership must register for a GST/HST account using program account code 'RT'. A foreign entity with a place of business in Canada, or that makes taxable supplies in Canada, must register, but is not required to have a Canadian resident agent for GST/HST registration. Applications are processed immediately when filed online; paper applications generally process within 4–6 weeks. The CRA will issue a formal Notice of Registration specifying the effective date, reporting period, and filing frequency.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not offer U.S.-style 'check-the-box' elections. A limited partnership is statutorily classified as a fiscally transparent pass-through entity for Canadian federal income tax purposes, meaning income, gains, losses, and tax credits flow through to its partners. Partners may elect, under subsection 96(3) of the Income Tax Act, to report partnership income on a fiscal-period basis aligned with the partnership’s fiscal year, subject to required deemed year-end rules and the 'stub period' rules for tiered partnerships. Hybrid mismatch rules under Canada’s tax code may require recharacterization of certain deductible payments between the Canadian LP and its foreign partners if a hybrid arbitrage exists; Canada has enacted OECD BEPS Action 2 hybrid mismatch rules that can deny deductions or create inclusions. No partnership-level election exists to be taxed as a corporation, as Canadian limited partnerships are not eligible for corporate status. The partnership may, however, be subject to the specified investment flow-through (SIFT) rules if it is a publicly traded partnership earning income from certain Canadian-source investments, which imposes entity-level tax on such income.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supply revenues over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. Non-resident persons without a permanent establishment in Canada are subject to a separate annual registration threshold of CAD 30,000 of Canadian-source taxable supplies, but must register if they make 'taxable supplies' in Canada unless zero-rated. For the limited partnership, the threshold is assessed at the partnership level; once the LP exceeds the threshold, it must register for and charge GST/HST on taxable supplies made in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Canadian limited partnership may claim zero-rated (0%) GST/HST treatment for (a) exports of tangible personal property where title passes outside Canada and the goods are exported within the time limits prescribed by section 6 of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act; and (b) supply of services to a non-resident person that are 'in respect of real or tangible personal property situated outside Canada' or are supplied to a non-resident and are not 'substantially performed in Canada' (section 7, Part V, Schedule VI). For AI avatar video generation software and related digital services supplied to foreign non-residents, the supply is generally zero-rated under section 23 (intangible personal property, e.g., software, when supplied to a non-resident recipient) or section 7 if performed outside Canada, provided the recipient is not registered under GST/HST and the supply is not made in the course of the recipient’s Canadian business. The LP must obtain and keep documentary evidence that the recipient is a non-resident, including the recipient’s name and address, a declaration of status as non-resident, and, if available, a foreign tax identification number.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Automated Export Declaration / B13A) filed with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for goods exports exceeding CAD 2,000
  • Bill of Lading / air waybill showing export from Canada and destination outside Canada
  • Evidence of non-resident recipient status: recipient name and address, foreign tax registration number, and signed declaration
  • Commercial invoice describing the exported goods or services and clearly identifying the non-resident recipient
  • Supply agreement or contract evidencing that the supply was made to a non-resident and, for services, that the supply was not substantially performed in Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / foreign exchange receipt proving proceeds were received from outside Canada
  • Accounting records linking the export transaction to the zero-rated supply charged in the GST/HST return

Canada has no federal real-time electronic invoice clearance or fiscal stamping mandate for GST/HST. The CRA accepts paper or electronic invoices provided they contain prescribed information (supplier name, recipient name, date, consideration, GST/HST charged, and the supplier’s BN/RT account). There is no statutory API-based pre-clearance requirement. However, all electronic invoices must be maintained in a machine-readable format readily accessible to the CRA, and the CRA may issue assessments requiring the taxpayer to provide electronic records in XML, CSV, or other standard formats. Beginning 2024–2025, the CRA has expanded its e-audit capabilities and expects taxpayers to maintain robust digital audit trails. For digital platform operators, simplified GST/HST registration and collection rules (the 'Digital Economy' measures effective July 1, 2021) impose registration and remittance obligations on non-resident suppliers of digital services and e-commerce supply through a platform, regardless of the CAD 30,000 threshold; affected suppliers must register under the simplified GST/HST framework and remit GST/HST on certain supplies.

The CRA may audit zero-rated export supplies and assess tax, penalties, and interest if the required documentary evidence is missing or false. Under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act, any person who wilfully attempts to evade or avoid GST/HST, or who makes a false statement in a return, is liable on summary conviction to a fine of up to CAD 10,000, or up to CAD 25,000 for a subsequent conviction, plus imprisonment for up to 12 months (summary conviction), or on indictment to a fine of up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment for up to five years. Additionally, any tax credit or refund claimed with respect to a zero-rated export will be denied and subjected to 10% to 50% gross negligence penalties under subsection 285(1.2) or civil penalties under subsection 280.1(1) for false statements or omissions. Retrospective clawbacks are not time-barred where misrepresentation is fraudulent or is a willful default; the normal four-year reassessment period is extended indefinitely in such cases. The CRA also uses 'indicators of risk' for digital service exporters, including absence of contracts, lack of commercial substance, or inconsistent documentation; where a zero-rating event is reversed, the LP must self-assess GST/HST on the export and may be subject to interest from the original due date.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay federal corporate income tax; taxable income is allocated to partners. Provincial or territorial income tax may apply to corporate partners or other taxable partners.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. An LP distribution is not legally a corporate dividend merely because it is a profit distribution. The applicable withholding treatment depends on the character and source of the underlying amount and the recipient's status.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 is the principal statutory threshold relevant to Form T106 reporting and transfer-pricing penalty protection for non-arm's-length non-resident transactions. Canada generally requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation under Income Tax Act section 247, but does not impose a universal automatic Local File and Master File filing regime equivalent to all OECD jurisdictions. CRA may request documentation, including a functional analysis, agreements, financial data, comparables, methodology, and adjustment calculations. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, subject to Canadian filing and notification rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime using the label Economic Substance Regulations that applies generally to all companies or partnerships. Substance is relevant through Income Tax Act section 245 general anti-avoidance rules, the specific anti-avoidance rules, sham and characterization principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty anti-abuse provisions, transfer-pricing functions-assets-risks analysis, and common-law business-purpose and factual-context analysis. An LP with no personnel, decision-making, development activity, risk control, or operational assets in Canada may face challenges to Canadian deductions, source characterization, treaty access, partnership allocations, or claimed IP ownership.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include a gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, plus tax, interest, and reassessment. Wilful tax evasion may be prosecuted under the Income Tax Act and can result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and authorized representatives may face personal exposure where they directed, participated in, or knowingly assisted the conduct. A limited partner normally has limited liability, but may lose protection under applicable partnership law if it takes prohibited management actions or acts as a general partner. Canadian law does not impose an automatic travel ban for ordinary tax debt; imprisonment is a criminal remedy, not an automatic civil collection measure.
Failure to File Partnership, Corporate, or Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Administrative late-filing penalties, information-return penalties, interest, reassessment, loss of penalty protection, and possible prosecution may apply. T5013 late filing is generally calculated at CAD 25 per day, subject to statutory minimum and maximum amounts. Deliberate failure or repeated non-compliance may increase penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for certain unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and other trust amounts if statutory due-diligence defenses fail. A general partner may be liable under provincial partnership law for partnership obligations. Directors are not ordinarily personally liable merely because an LP or corporation filed late.
Unremitted Non-Resident Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted amount, late-remittance penalties, prescribed interest, and penalties for incorrect or missing NR4 slips. A reduced treaty rate applied without valid residency, beneficial-ownership, or anti-abuse support may be denied.

Director personal liability: Directors can face statutory director-liability assessments for unremitted source deductions where the corporation failed to remit and the statutory conditions are met. General partners may also face partnership-law exposure. Personal criminal liability requires participation, authorization, or other legally sufficient conduct.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment under section 247, deny deductions, increase income, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on qualifying adjustments where statutory conditions are met, and apply interest. The general anti-avoidance rule may apply to abusive arrangements.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face civil and, in cases involving intentional falsification, criminal exposure if they approved or participated in the arrangement. Limited liability is not a defense to personal fraud or knowing statutory violations.
Improper Partnership Allocation or Disguised Distribution

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize allocations or payments, deny deductions, reassess partners, apply withholding, impose penalties and interest, and invoke section 245 where the arrangement is abusive.

Director personal liability: The general partner and persons controlling the allocation may be personally exposed for fraud, misrepresentation, or statutory remittance failures. Limited partners generally remain protected from ordinary partnership liabilities unless statutory or provincial exceptions apply.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, and Corporate Transparency

Statutory sanction: A shell entity is not unlawful solely because it has limited operations. Concealment, fraud, money laundering, false corporate records, or sanctions evasion can trigger tax reassessment, criminal prosecution, forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment. Federal corporate beneficial-ownership registers and applicable provincial partnership records must be maintained where the relevant statute applies.

Director personal liability: Individuals who own, control, direct, or knowingly assist the structure may face criminal and civil liability. Travel restrictions are not an automatic consequence of shell-company status, but bail conditions, court orders, passport surrender, or immigration consequences may arise in criminal proceedings.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act imposes applicable registration, client-identification, recordkeeping, reporting, and compliance-program duties on regulated reporting entities. Criminal money laundering can result in substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment. Sanctions breaches may result in administrative or criminal penalties under applicable Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and compliance personnel may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, authorization, or wilful blindness. A payment processor's onboarding does not replace Canadian AML, sanctions, tax, or beneficial-ownership obligations.
Permanent Establishment and Unreported Canadian Operations

Statutory sanction: CRA may assert Canadian business income, permanent-establishment consequences under an applicable treaty, payroll and GST/HST obligations, withholding obligations, interest, penalties, and reassessments for prior years.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face personal liability for payroll and GST/HST trust amounts and for knowing misrepresentations. Ordinary permanent-establishment disputes do not automatically create criminal or travel-ban consequences.
