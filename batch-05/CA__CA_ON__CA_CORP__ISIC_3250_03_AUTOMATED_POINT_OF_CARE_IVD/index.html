Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), administering the Business Number (BN), GST/HST and corporate income tax accounts, with electronic filing through Business Registration Online (BRO), GST/HST NETFILE, and My Business Account; Ontario component: Ontario Ministry of Finance and Ontario Business Registry for provincial tax registration, Employer Health Tax (EHT) account, and Ontario corporate tax filings.

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities: (1) confirm whether the corporation carries on business in Canada (permanent establishment, employee solicitation, or inventory/assets in Canada); (2) obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online, Form RC1, or written application; (3) activate accounts as needed: corporate income tax (RT), GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP) if Canadian employees are engaged, and customs (BN15/BN17) with the Canada Border Services Agency; (4) register with the Ontario Business Registry for an Ontario corporation tax account and Employer Health Tax if Ontario remuneration exceeds the CAD 1,000,000 exemption threshold; (5) mandatory GST/HST registration arises when worldwide taxable supplies in Canada exceed the CAD 30,000 small supplier threshold or when taxable supplies of imported services and intangibles fall under the reverse-charge rules; (6) voluntary GST/HST registration is available to foreign persons carrying on a business in Canada, enabling input tax credit recovery; and (7) appoint an Ontario agent for service of process if carrying on business in Ontario.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a Canadian business corporation incorporated federally or in Ontario is a separate legal taxpayer; a corporation resident in Canada is taxable on worldwide income under Part I of the Income Tax Act; a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada is taxable on Canadian-source business income and on dispositions of taxable Canadian property, with an additional Part XIV branch tax of 25% (often reduced by tax treaty) on Canadian-source branch earnings; no pass-through eligibility exists for a Canadian corporation; Ontario corporate income tax applies to taxable income allocated to Ontario in addition to federal Part I tax.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; entity classification is determined by the Income Tax Act deeming rules and treaty/domestic partnership analysis. Available elections include: (a) ITA s. 85(1) tax-deferred rollover when transferring property into a corporation; (b) ITA s. 216(1) election permitting a non-resident receiving Canadian rental or timber royalty income to file a net-basis Part I return; (c) ITA s. 219 election in treaty contexts to align branch tax on Canadian branch earnings with the applicable dividend withholding rate; (d) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election for closely related corporations to treat intercompany supplies as non-taxable supplies; (e) Excise Tax Act s. 167 election to treat a supply of a business as a going concern as non-taxable; (f) GST/HST administrative elections for annual or quarterly filing periods and the quick method of accounting; and (g) the hybrid mismatch rules introduced by the Fall Economic Statement Implementation Act, 2023 (Bill C-59), which operate automatically under new ITA s. 247.1 to deny deductions for structured hybrid mismatches and imported mismatches, with no substantive taxpayer election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 small supplier threshold under ETA s. 148(1): a person, including a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, is not required to register for GST/HST until the total worldwide value of taxable supplies made in Canada exceeds CAD 30,000 in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in the current calendar quarter; registration becomes compulsory once the threshold is exceeded; non-residents supplying taxable services or intangible property to Canadian recipients may also be subject to reverse-charge self-assessment rules and, for digital services supplied to Canadian consumers, a simplified non-resident registration regime with the same CAD 30,000 threshold; voluntary registration is available where the person is engaged in a commercial activity in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate (0% HST) supplies of tangible personal property exported from Canada in the course of its commercial activity, where delivery occurs outside Canada or to a carrier for export, and the supplier retains prescribed documentary evidence of export (ETA Schedule VI, Part I; CRA may require proof of export within two years). Services performed on tangible personal property that is exported within 60 days of the service are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7, as are advisory and professional services supplied to non-residents in respect of property situated outside Canada. For automated point-of-care IVD manufacturing: sales of immunoassay analyzers, microfluidic cartridges, reagents, and quality controls shipped to foreign distributors or OEM customers qualify as zero-rated exported supplies when title passes outside Canada and freight terms evidence export; domestic Canadian sales of IVD equipment are generally subject to HST unless the item is a listed zero-rated medical device under Schedule VI, Part II; intercompany export billing to foreign related distributors must be supported by transfer pricing documentation to preserve the zero-rating on audit.

  • CBSA export declaration (Form B13A/B13 or automated EDI/ATO report) for exports valued at CAD 2,000 or more or for controlled, regulated, or prohibited goods
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order or sales contract showing sale price, Incoterms (FOB, CIF, EXW), and foreign consignee
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, or carrier manifest evidencing departure from Canada
  • Packing list and, where applicable, certificate of origin (e.g., CUSMA/USMCA) for preferential tariff claims
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank credit advice (SWIFT MT103) evidencing receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident purchaser
  • Customs broker outbound documentation or internal proof-of-export log (CRA GST11 declaration format) retained for zero-rating substantiation

No statutory obligation to clear individual invoices in real time with the CRA; Canada has not adopted mandatory e-invoicing, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping. CRA mandates: (a) electronic filing of GST/HST returns (GST/HST NETFILE) and T2 corporate returns for taxpayers above prescribed thresholds; (b) electronic remittance of federal tax amounts of CAD 10,000 or more; and (c) retention of invoices containing mandatory ETA disclosure elements (supplier GST/HST registration number, date, vendor name, purchaser name where the taxable amount exceeds CAD 50, description of the goods or services, and the amount of tax). The CRA operates no API-based invoice clearance gateway; e-invoicing may be exchanged voluntarily via PEPPOL-compliant platforms, while CRA validation uses My Business Account and EDI for return transmission rather than invoice-by-invoice approval.

CRA export-verification audits inspect documentation supporting zero-rated export claims; failure to produce evidence within the six-year retention period results in reassessment of the supply at 13% HST (Ontario rate) with interest, and can trigger reversal of input tax credits claimed by the exporter. Civil penalties: failure to collect or remit GST/HST attracts a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the amount not collected or not remitted (ETA s. 280); knowingly making false statements on a return attracts a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the understated net tax or overstated rebate (ETA s. 285); gross negligence on the corporate T2 return carries a 50% penalty of the understated income tax (ITA s. 163(2)). Criminal prosecution for wilful evasion under ETA s. 327 and ITA s. 239 can result in fines up to CAD 100,000, an additional fine of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment or two years on summary conviction. Retrospective clawbacks proceed by reassessment: CRA has a four-year normal reassessment window for GST/HST and income tax, extended without time limit where the taxpayer has made misrepresentations due to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, or has committed fraud (ETA s. 298(2); ITA s. 152(4)); CRA may set off clawback liabilities against any GST/HST refunds and may cancel registrations of exporters lacking commercial substance. Transfer pricing (ITA s. 247) and the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s. 245) permit CRA to recharacterize export arrangements with no real substance or with artificial revenue allocation. In this pipeline, export proceeds are monitored against the 180-day repatriation control benchmark; Canada imposes no statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline, but sustained non-repatriation may trigger CRA scrutiny of export substance and pricing.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a free-floating exchange-rate regime with unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility; the Canadian dollar (CAD) is a fully convertible G10 currency and there is no exchange-control legislation under the Currency Act or the Bank Act. Equity injections, intercompany loans, trade payments, and profit remittance may flow freely without central-bank approval. Remittance of dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees to non-residents is subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) at a 25% statutory rate, typically reduced to 5–15% under an applicable tax treaty; the payor must obtain a treaty-benefit declaration (NR301) and retain transfer-pricing documentation under section 247 of the Income Tax Act for intercompany cross-border flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory repatriation timeline, or forced conversion ratio for export receipts in Canada. Exporters may retain foreign-currency proceeds in domestic operating, collection, or offshore accounts at their discretion. The only related obligations are documentary: (i) zero-rated GST/HST export substantiation under the Excise Tax Act, and (ii) the PCMLTFR five-year record-retention duty and the FINTRAC electronic funds transfer report for any single transaction of CAD 10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — Canada has no centralized foreign-exchange control authority; monetary policy and financial system stability are vested in the Bank of Canada, while AML compliance for cross-border payments is overseen by FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Non-resident investment screening is administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) under the Investment Canada Act.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing protocol: (1) execute an MT103 SWIFT customer credit transfer from the non-resident parent or investor bank to the Ontario corporate operating or capital-escrow account, quoting the corporate SWIFT BIC and the Canadian transit/institution/account number; (2) populate the wire narrative with 'Equity Contribution — [Shareholder Name] — Subscription Agreement No. [X] — ISIC 3250 IVD Manufacturing'; Canada imposes no statutory purpose-code scheme, but banks pass all transactions through PCMLTFR EFT screening, so the remitter must appear on the bank's approved-source list; (3) single transactions of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC and the bank will require a source-of-funds declaration; (4) for amounts above CAD 50,000, pre-clear the transfer with the relationship manager and present the board resolution, share subscription, and investor audited financial statements; (5) if a correspondent or intermediary bank is used, require receipt of the SWIFT MT202 COV rather than a plain MT202 to preserve end-to-end originator transparency; (6) for multiple injections, ensure the aggregate capital paid in does not exceed the authorized share capital without amendment and is posted correctly to share capital or contributed surplus on the corporation's balance sheet.For trade settlements under ISIC 3250 / NAICS 334516, use the following rails: (i) documentary Commercial Letters of Credit issued under UCP 600 by the buyer's bank, with the Canadian Schedule I bank acting as advising, confirming, or negotiating bank — preferred for high-value analyzer and cartridge purchase orders, with settlement on the Canadian Lynx high-value payment system; (ii) SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers (TT wires) for repeat, fully KYC-verified overseas distributors, typically with a 30% prepayment structure and document-backed invoice; (iii) for cross-currency payments in USD, settlement occurs via the bank's USD clearing account held at a U.S. correspondent, with T+0/T+1 value dating; all outbound wires above CAD 10,000 are FINTRAC-reportable and must be supported by an underlying commercial invoice, packing list, transport document, and retained for five years under PCMLTFR record-keeping obligations.Deliverable FX outright forwards (plain-vanilla forward contracts) executed with a licensed Schedule I Canadian bank, commonly CAD/USD and CAD/CNH, permitting full or partial rolling of USD-denominated receivables into CAD; hedged items must be matched to identified commercial exposures.FX swaps and cross-currency swaps (CCS) executed under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement with collateral under a Credit Support Annex, used to swap USD trade receivables or intercompany loans into CAD or to match asset and liability currency positions.Vanilla FX options (call/put), including exotic-strike but not barrier-ladder structures, purchased onshore for tender-price or procurement-cycle protection, with premium paid in CAD or USD.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) — permissible but rarely needed for CAD because the Canadian dollar is fully convertible and a liquid deliverable forwards market exists; NDFs are accepted only for exotic currencies such as INR, KRW, or TRY where settlement convertibility is restricted.Forward contracts on commodity indices are not permitted for treasury hedging unless separately approved as part of the manufacturing input-cost programme; crypto-asset or blockchain-based hedging instruments are not acceptable for the corporate treasury account.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low-to-Moderate direct viability for offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, Revolut Business) as the primary treasury rail. Canada does not operate an electronic-money-institution licensing class; non-bank payment providers must register as a Money Services Business or Foreign MSB with FINTRAC and must comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which requires risk-based registration with the Bank of Canada for retail payment activities. Offshore EMIs generally cannot access the Lynx high-value settlement system or Payments Canada clearing infrastructure and therefore depend on sponsor-bank arrangements with Schedule I banks; they are suitable only for operational expense accounts and sub-CAD 10,000 supplier payments, not for capital injections, dividend repatriation, or documentary trade settlements, where legal-entity clearing, indemnity, and sanctions-related integrity require a licensed Schedule I bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for goods transactions; CAD 500,000 for services transactions (annual, with non-arm's length non-residents); Master File required if gross revenue exceeds CAD 50 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) like UAE; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles apply. CRA may challenge transactions lacking economic substance, and the OECD BEPS actions are incorporated.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239: fine up to 200% of tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for corporate tax debts if they fail to withhold/remit source deductions (s.227.1); civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment for fraud.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under GAAR, deny treaty benefits, and impose penalties up to 10% of the tax benefit; potential criminal charges for tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in the scheme; travel bans and asset freezes possible.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Penalty under s.247(3): 10% of the net adjustment (if adjustment exceeds CAD 50,000); interest on reassessed tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they knowingly or negligently participated in the mispricing; potential criminal prosecution for tax evasion.
AML Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for corporations; criminal penalties for willful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable for fines if they knowingly failed to comply; travel bans and imprisonment for money laundering offenses.
