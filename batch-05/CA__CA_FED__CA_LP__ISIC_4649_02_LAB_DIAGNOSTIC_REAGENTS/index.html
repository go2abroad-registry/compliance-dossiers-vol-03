Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration online portal (My Business Account / GST/HST NETFILE)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity that is a non-resident carrying on business in Canada and required to remit GST/HST must apply for a CRA business number (BN) and GST/HST registration account. Application can be filed online via the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service, by mail using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts), or by telephone. The registration must include the partnership's legal name, operating name, Canadian business address or agent address, business activity codes (NAICS), date of first business activity, and estimated annual taxable supplies. For a limited partnership, the general partner or authorized representative files the registration. A non-resident without a Canadian address must provide a resident agent's address in Canada for service of notices. The CRA issues a single BN with a program account suffix (e.g., RT0001 for GST/HST) upon successful registration. Non-resident entities that are solely earning income in Canada may require a separate corporate income tax account (RC account) if they are subject to Part I tax filing obligations, but a partnership itself does not file income tax; it files an T5013 partnership information return and its non-resident partners are separately liable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and the Excise Tax Act (Canada), a limited partnership is generally a fiscally transparent entity for income tax purposes and a 'selected listed financial institution' (SLFI) or 'registrant' for GST/HST purposes depending on its activities. No 'check-the-box' election analogous to the U.S. exists; however, partnerships may elect under subsection 20(2) or file Form T5013 to designate the partnership's status. For GST/HST, partnerships can elect under section 156 for certain supplies between members of a closely related group or use the quick method election (Form GST74) if eligible. Hybrid mismatch elections are limited; Canada's anti-hybrid rules (subsections 18.4(3) and 18.4(4)) may require adjustments but do not provide elective flexibility. A non-resident entity may rely on the Canada-U.S. Competent Authority procedure to determine its status, but the default rule for an LP formed under Canadian law is pass-through.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

30,000 CAD in worldwide annual taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) for non-resident businesses; once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. For a foreign entity that only makes zero-rated supplies, registration is optional but may be recommended to claim input tax credits. Provincial sales taxes may apply in separate jurisdictions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods exported by the supplier from Canada under subsection 1(a) Part V Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act, and to certain services provided to non-residents (e.g., transportation, or intangible property delivered to a non-resident). The supplier must retain documentary evidence showing that the goods were exported within a reasonable time (usually within 60 days) and that possession and risk passed to the purchaser outside Canada. For diagnostic reagents and ELISA assays, if the goods are physically shipped out of Canada to a non-resident customer and the supplier obtains proof of export, the supply is zero-rated. Services that are 'exported' to a non-resident but are in respect of tangible property situated in Canada or services likely to be performed in Canada may not be zero-rated, and careful analysis is required. The supplier must be a GST/HST registrant and must not be a selected listed financial institution for the relevant supply; otherwise, the zero-rating may be denied.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (via Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) G13/G7 eManifest or Canadian Export Reporting System), including the unique transaction reference number
  • Standard commercial invoice with purchaser name and address, description, quantities, terms of sale (Incoterms), and declared value
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill evidencing physical shipment across the customs border
  • Proof of export such as CBSA coded export report or carrier's export documentation
  • Packing list
  • Purchase order or contract from the non-resident buyer
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire proof of payment in foreign currency (if applicable to validate the FX repatriation and the commercial reality of the supply)

Canada does not currently mandate a real-time e-invoicing clearance or continuous transaction controls system at the federal level. However, CRA requires businesses to issue and retain electronic or paper invoices that contain specified information (name, address, BN, invoice date, description, tax rate and amount). GST/HST returns must be filed electronically using GST/HST NETFILE or GST/HST Program Account via the CRA's online portal if the business is registered; returns are due one month after the fiscal period end (for annual filers) or on a quarterly/monthly basis. The CRA has implemented a mandatory e-file requirement for payroll accounts and encourages e-invoicing standards (Peppol/PAN-AC). There is no digital fiscal stamping, but CRA may require electronic records in a specified format upon audit, and for goods exported, the CRA's export commercial invoice and proof of export must be retained digitally. For interprovincial supplies, real-time clearance is not applicable; however, the eventual harmonization may introduce Quebec's Revenu Québec electronic reporting for QST.

CRA may deny zero-rating retroactively if the taxpayer fails to retain adequate proof of export within the statutory record-keeping period (six years). Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include a 5% plus 1% per month penalty for failure to collect or remit GST/HST, and GST/HST penalties for knowingly or negligently making a false statement or omitting information can reach 25% of the net tax or greater for gross negligence (subsection 285.1) plus interest (usually 2% above the bank rate). A clawback of input tax credits previously claimed may be assessed with interest and penalties, and fraudulent claims may lead to criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act. For customs exports, misrepresentation carries penalties under the Customs Act (up to $200,000 for false statements) and the Canada Border Services Agency may impose administrative monetary penalties (AMPs). Exporters are advised to implement a robust documentary retention policy and maintain a clear audit trail for every zero-rated export transaction.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally freely convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no general statutory restrictions on converting Canadian dollars into foreign currencies, repatriating capital, receiving foreign investment, or remitting operating profits. Conversion and remittance remain subject to bank-level anti-money-laundering, sanctions, tax, beneficial-ownership, source-of-funds, and transaction-purpose controls. A Canadian limited partnership should maintain executed partnership agreements, contribution records, financial statements, tax filings, invoices, distribution resolutions, and evidence of tax compliance before making material outbound distributions. Canadian-source amounts paid or allocated to non-residents may trigger Canadian withholding-tax obligations, commonly at a statutory rate of 25% where applicable, subject to treaty relief and the legal characterization of the payment. A partnership may also have Canadian information-reporting, allocation, and withholding obligations depending on its activities, partners, and payment types. Non-resident partners should obtain professional advice on treaty eligibility, permanent-establishment exposure, effectively connected income, and applicable provincial tax issues.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no mandatory export-proceeds surrender regime, no prescribed conversion ratio, and no statutory requirement to convert export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may generally be retained in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts and may be used for working capital, supplier payments, debt service, hedging, or distributions, subject to contractual banking controls, tax compliance, sanctions screening, and accurate customs and accounting records. International electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, or equivalent amounts, may trigger financial-institution reporting to FINTRAC; physical cross-border transportation of monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more may trigger Canada Border Services Agency declaration requirements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no separate foreign exchange administration imposing general exchange controls. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers applicable anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing obligations, while the Canada Border Services Agency and the Canada Revenue Agency administer relevant customs, tax, and trade-reporting requirements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and foreign-exchange execution; Low to Medium as a replacement for a primary Canadian operating account. An offshore electronic-money institution may provide collection, foreign-exchange, or payout services only within the scope of its home-jurisdiction authorization and contractual permissions. It does not automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking, Canadian payment-service, trust, escrow, or client-money protection. Canadian payment-service-provider obligations may apply under the Retail Payment Activities Act and related Bank of Canada supervision if the provider performs covered payment functions in Canada. The structure must also satisfy FINTRAC registration and compliance requirements where applicable, Canadian sanctions requirements, correspondent-bank requirements, data and outsourcing controls, and the Canadian LP's bank-account, tax, and audit requirements. Offshore EMI accounts should not be used to obscure beneficial ownership, bypass sanctions screening, avoid Canadian reporting, or route unexplained third-party funds.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for payment collection and foreign-exchange execution; Low to Medium as a replacement for a primary Canadian operating account. An offshore electronic-money institution may provide collection, foreign-exchange, or payout services only within the scope of its home-jurisdiction authorization and contractual permissions. It does not automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking, Canadian payment-service, trust, escrow, or client-money protection. Canadian payment-service-provider obligations may apply under the Retail Payment Activities Act and related Bank of Canada supervision if the provider performs covered payment functions in Canada. The structure must also satisfy FINTRAC registration and compliance requirements where applicable, Canadian sanctions requirements, correspondent-bank requirements, data and outsourcing controls, and the Canadian LP's bank-account, tax, and audit requirements. Offshore EMI accounts should not be used to obscure beneficial ownership, bypass sanctions screening, avoid Canadian reporting, or route unexplained third-party funds.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate (General Rate). Note: a Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent; federal income tax is assessed at the partner level, not at the partnership level. Corporate partners are subject to the 15% federal general rate (or the 9% reduced small-business rate on eligible active business income up to CAD 500,000 for Canadian-controlled private corporations).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding applies to taxable dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. A limited partnership is not subject to Part XIII withholding on ordinary partnership profit distributions; however, non-resident partners are subject to Part I income tax on Canadian-source business income (remittance may be required via section 116 waiver or by filing a T1/T2 return).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Within 5 months after the partnership's fiscal year-end. For a calendar-year partnership, the T5013 is due no later than May 31 of the following calendar year.

Within 6 months after the end of the corporation's taxation year. For a calendar-year corporate partner, the T2 due date is June 30 of the following calendar year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of non-arm's-length transactions with each non-resident taxpayer in the fiscal year triggers the Form T106 filing obligation and contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. Canada does not statutorily prescribe OECD Local File/Master File, but CRA will expect documentation aligned with the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines for any controlled transaction above this threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations. Instead, the Income Tax Act contains: (a) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245), which denies a tax benefit where an avoidance transaction lacks bona fide business purpose or economic substance; (b) anti-hybrid and anti-deferral rules for foreign affiliates (ss. 94-95); and (c) the common-law substance-over-form doctrine. A limited partnership used solely to shelter per-unit royalties or to disguise a deemed Canadian subsidiary as an offshore entity may be recharacterized, and the CRA can disregard the partnership's existence or reallocate income to a permanent establishment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s. 239(1), a person who evades tax or makes false or deceptive statements is liable to a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and to imprisonment for up to 5 years on indictment (or up to 2 years on summary conviction). CRA may also impose civil penalties under s. 163(2) equal to 50% of the misreported amount where the misrepresentation is due to gross negligence.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the corporate general partner who participated in or authorized the evasion can be held personally liable under s. 160 or as co-conspirators; they may face criminal records, travel restrictions (Canada Border Services Agency watchlists), extradition proceedings, and civil restitution orders for the full tax evaded plus interest and penalties.
Repeated Non-Filing or Late Filing

Statutory sanction: T5013 penalties: CAD 25 per partner per month (max 12 months), minimum CAD 100, maximum CAD 2,500. T2 penalties: 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per month (max 12 months), and 10% plus 2% per month on a second failure. Failure to file T106 carries a penalty of CAD 100 per month (max 12 months) per non-arm's-length contract, plus transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of any net adjustment (minimum CAD 1,000, maximum CAD 100,000).

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner are not automatically liable for the partnership's income tax penalties, but they are jointly and severally liable under ITA s. 227.1 for unpaid source deductions (payroll) and GST/HST remittances. Continuing failure can result in director liability certificates being filed and enforcement against personal assets.
Transfer-Pricing Abuses

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the taxpayer on an arm's-length basis under ITA s. 247(2), adding taxable income and interest. A separate penalty under s. 247(3) equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment (min CAD 1,000, max CAD 100,000) applies where the taxpayer cannot demonstrate reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false or misleading T106 schedules or knowingly cause improper transfer-pricing positions may be subject to the penalty for false statements under s. 163.2 (up to CAD 100,000 for corporations and CAD 10,000 for individuals) and criminal prosecution if fraud is established. They are personally liable for collected taxes only in narrow withholding contexts, but can be made third-party liable for corporate tax assessments if they participated in a conspiracy.
Treaty Shopping / Artificial Avoidance of Canadian Withholding

Statutory sanction: CRA will apply GAAR under s. 245 to deny the tax benefit, and may recast distributions as deemed dividends subject to Part XIII withholding at 25% (treaty relief denied). Interest on the reassessment accrues from the date the tax should have been paid; a 50% gross-negligence penalty may also be asserted under s. 163(2) if the avoidance is accompanied by knowingly false statements.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner who execute such arrangements without commercial substance can be personally liable under joint-commitments or unjust-enrichment theories, and CRA may obtain freezing orders (Anton Piller injunctions) and criminal records for fraud if the scheme crosses into wilful blindness.
Unpaid Source Deductions and GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Failure to withhold and remit source deductions attracts a 10% penalty (or 20% if wilfully failing) plus daily interest on unpaid amounts; GST/HST unremitted amounts are subject to a penalty of 6% plus 1% per month up to 12 months; CRA can issue director's liability assessments and seize partnership assets.

Director personal liability: Under ITA s. 227.1 and Excise Tax Act s. 323, directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's failure to withhold or remit source deductions and GST/HST, provided they did not exercise reasonable care. Personal liability includes court orders, bank account seizures, and travel bans if the director attempted to leave Canada with unpaid amounts.
