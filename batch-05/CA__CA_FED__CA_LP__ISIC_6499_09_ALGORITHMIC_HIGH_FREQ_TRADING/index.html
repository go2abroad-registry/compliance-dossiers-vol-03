Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); national portal: My Business Account (CRA) at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html

E-government filing portal

For a limited partnership, a Business Number (BN) is required for federal tax compliance. The partnership must apply for a BN and a GST/HST account using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts) or via CRA's Business Registration Online service. Non-resident partnerships with no Canadian establishment may apply directly to CRA's International Tax Services Office. After obtaining a BN, the partnership may register for GST/HST, payroll (if applicable), and corporation/income taxes applicable to its activities. The partnership is also required to file an annual T5013 Partnership Information Return to report each partner's share of income/loss. For foreign partners, the partnership must file T5013 Summary and provide foreign partners with T5013 slips or equivalent statements.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible; under Canadian federal income tax law, a limited partnership is not a taxable entity. The partnership computes its income at the partnership level, and each partner (general or limited) includes their allocable share in computing their own taxable income. The partnership must file an information return (T5013) but does not pay federal income tax. With respect to GST/HST, a partnership is treated as a person and must register if it is not a small supplier and makes taxable supplies in Canada. Non-resident partners are generally taxable on Canadian-source income only; the partnership may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax on distributions to non-resident partners unless treaty relief applies. Territorial scope: Canadian-source business income is taxable regardless of where the partnership is resident; the partnership is considered resident where the central mind and management is located. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; under Canadian federal income tax law, a limited partnership is not a taxable entity. The partnership computes its income at the partnership level, and each partner (general or limited) includes their allocable share in computing their own taxable income. The partnership must file an information return (T5013) but does not pay federal income tax. With respect to GST/HST, a partnership is treated as a person and must register if it is not a small supplier and makes taxable supplies in Canada. Non-resident partners are generally taxable on Canadian-source income only; the partnership may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax on distributions to non-resident partners unless treaty relief applies. Territorial scope: Canadian-source business income is taxable regardless of where the partnership is resident; the partnership is considered resident where the central mind and management is located. Tax-Exempt Zone: Not applicable for this jurisdiction.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a 'check-the-box' regime equivalent to the US. Available elections include: (1) Election under subsection 97(2) of the Income Tax Act to transfer eligible property to the partnership on a tax-deferred rollover basis; (2) Election under section 216 of the Income Tax Act for non-residents to file a return for rental income, instead of Part XIII withholding; (3) Election for persons to be deemed a 'qualifying person' under Article IV of the Canada-US tax treaty for treaty shopping prevention; (4) For GST/HST, an election under the Excise Tax Act for closely related corporations or partnerships to treat certain intra-group supplies as not being supplies (section 156); (5) Election under the Government of Canada's general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) to disclose a 'transactions with a significant tax purpose' (Form RC3121). Hybrid mismatch elections are not expressly available in Canadian federal law; however, Canada applies OECD BEPS hybrid mismatch rules, and certain arrangements may be excluded or restructured with advance tax rulings.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For GST/HST, a person is a 'small supplier' if the total worldwide taxable supplies (including exports, excluding zero-rated exports) and their predecessor's taxable supplies are CAD 30,000 or less in the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Non-resident persons supplying goods/services in Canada that are not small suppliers must register. Even without exceeding the threshold, a non-resident may voluntarily register. Proprietary high-frequency trading activities generally constitute exempt financial services under the Excise Tax Act (Schedule V, Part VII). Registration is required if the partnership supplies taxable (not exempt) services in Canada, or if it makes zero-rated exports exceeding CAD 30,000. In all cases, to claim input tax credits (ITCs), registration is mandatory. The threshold for mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns is CAD 1.5 million in annual taxable revenues.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act, the following supplies qualify for zero-rating (0% GST/HST): (a) most supplies of goods exported from Canada, provided the exporter retains proof of export in accordance with section 169 and the Export and Import Permits Act; (b) supplies of services made to a non-resident person, where the service is performed wholly outside Canada or is directly in relation to exported goods, provided the services are not financial services; (c) financial services supplied to a non-resident person (e.g., trading in securities, commodities, or derivatives on offshore markets), but only if the service is not considered to be 'made to a person resident in Canada' or the financial service is not 'in relation to a Canadian financial activity' (for proprietary HFT, the partnership acts as a principal and does not supply financial services to customers; thus it does not earn zero-rated revenue but also does not incur GST/HST on its trading activities; its advisory or execution services to offshore clients could be zero-rated if requirements under subsection 123(1) and Part V.1 of Schedule V are met). For zero-rating of goods, the supplier must have the goods physically removed from Canada and export within specified time frames (generally within 60 days of the invoice date or within 60 days of receipt of payment, with extension up to one year if provided for in the supply contract). For services, there must be a written agreement noting the recipient is a non-resident and the service is performed entirely outside Canada.

  • Bill of Lading (or airway bill / marine cargo manifest)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or alternative proof of international settlement, e.g., SWIFT MT103)

Canada does not have a real-time fiscal clearance mandate (unlike Latin American jurisdictions). There is no requirement for digital stamping or pre-clearance of invoices before issuance. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and governed by normal commercial law; CRA accepts electronic invoices if they contain all mandatory supplier information (BN, date, description, amount). For GST/HST, all returns must be filed electronically (via CRA's My Business Account, TELEFILE, or using service providers) if the registrant's annual taxable revenues exceed CAD 1.5 million. Payments of GST/HST due of over CAD 10,000 may be required to be remitted electronically. The CRA mandates XML/SB certified software for customs export reporting via the Canadian Border Services Agency (CBSA) for goods. No API-based fiscal invoicing mandates exist.

CRA and CBSA routinely audit export transactions for zero-rating substantiation. If a taxpayer claims zero-rating without complying with documentation requirements, CRA may reassess the supplies as taxable at the rate of 5% GST/HST plus provincial portion (if applicable). Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include: (1) a penalty of up to 25% of any net tax that was overstated or input tax credit claimed improperly for 'false statements or omissions' (section 285.01); (2) a penalty of up to 20% of the amount of tax not remitted for a failure to collect or remit tax (section 280); (3) for gross negligence or wilful evasion, penalties of 50% of tax evaded (section 280.1) and criminal fines/restitution under section 327 of the Income Tax Act and section 282 of the Excise Tax Act, including imprisonment up to 5 years for tax evasion. There is a statutory 4-year limitation period for a normal reassessment, but in cases of fraud, misrepresentation, or neglect, CRA may reassess without time limits. Clawback provisions permit the Minister to recover unreasonably claimed refunds and input tax credits, regardless of whether the underlying transaction has been settled, plus interest at prescribed rates. The onus is on the taxpayer to retain complete export evidence (including FX repatriation) for at least six years after the taxation year.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions pursuant to its acceptance of IMF Article VIII obligations. No exchange control restrictions are imposed under the Currency Act or any foreign exchange control statute. Capital contributions, profit repatriation, and cross-border treasury flows are freely permitted. A non-resident limited partner's share of Canadian-source income may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, with potential relief under applicable income tax treaties, but no exchange-control approval is required for remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose any statutory surrender or mandatory conversion requirement for export receipts or any other inward remittance. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency, deposited with a chartered bank or authorized foreign exchange dealer, or used directly to settle obligations. Reporting obligations are limited to FINTRAC cross-border currency reporting (over CAD 10,000) and FATCA/CRS account disclosures, not surrender rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential bank supervision and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CTF surveillance.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low feasibility as a primary treasury rail. Canada does not maintain a distinct electronic money institution (EMI) license; fintech platforms operate as domestic or foreign money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC. Offshore digital EMIs generally cannot serve as the exclusive settlement bank for a regulated proprietary trading firm because they lack participation in the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and do not meet OSFI's clearing-member or CDS clearing agent criteria. Such platforms may be used only for minor operational expenses and must be fully transparent to the designated settlement bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate under s.123(1) of the Income Tax Act (applies to corporate partners on their allocable share; the limited partnership itself is fiscally transparent and not subject to Part I tax)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation under s.212(2); a Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent and does not pay dividends, so the 25% Part XIII rate only applies to actual interest, rents, or royalties paid to non-resident partners, not to ordinary profit allocations

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million aggregate fair market value per non-arm's length transaction category in a taxation year triggers the mandatory contemporaneous documentation obligation for the Local File; a Master File is required for multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue above CAD 50 million, and Country-by-Country reporting applies at the EUR 750 million group revenue threshold

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a separate Economic Substance Regulation as found in certain offshore financial centres, but the following substance requirements apply: the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245), the active business and carrying-on-business tests, and OECD BEPS Action 5 / Action 6 substance-over-form analyses; a Canadian limited partnership conducting algorithmic high-frequency trading must maintain a real physical presence, staffed trading and risk-management operations, substantive decision-making, and adequate capital at risk in Canada; otherwise CRA may deny treaty benefits, recharacterise transactions, and apply s.247 transfer pricing adjustments

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under s.239 of the Income Tax Act: on summary conviction a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment for up to 2 years; upon indictment a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment for up to 5 years; civil gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) of 50% of the understated tax and interest accruing from the due date

Director personal liability: Directors of a general partner or corporate partner may be jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid Part I or Part XIII taxes under s.227.1, including interest and penalties; a court may issue a compliance order, travel ban, and a writ of sequestration against director assets; criminal fines and incarceration are personal and not indemnifiable
Shell Company / Artificial Economic Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA will reassess under the GAAR (s.245) to deny the claimed tax benefit, add a 25% penalty on the disallowed benefit, and assess interest; transfer pricing adjustments under s.247 may also apply when the arrangement lacks arm's length substance; an additional foreign reporting and non-filing penalty of C$2,500 plus C$25/day may apply

Director personal liability: The directors of the general partner may be subject to personal liability for the adjusted corporate tax, penalties, and interest under s.227.1; additionally, a court may order restitution, impose a travel ban, disqualify the directors from acting as directors under CBCA s.118, and where the arrangement is used to launder funds, criminal exposure under the Criminal Code and anti-money laundering statutes
AML / Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), administrative monetary penalties up to C$500,000 for an entity and C$100,000 for an individual per violation; a criminal conviction may result in imprisonment for up to 5 years and fines at the court's discretion; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) may also suspend or revoke the trading entity's registration

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly fail to implement AML controls face personal criminal exposure for money laundering and financing of terrorism under the Criminal Code; courts may impose imprisonment, disgorgement of profits, and a personal travel ban pending prosecution; civil liability may extend to restitution to victims and shareholders under oppression remedies
Misclassification of Income / Improper Loss Importation

Statutory sanction: CRA will disallow loss allocations under the partnership allocation rules (ss.96–103) and the anti-avoidance rule in s.103(1); the taxpayer will face reassessment, a 25% GAAR penalty, and interest; if a false statement is made, a 50% gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2)

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner may be held personally liable for the resulting tax deficiency and penalties under s.227.1; a Criminal Code investigation may follow if the misallocation involves deception or fraudulent intent, leading to imprisonment for up to 5 years and an order to pay full restitution
