Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes: the partnership computes income under the Income Tax Act and allocates its fiscal-period income or loss to partners under the partnership agreement and applicable tax rules. The partnership may nevertheless have filing, withholding, information-reporting, GST/HST, customs, payroll, and provincial tax obligations. Foreign tax treatment is determined separately under the law of the partners' jurisdictions and may not follow Canadian classification.

Available election forms

Canada has no general US-style check-the-box election for Canadian partnerships. Relevant mechanisms may include partnership income allocation under the Income Tax Act, a section 97(2) rollover for qualifying transfers of property to a Canadian partnership, section 85 elections in qualifying corporate transfers where applicable, GST/HST joint-election mechanisms for qualifying joint ventures, and treaty-based relief where available. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules can deny or limit deductions or impose inclusion consequences for arrangements producing deduction or non-inclusion or duplicate deduction outcomes; these rules are statutory and are not elective entity-classification elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, generally measured over the applicable small-supplier determination periods, for most businesses. A person carrying on business in Canada and making taxable supplies may be required to register even where the threshold analysis is complex or the person is a non-resident. The federal GST rate is 5%. HST applies at 13% in Ontario and 15% in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, and Prince Edward Island; British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Quebec, and Alberta generally use separate provincial regimes or GST/PST combinations. Voluntary registration is generally available to eligible persons below the threshold, subject to statutory conditions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from Canada are generally zero-rated supplies for GST/HST purposes where the statutory place-of-supply and export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or transfer outside Canada and prescribed evidence that the goods were exported. Goods supplied to a recipient for export may qualify where the recipient exports the goods under the applicable conditions and the supplier retains required documentary evidence. Services supplied to a non-resident may qualify for zero-rating where the service is supplied to a person not resident in Canada and the statutory conditions are met; important exclusions apply, including many services relating to real property, tangible personal property, litigation, and services performed in Canada or otherwise specifically excluded by the Excise Tax Act and regulations. International transportation services and related freight or handling services may be zero-rated where the prescribed statutory conditions are met. Zero-rating generally permits recovery of eligible GST/HST input tax credits, subject to ordinary documentary and attribution rules.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, consignee, description, value, origin, destination, and transaction terms
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, truck manifest, or other carrier transport document
  • Proof of export, such as carrier confirmation, customs exit evidence, delivery records, or equivalent commercial documentation sufficient to establish removal from Canada
  • Purchase orders, contracts, packing lists, delivery receipts, and accounting records linking the exported goods or services to the zero-rated supply

Canada has no general federal real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance mandate or universal digital tax-stamping requirement. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices or other prescribed supporting documentation sufficient to substantiate tax charged and input tax credits, retain books and records, and file electronically where CRA filing rules require or mandate electronic filing for the relevant registrant. Electronic records, electronic customs declarations, CERS reporting, and CARM customs processes are accepted or required for specified customs transactions. Commercial invoices, bills of lading, proof of export, and transaction records must be retained for the statutory record-retention period, generally six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to extensions and specific rules.

Canada does not generally impose a mandatory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds; therefore, no statutory 180-day repatriation rule applies solely because a supply is zero-rated. CRA may reassess GST/HST where export conditions, recipient status, place-of-supply rules, or supporting evidence are not established. The supplier may become liable for uncollected or underreported tax, arrears interest, failure-to-file or failure-to-remit penalties, and other applicable administrative penalties. Gross-negligence penalties may apply where conduct shows intentional or grossly negligent disregard of the rules; false statements, forged customs evidence, tax evasion, or fraudulent claims may result in civil penalties and criminal prosecution. Input tax credits or GST/HST refunds may be denied or clawed back, and reassessments may extend beyond ordinary periods in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful blindness, or fraud. CBSA export-reporting or customs non-compliance may separately trigger administrative monetary penalties, seizure, accounting corrections, or prosecution under customs legislation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. Canada has accepted Article VIII obligations of the IMF Articles of Agreement and maintains no exchange controls, no central-bank approval requirements for inward or outward capital movements, and no restrictions on profit, dividend, or liquidation-remittance flows. A non-resident limited partner of a Canadian LP may freely repatriate its share of distributable cash or capital, subject only to ordinary income tax compliance under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and, where applicable, Part XIII withholding on treaty-tinted amounts such as interest or management fees; no blocked-funds or currency-rationing risk exists. The LP's Canadian business income allocable to a non-resident partner is taxable in Canada as income attributable to a permanent establishment, and the partner must file a Canadian non-resident return.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender, no mandatory conversion into CAD, and no repatriation deadline for foreign-currency receivables. The customs-bonded trucking LP may retain USD and other foreign currency in designated foreign-currency accounts at Canadian Schedule I banks, convert at commercial rates, or apply such proceeds directly to foreign-currency payables. Cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported by the bank to FINTRAC as EFTRs; the LP must maintain supporting commercial documents (invoices, customs entries, GPS-sealed delivery manifests) for verification and audit.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) is the monetary and financial system authority; prudential regulation and supervision of deposit-taking institutions is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (Canada); AML/ATF supervision and cross-border transaction reporting is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); Payments Canada operates the Lynx (RTGS high-value system) and ACSS (Automated Clearing Settlement System) clearing rails.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium, with strict substitutability limits. Non-bank electronic money institutions (EMIs) including Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, and Stripe are not 'banks' under the Bank Act and cannot provide insured deposit-taking or full commercial trade-finance services. Since November 1, 2024, payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and comply with safeguarding and reporting obligations. EMIs are viable as an overlay for small-value cross-border vendor payments and payroll disbursements, but the primary operational account, capital escrow, customs-bond collateral, and trade-finance facilities must sit with a Schedule I chartered bank because of CBSA bond undertakings, airport cargo security clearances, and the ISIC 5222 bonded trucking licensing framework.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward capital contributions from non-resident partners are unrestricted and require no Bank of Canada or Investment Canada approval. Standard compliant routing: (i) the partner's bank transmits a SWIFT MT103 to the Canadian bank's correspondent; (ii) CAD funds settle over Lynx (RTGS) or USD funds settle over Fedwire/CHIPS through the Canadian bank's U.S. correspondent; (iii) the proceeds are credited to the LP's Capital Escrow Account or Operational Account. The remittance instruction must include the LP's exact legal name, account number, and a purpose description such as 'Capital contribution – LP interest subscription'; Canadian receiving banks require selection of a standard remittance purpose code (e.g., 'T101 – Capital contribution to partnership') to satisfy FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting. A source-of-funds memorandum documenting each contributor and the provenance of funds must be retained for AML defense. Capital subscriptions are not subject to withholding tax; a record of contributed capital should be maintained for the LP's accounting and tax allocation schedules.For trade settlements and operating disbursements the LP may use: (i) domestic CAD supplier and payroll payments via ACH Direct (Payments Canada ACSS) or Bank Wire; (ii) cross-border USD and foreign-currency trade payables via SWIFT MT103 with correspondent banking through the primary Schedule I bank; (iii) documentary instruments, including Commercial Letters of Credit (UCP 600) and standby letters of credit or bank guarantees for CBSA customs-bonded undertakings and airport cargo security commitments; and (iv) high-value CAD payments of CAD 100,000 or more which must settle over Lynx (RTGS) on a real-time basis. The treasury package should include a trade-finance facility with an FX conversion sub-limit, a customs-bond surety sub-limit, and dedicated USD account sub-ledgers. Distributions to non-resident partners should be routed by wire with a clear 'partnership distribution' purpose code and supported by the partnership agreement and capital accounts to evidence the arm's-length nature of the remittance.Deliverable FX forwards (CAD/USD, CAD/EUR) – standard OTC instrument executed under a master forward or ISDA agreement with a Schedule I bankFX swaps (simultaneous spot and forward purchase/sale of principal in two currencies)Cross-currency basis swaps – permissible for hedging USD-denominated equipment financing or lease liabilities of the truck fleetVanilla FX options (CAD/USD calls and puts) – permissible for hedging budgeted operating revenues and capital expenditure exposuresNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) – technically available but commercially unnecessary for CAD because the currency is fully convertible and a deep onshore deliverable market exists; Canadian banks will normally execute only deliverable forwards unless a counterparty mandates synthetic settlement

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium, with strict substitutability limits. Non-bank electronic money institutions (EMIs) including Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, and Stripe are not 'banks' under the Bank Act and cannot provide insured deposit-taking or full commercial trade-finance services. Since November 1, 2024, payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and comply with safeguarding and reporting obligations. EMIs are viable as an overlay for small-value cross-border vendor payments and payroll disbursements, but the primary operational account, capital escrow, customs-bond collateral, and trade-finance facilities must sit with a Schedule I chartered bank because of CBSA bond undertakings, airport cargo security clearances, and the ISIC 5222 bonded trucking licensing framework.
