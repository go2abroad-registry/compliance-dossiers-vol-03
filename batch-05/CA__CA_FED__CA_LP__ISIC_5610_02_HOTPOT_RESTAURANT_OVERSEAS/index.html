Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); official portal: My Business Account (via canada.ca; CRA GST/HST Web Forms and Represent a Client)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident limited partnership carrying on taxable activities in Canada must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The general partner or authorized representative completes Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or registers online through the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service. Once the BN is issued, a separate GST/HST account is opened if the partnership is not a small supplier and is engaged in commercial activity in Canada. The partnership must provide its legal name, registered address, general partner details, fiscal year-end, and a description of taxable supplies (e.g., restaurant dining and condiment supply). Where the LP is formed outside Canada, it must also provide evidence of foreign registration and appoint a tax representative under ITA s.220(4.1) and Excise Tax Act s.293 where the CRA so requires. After registration, the CRA issues a BN/GST/HST program account with a two-letter suffix (RT/RM) for filing GST/HST returns.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not have a US-style 'check-the-box' election. A limited partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent for income tax purposes under the Income Tax Act (ITA) s.96; each partner computes their share of income, gains, losses, and credits directly. For GST/HST purposes, however, the partnership itself is a 'person' that must register if it is making taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier. Elective mechanisms include: (i) ITA s.96(3) election to maintain a non-calendar partnership fiscal period, (ii) an election under ITA s.97(2) to transfer property to the partnership on a rollover basis, and (iii) a joint election under ITA s.152(4) to waive or extend reassessment periods on certain loss-carryback transactions. Hybrid mismatch rules in ITA s.94.1 and s.94.2 may apply to restrict benefits if there is inconsistent tax treatment of the partnership by a foreign jurisdiction; no check-the-box style election alters Canadian entity classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies (excluding financial services and certain zero-rated exports for small-supplier determination) in any single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters; once surpassed in any quarter, registration is mandatory and effective from the date the threshold is exceeded or the first day of the quarter, subject to the small supplier rules in ETA s.148 and s.149. For non-residents carrying on business in Canada, substantially the same threshold applies, but a non-resident also becomes registrable if it maintains a Canadian physical establishment and makes taxable supplies in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export supplies are eligible under ETA s.199 and Schedule VI: supplies of goods exported by the vendor or by a common carrier on the vendor's behalf; services performed wholly outside Canada (unless certain exceptions apply); and services provided to non-residents in respect of goods or real property outside Canada. For direct-operated hotpot restaurant and condiment supply, eligibility requires: (i) title to packaged condiments and goods to pass to a non-resident purchaser, (ii) the goods are physically exported within reasonable time (normally within 60 days or as industry practice, but no formal statutory period), (iii) the vendor retains evidence of export, and (iv) for services (e.g., management fees charged to overseas franchisee or offshore entity), the recipient is not a resident of Canada, and the service is not performed in Canada or the service relates to foreign property. Zero-rating applies only if all export documentation is retained; unsubstantiated exports are taxable at 5% GST (or HST where applicable) and subject to reassessment.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada CBSA Form B13A – Export Declaration, or e-export via CAED/ARD for goods > CAD 2,000 or under regulation)
  • Bill of Lading or waybill showing the vendor as shipper or exporter and the foreign port/address of delivery
  • Commercial invoice issued by the partnership to the non-resident buyer
  • Packing slip / weight certificate evidencing goods description and quantity
  • Freight or courier receipts (e.g., carrier proof of export with Canadian Customs control number)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wiring/foreign currency conversion record evidencing receipt of payment from the original non-resident customer
  • Contract or purchase order confirming overseas destination and sale terms (Incoterms)
  • CBSA/trade transaction number or export summary for goods subject to export reporting
  • For zero-rated services: a written service agreement with non-resident recipient and evidence that the service is performed outside Canada or relate to non-Canadian property

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) mandate for GST/HST purposes. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and the CRA only requires that a supplier issue a 'proof of taxable supply' containing prescribed information (e.g., supplier name, GST/HST registration number, amount, tax charged) for taxable supplies over CAD 30. Accepted e-invoicing formats include PDF, EDI, XML, or PEPPOL. GST/HST returns (GST34) are filed periodically (monthly, quarterly, or annually) through CRA-approved EDI, internet file transfer, or paper; there is no API-based pre-clearance of invoice transactions from the CRA.

The CRA audits export zero-rating claims with heightened scrutiny wherever documentation relates to transfers of goods from a restaurant chain to overseas affiliates or supply of condiments to foreign purchasers. Where zero-rated export treatment is claimed without satisfactory documentary proof, the CRA may reassess to collect unremitted GST/HST plus arrears interest (daily compound interest under ETA s.280), apply negligence penalties under ETA s.280.1 and s.285 (typically 25% of net tax deficiency, increased to 50% under the Canada Revenue Agency's gross negligence provisions), or impose administrative monetary penalties for false or mis-identified export statements. In egregious cases, section 327.1 of the Excise Tax Act exposes persons to criminal prosecution for tax evasion committed by wilfully making false statements, with penalties of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. Retroactive clawback is not time-limited where the CRA establishes misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default; in ordinary cases, the normal four-year reassessment window applies. The CRA also requires recovery of input tax credits (ITCs) claimed on purchases related to improperly zero-rated export supplies, and may invoke the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245) against arrangements using pass-through entities to artificially convert taxable domestic supplies into zero-rated exports. Exporters should maintain the complete customs bundle for at least seven years from the end of the fiscal year to survive audit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted currency convertibility pursuant to its IMF Article VIII obligations. Both capital and current account transactions are freely convertible between CAD and foreign currencies without exchange control licensing or central bank permit. Profit remittances by foreign partners of the Canadian Limited Partnership are freely permitted, subject to standard Part XIII non-resident withholding tax, annual income tax filings, and FINTRAC cross-border fund transfer reporting for electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more. No prior approval is required for dividend or profit repatriation; however, large cross-border movements remain subject to sanctions screening and AML monitoring, and the foreign partner must maintain documentary evidence of source of funds to avoid FATF red-flag referrals.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters and service providers, including the LP selling condiment supplies to overseas restaurant affiliates, may retain all foreign currency receipts in resident or non-resident accounts. The only related obligations are: (i) trade data reporting under Canada's General Export Reporting system administered by the Canada Border Services Agency with Statistics Canada for goods exported above the CAD 2,000 threshold and, where applicable, the Export and Import Permits Act; (ii) FINTRAC large cross-border EFT reporting on a 24-hour aggregation basis for transactions of CAD 10,000 or more; and (iii) contemporaneous documentation supporting tax treaty and transfer-pricing positions for intercompany royalty and goods flows.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential supervision delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money-laundering administration vested in the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign exchange control authority; the Minister of Finance, the Bank of Canada, and OSFI jointly administer the limited statutory review mechanisms.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM (conditional). Non-bank fintech firms offering cross-border payment services in Canada must register as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and, where they provide payment account or clearing services, must comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime administered with Bank of Canada oversight. Offshore digital EMIs that do not hold a Canadian MSB/RPP registration cannot lawfully open a domestic clearing account for the LP or access the Lynx/RTR payment systems; they may be used only as an auxiliary corridor for low-value operational disbursements in the destination market, provided the destination jurisdiction permits such use and the EMI holds a valid license there. Critical legal constraint: the LP retains full AML and sanctions compliance responsibility; concentrating operating liquidity in an unregulated digital EMI constitutes a high-risk red flag and should be avoided for principal treasury flows.

GST/HST annual return and remittance (if the LP is an annual filer) Within three months after the fiscal year-end for annual filers; monthly or quarterly filers follow the due dates of each assigned GST/HST reporting period. $0 USD Failure to file or remit triggers a penalty of 5% of the balance due plus 1% per full month up to 12 months, daily interest on unpaid amounts, and possible prosecution for deliberate failure to file.
Annual return or renewal under the applicable provincial Limited Partnership Act Province-specific; generally 30 to 60 days after the LP's registration anniversary or the date shown on the provincial renewal notice. $30 USD The LP may incur late fees, lose good standing, be administratively struck off or dissolved after a statutory default period and notice, and lose the capacity to maintain legal proceedings; lapsed registration can also expose partners to liability.
CBCA Annual Return for a corporate general partner (where the general partner is incorporated under the CBCA) Within 60 days after each anniversary of the corporate general partner's incorporation or registration under the CBCA. $15 USD Failure to file may result in a fine of up to C$5,000; two consecutive missed annual returns may trigger administrative dissolution of the corporate general partner, terminating its legal authority to manage the LP.
