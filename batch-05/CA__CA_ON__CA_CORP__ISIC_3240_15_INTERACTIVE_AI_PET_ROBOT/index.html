Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal tax administration portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); Ontario Ministry of Finance for Ontario provincial tax matters (https://www.ontario.ca/page/ministry-finance).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through the CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) service or by completing Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number'. The same BN serves as the GST/HST account number once the entity registers for the GST/HST, as the payroll account number if applicable, and as the import/export account number for CBSA purposes. For Ontario non-resident corporations, a provincial tax registration with the Ontario Ministry of Finance may be required where the corporation has Ontario taxable presence or carries on business in Ontario.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No 'check-the-box' election exists under Canadian income tax law; corporate residence and entity classification are determined by the Income Tax Act (Canada) and common law. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada are subject to Part I tax on Canadian-source business income without a separate entity-classification election. Specific statutory elections available to certain taxpayers include: the section 216 election for non-resident owners of Canadian real estate to file on a net rental income basis in lieu of Part XIII withholding tax; the section 217 election for non-residents to file a return on employment income; and the joint election under subsection 216(1) for the disposition of Canadian taxable property. Canada has enacted hybrid mismatch rules in sections 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act that disallow deductions in respect of certain hybrid mismatch arrangements, but these operate as mandatory rules rather than elective mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD in worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated financial services and certain excluded supplies) made in Canada over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, the supplier must register and collect GST/HST, subject to the small-supplier exception.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under the Excise Tax Act applies to supplies exported from Canada, including goods supplied to a non-resident for delivery outside Canada where the exporter satisfies the documentary requirements of section 24 of the GST/HST Regulations. Goods must be exported 'as soon after the supply is made as is reasonable having regard to the circumstances' and must not be acquired for consumption, use, or supply in Canada. Zero-rated services include services performed entirely outside Canada, advisory services to non-residents in respect of real property or movable property situated outside Canada, and certain services supplied to non-residents not in connection with Canadian real estate or consumable goods. For the assembly of bionic companion robotic animals with speech synthesizers classified under ISIC 3240 / NAICS 339930, exported tangible robotic toys qualify for zero-rating provided the supplier maintains proof of export and receives payment in a permitted manner.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A – Export Declaration) or equivalent electronic export declaration filed via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS)
  • Commercial Invoice and contract of sale showing the non-resident purchaser, description of goods, terms of sale, and value in Canadian dollars
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing shipment of the goods to a destination outside Canada
  • Proof of export in the form of a carrier certificate, customs broker confirmation, or terminal receipt demonstrating departure from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or a valid bank remittance statement, foreign exchange contract, or credit card/Stripe settlement record evidencing receipt of foreign currency or payment in the ordinary course of the cross-border transaction

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, government pre-approval of invoices, digital stamping, or continuous transaction controls. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices containing prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act (e.g., supplier name, BN/GST/HST registration number, date of invoice, description, tax payable, and tax rate). Electronic invoices are accepted if all required particulars are present and the records are maintained in legible form; CRA requires record retention in an electronic format that can be converted to readable paper form. No API-based clearance or government electronic stamping requirement applies.

CRA auditors conduct targeted GST/HST verification of zero-rated exports, requiring strict compliance with the documentary evidence requirements to sustain the 0% rate. Failure to maintain required records or inaccurate reporting can result in reassessment of uncollected GST/HST at 13% on the full export value, interest on overdue amounts, and administrative penalties under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions of up to the greater of amounts specified in that section (including 15% of the net tax in certain cases). Knowingly making false statements, participating in fraudulent refund schemes, or claiming zero-rating with forged Bills of Lading or false customs declarations may also lead to criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under the Excise Tax Act, the Customs Act, and the Criminal Code, plus retrospective clawback of any ITCs previously claimed.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible, free-floating currency system with no general exchange control laws. Capital and current account convertibility are unrestricted; direct investment, repatriation of capital, and profit remittances are permissible without prior Bank of Canada or government approval. Remittances may be subject to non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and any applicable tax treaty. Practical hurdles include banking AML/KYC verification, FINTRAC cross-border reporting thresholds (C$10,000 export/import of currency), and documentary evidence of underlying corporate transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export proceeds surrender requirements, no mandatory conversion ratios, and no requirement to repatriate export proceeds into Canadian dollars within a prescribed timeline. Traders may retain export receivables in any currency, but must deposit funds in a Canadian chartered bank or authorized foreign bank branch for corporate banking purposes and comply with general anti-money-laundering recordkeeping and cross-border reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions and FINTRAC as related regulatory authorities)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium feasibility for non-bank fintech payment service providers. Digital-only EMI-style institutions are not charterable under Canadian banking law, but entities such as Wise Business, Mercury, and other foreign EMIs can provide ancillary cross-border payments if registered as money service businesses under FINTRAC (and/or licensed as payment service providers under provincial consumer protection regimes). Core Canadian-dollar settlement and capital escrow lend themselves best to a Schedule I bank or a fully authorized foreign bank branch; EMIs are viable for auxiliary disbursement, vendor settlement, and employee payouts, though risk ratings and KYC duplication increase. Direct Canadian banking access for a newly incorporated Ontario robotics manufacturer is generally available through remote onboarding workflows.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound routing uses the Swift MT103 wire format from the parent's or investor's bank to the corporation's CAD-denominated operating account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. The remittance information must clearly state 'Capital Contribution / Equity Subscription' together with the corporate legal name, Ontario jurisdiction, and share class reference. Canadian banks require the receiving company to provide documentary support such as the certificate of incorporation, shareholder resolution to issue shares, and a capital account roll-forward. Any transfer exceeding the equivalent of C$10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC by the bank; for transfers greater than the reporting threshold, the bank typically performs enhanced due diligence on the remitter and the recipient before crediting. The bank will not require OSFI approval for a standard direct capital injection in an ISIC 3240 robotic-toy company.Outbound trade settlements are executed through SWIFT MT103 single customer credit transfers for low-value component purchases, and for larger/verifiable transactions through irrevocable commercial letters of credit (LC) confirmed by the Canadian issuing bank. All disbursements must be screened against OFAC/UN sanctions and FINTRAC requirements. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined Federal + Ontario general corporate rate (15% Federal Part I + 11.5% Ontario); 25.0% combined for qualifying Ontario Manufacturing and Processing profits (15% Federal + 10% Ontario M&P rate); 12.2% combined under the Small Business Deduction on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income earned in a Canadian-controlled private corporation.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act, payable on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder (gross basis, s.212(2)); the Ontario corporation, as a Canadian payor, is obliged to withhold and remit on every dividend paid to the Hong Kong or foreign parent or any non-resident beneficial owner.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 must be filed no later than 6 months after the fiscal period end (Income Tax Act s.150(1)); final income tax balance for a CCPC is due no later than 3 months after fiscal year-end, and 2 months after fiscal year-end for other corporations (s.157); corporations with aggregate federal and Ontario tax payable exceeding CAD 3,000 must make quarterly or monthly installment payments.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual EHT return is due on or before March 15 following the calendar year end; installments are due on the 15th day of each month after the year-end for employers with exemptions pending.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate annual consideration with each non-arm's-length non-resident triggers mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under s.247(4); Form T106 must be filed with the T2 for related-party cross-border transactions exceeding C$50,000 in a taxation year; a Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) is mandatory for multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of at least C$750,000,000; Ontario imposes no separate transfer pricing documentation but follows the federal CRA documentation hierarchy through the Federal–Provincial Tax Collection Agreement.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) statute; economic substance is enforced through general anti-avoidance rules (s.245 GAAR), transfer-pricing recharacterization (s.247(2)(b)), and permanent establishment allocation concepts. An Ontario shell corporation with no physical assembly line, no bank account in Canada, no resident employees, and no business decision-making functions will be disregarded or have its income re-allocated under the OECD permanent establishment and substance principles; treaty benefits for dividends and royalties can be denied when the recipient is a conduit without real economic activity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Tax Evasion / Deliberate Omission

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under Income Tax Act s.239: fine of 50% up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment or up to 2 years less a day on summary conviction; civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) of 50% of the misstatement; CRA gains the right to reassess beyond the normal 3-year limitation period (up to 10 years) where misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default is established under s.152(4).

Director personal liability: Directors who directed, assented to, or acquiesced in the evasion may be prosecuted as parties to the offence under s.239(1)(c); civilly, directors face joint and several liability for unremitted taxes under s.227.1; CRA can issue an intention-to-collect notice, freeze personal bank accounts, and obtain a departure-control court order restricting travel while prosecution is pending; a criminal conviction disqualifies the director from acting as an officer of any corporation under the Ontario Business Corporations Act and bars entry into the United States under the U.S. Immigration and Nationality Act absent a waiver.
Failure to File, Late Remittance, and Withholding Defaults

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalty under s.162(1) of 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per full month late (maximum 12 months); repeated failure attracts 10% plus 1% per month to a maximum of 24 months; daily compounding interest under s.161; failure to remit source deductions and Part XIII withholding attracts a penalty under s.227(9) of up to 10% of the unremitted amounts and, where the failure is deliberate, criminal liability under s.227(10); CRA may also issue a formal demand, garnishee bank accounts, and seize receivables without judicial authorization.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for unreported corporate amounts withheld from employees and for GST/HST and Part XIII amounts where the corporation cannot pay; CRA requires directors to pay the full deficiency before obtaining a clearance certificate; directors who transferred corporate assets to avoid the deficiency are personally liable under s.160 for the fair-market-value shortfall; travel restrictions and denial of CRA clearance certificates prevent directors from leaving Canada or winding up the corporation until the deficiency is resolved.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment applying CUP/TNMM to restore arm's-length margins; penalty under s.247(10) equal to the greater of C$500 and 10% of the net adjustment where the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices; the normal 3-year reassessment limitation period is extended under s.152(4) to 10 years where the transfer pricing documentation is false or incomplete; interest accrues on the reassessed liability from the original filing due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed inaccurate T106 forms or directed the underpricing are exposed to civil liability for inducing breach of statutory tax obligations; CRA may invoke s.160 against directors who received corporate distributions or asset transfers in anticipation of reassessment; officers who knowingly attached fraudulent transfer pricing documentation to the T2 face prosecution under s.239(1)(a) as parties to tax evasion, with penal consequences of fines and imprisonment.
AML / FINTRAC and Economic Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties can reach C$500,000 per contravention and C$1,000,000 for repeat or egregious violations; exporting or re-exporting controlled or sanctioned robotic AI goods without an Export and Import Permits Act permit attracts fines, forfeiture of goods, and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment; failure to report suspicious transactions to FINTRAC when the conduct amounts to money laundering triggers independent criminal penalties.

Director personal liability: Senior officers who knowingly direct, aid, or abet money laundering or sanctions evasion face fines and imprisonment under the Criminal Code and the United Nations Act; corporate veil-piercing is routinely exercised through asset forfeiture under the Seized Property Management Act, and directors can be subject to personal restraining orders, freezing of personal assets, and permanent exclusion from regulated markets; Canadian banks and payment processors including Stripe and credit-card acquirers will terminate merchant accounts, and FINTRAC may issue a compliance order requiring the corporation to appoint an independent auditor at the directors' personal cost.
