Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada; online portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services/business-account.html) and Business Registration Online.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated limited partnership obtains the federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) with the CRA, either through Business Registration Online or by mail/fax to the appropriate CRA tax centre. The application must identify at least one general partner, provide the partnership agreement and foreign registration certificate, state the nature of Canadian commercial activity, and disclose the partnership’s non-resident ownership structure. Where the LP makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier, CRA will concurrently open a GST/HST program account. Non-resident LPs supplying digital services to Canadian customers may use the CRA’s simplified non-resident GST/HST registration process, which is available through the same BN account mechanism without requiring a Canadian registered office.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style ‘check-the-box’ election or a general hybrid-mismatch entity election for income tax purposes. A limited partnership is conclusively treated as a flow-through partnership for federal income tax, and no election can cause the LP itself to be taxed as a corporation. The only relevant elections are procedural or administrative: a registrant partnership may elect under subsection 273 of the Excise Tax Act to treat members as a single GST/HST registrant in certain joint-venture or corporate groups, and partnerships may make fiscal-period elections under the Income Tax Act. None of these alter the underlying pass-through classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: a partnership must register for GST/HST once the total amount of its taxable supplies exceeds CAD 30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters, determined on a worldwide-basis for small-supplier status; registration becomes mandatory once the threshold is exceeded, with an additional liability if the supplier is not registered.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods physically exported from Canada are zero-rated when the supplier possesses acceptable proof of export within the prescribed time. Services and intangible property (including esports-platform subscriptions, tournament entry fees, and digital matchmaking services) supplied to a non-resident person who is not registered under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act are zero-rated if the supply is not in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada and the service is not supplied for consumption/use in Canada. The platform must substantiate the non-resident status of the customer and the foreign place of supply through billing address, IP address, payment-processor country codes, and the underlying service contract. Supplies made to Canadian residents remain taxable at the applicable GST/HST rate.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration (B13A form) for physical goods
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / carrier certificate of export
  • Commercial invoice describing the exported goods or digital services
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank/FX receipt evidencing settlement into the LP’s Canadian bank account
  • Non-resident status evidence and place-of-supply records (contract, IP/billing data) for offshore digital services

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance system, invoice digital stamping regime, or a statutory API-based clearance portal. Registrants must issue GST/HST invoices containing prescribed information, retain electronic transaction records, and file GST/HST returns electronically through CRA’s GST/HST NETFILE or related XML/API channels where CRA’s filing threshold applies. B2B e-invoicing may be exchanged voluntarily through PEPPOL or commercial networks, but there is no government-mandated e-invoice clearance step prior to issuance.

If export zero-rating is claimed without sufficient documentary support, CRA may reassess the GST/HST forgone within the normal four-year reassessment period, or extend to eight years in cases of misrepresentation or wilful default. Civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act include penalties of 25% of the net tax understated for false or omissive statements and, where knowledge is proven, higher penalties plus interest. Criminal prosecution for tax evasion is available for deliberate false zero-rating claims. CRA’s cross-border audit program specifically examines digital platforms with non-resident revenue allocations, and retrospective clawbacks extend to both federal GST and provincial HST portions where applicable.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full capital and current account convertibility under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible; there are no statutory exchange controls distinguishing current vs capital account transactions. Resident and non-resident entities may hold foreign currency accounts, remit dividends, royalties, and partnership distributions, and repatriate capital without Bank of Canada or FINTRAC exchange-control approval. Profit remittance hurdles are limited to tax compliance: Part XIII withholding may apply to passive payments such as dividends, interest, rent, and royalties, generally reduced by applicable tax treaties; allocations of active business income to non-resident partners of a Canadian LP are not subject to Part XIII withholding but may create filing obligations if a Canadian permanent establishment or carrying-on-business nexus exists. No currency-specific central bank approval or post-transaction documentation is mandated for outward remittances.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion requirements in Canada. Export receipts may be retained in any currency in a Canadian or offshore bank account without an enforced timeline or conversion ratio. Canada abolished surrender obligations in 1951 and is classified as an Article VIII jurisdiction by the IMF. For statistical purposes, exporters must maintain normal supporting documentation for the Canada Revenue Agency and FINTRAC, but no certificate of surrender or Bank of Canada conversion approval is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Low as a principal treasury holding account; Medium as an ancillary payout/collection rail under strict regulatory conditions. Offshore EMI/e-money institutions are not recognized as Canadian banks under the Bank Act and cannot accept deposits in Canada. An offshore EMI that offers foreign exchange, remittance, or payment services to Canadian residents must itself register as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and comply with the PCMLTFA. Consequently, the LP's liquidity and capital custody must rest with a federally regulated Schedule I bank; a foreign digital EMI may only be used as an auxiliary rail for cross-border tournament payouts or vendor disbursements, and never for client-fund custody or capital escrow.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate; a Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to federal corporate income tax. A corporate partner is taxed at the applicable corporate rate on its allocated partnership income. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access the 9% federal small-business rate on eligible active business income, subject to the business limit and associated-corporation rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. The Canada-United States treaty commonly reduces withholding to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership conditions and to 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends. Other Canadian treaties commonly reduce dividends to rates ranging from 5% to 15%, depending on ownership and treaty conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal transaction-value threshold that eliminates contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. Section 247 applies to non-arm's-length cross-border transactions involving a Canadian taxpayer, including a Canadian partnership where applicable. Form T106 reporting generally applies when aggregate reportable transactions or indebtedness with non-arm's-length non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 for the reporting period. CRA contemporaneous-documentation rules require the taxpayer to prepare or obtain specified information by the filing deadline and provide it within 3 months of a CRA request. A transfer-pricing penalty may generally apply where a net adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue, subject to statutory conditions and reasonable-efforts defenses. These thresholds are penalty or reporting thresholds and are not safe harbours for omitting arm's-length analysis.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates Economic Substance Regulations. Substance is nevertheless relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, transfer-pricing rules, sham and form-over-substance doctrines, residency and management-and-control analysis, and specific anti-avoidance rules. CRA will examine whether a Canadian partnership or corporate partner has real functions, personnel, decision-making, risks, assets, contractual capacity, and commercial purpose in Canada. A low-substance entity receiving platform, IP, financing, or management income may face denial of treaty benefits, transfer-pricing adjustments, recharacterization, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and GAAR assessments.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act criminal evasion can result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to 5 years, or both. The CRA may also assess the unpaid tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, repeated-failure penalties, and applicable provincial tax.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and individuals who participated in the conduct may face personal criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, restitution orders, and civil recovery. A limited partner is not automatically liable merely by holding a limited interest, but personal participation, direction, fraud, agency, or loss of limited-liability protection can create personal exposure. Travel restrictions may arise from bail, probation, enforcement, or court orders but are not an automatic tax penalty.
False Statements and Gross Negligence

Statutory sanction: Gross-negligence penalties are generally 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit or benefit. Other information-return penalties can apply per failure or per day, and interest applies to unpaid amounts.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly authorize or participate in false filings may incur civil and criminal exposure. Corporate status does not protect a director from liability for personal fraud or deliberate statutory contraventions.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny deductions, impose withholding tax, reassess GST/HST, apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally linked to a material net adjustment, and charge arrears interest. A corresponding adjustment is not automatic for the related foreign entity.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where they knowingly approve fabricated pricing, sham agreements, or false books. Ordinary poor judgment without personal participation does not automatically create personal liability.
Failure to File T5013 or T106

Statutory sanction: Daily information-return penalties may apply, commonly CAD 25 per day subject to the applicable statutory maximum. Repeated, intentional, or knowingly false failures may attract higher penalties and criminal consequences.

Director personal liability: The partnership's general partner and responsible officers may face enforcement action for deliberate non-compliance. Limited partners are not automatically personally liable solely because the partnership failed to file.
Part XIII Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed the unremitted withholding tax, arrears interest, a 10% or 20% failure-to-remit penalty depending on the circumstances, information-return penalties, and penalties for knowingly false treaty claims.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and other trust amounts where the corporation failed to deduct, remit, or account for them and the statutory director-liability conditions are met. A two-year limitation period generally applies to a director assessment, subject to statutory exceptions.
GST/HST Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may assess unremitted GST/HST, interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, denied input tax credits, gross-negligence penalties, and provincial component amounts. False invoices or fraudulent input tax credits may result in criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for GST/HST trust amounts where corporate failure meets the director-liability rules. Personal liability is not automatic for ordinary corporate tax debts.
Payroll and Trust-Account Diversion

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed the trust amount, interest, penalties, and enforcement costs. Knowingly diverting trust funds may support criminal charges, including fraud-related charges, depending on the conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for eligible unremitted source deductions and GST/HST amounts. Personal assets may be pursued after statutory assessment and collection steps.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the GAAR, recharacterize payments, impose withholding tax, deny deductions, assess transfer-pricing adjustments, and apply interest and penalties. Treaty relief may be denied under principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions.

Director personal liability: Individuals who create or operate the arrangement may face civil tax recovery, director liability for trust amounts, fraud or evasion prosecution, fines, imprisonment, and asset-forfeiture or restitution consequences where criminal conduct is proven. No automatic travel ban follows from shell-company status alone.
Beneficial Ownership and Nominee Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: The reduced treaty rate may be denied and the payer may be assessed the 25% statutory withholding rate, interest, penalties, and information-reporting sanctions. Knowingly false declarations can support criminal charges.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and beneficial owners who knowingly submit false ownership information may face personal civil and criminal exposure. Corporate separateness does not shield personal fraud.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code consequences may include administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, seizure, forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment. Reporting obligations depend on whether the entity is a reporting entity or otherwise subject to applicable AML rules.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face criminal prosecution, imprisonment, fines, forfeiture, restitution, and asset restraint. A travel ban may be imposed as a bail or court condition but is not an automatic statutory consequence.
Criminal Code Fraud and Forgery

Statutory sanction: Fraud over CAD 5,000 is an indictable offence punishable by imprisonment for up to 14 years; fraud at or below CAD 5,000 may be prosecuted by indictment or summary conviction. Forgery and use of forged documents can attract separate criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: The individuals who commit, direct, counsel, or knowingly assist the conduct may face imprisonment, fines, restitution, civil damages, asset seizure, and disqualification or restrictive court orders.
Corporate and Partnership Authority Abuse

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include creditor claims, oppression or derivative proceedings, fraudulent-conveyance remedies, insolvency recovery, tax collection, and director or officer liability under applicable federal or provincial corporate and insolvency law.

Director personal liability: Courts may impose civil restitution, damages, contribution, injunctions, or director disqualification. The corporate veil may be pierced in exceptional cases involving complete domination, fraud, improper purpose, or abuse of the corporate form; imprisonment requires a separate criminal offence.
Unlawful Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may recharacterize the payment as a dividend, interest, royalty, management fee, shareholder benefit, or partnership allocation and assess withholding tax, tax on benefits, interest, penalties, and denied deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face civil recovery for unlawful distributions, breach of fiduciary or statutory duties, and personal liability for unremitted trust amounts. Imprisonment requires fraudulent or criminal conduct and is not automatic.
