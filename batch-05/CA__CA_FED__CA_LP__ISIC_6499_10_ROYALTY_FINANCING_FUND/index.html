Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); Business Registration portal: Canada Revenue Agency My Business Account (MyBA) and provincial registration via Business Registration Online (BRO)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident entity establishing a Canadian limited partnership must apply to the CRA for a federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or, if already incorporated, through amended registration of a partnership. The partnership itself is assigned a BN and, for GST/HST purposes, the partnership must register through Form RC1 or the GST/HST electronic registration service if taxable supplies exceed the threshold. Non-residents may also obtain a de facto TIN through filing Form RC151, 'Application for a Business Number (BN) for Non-Residents', where applicable. Each partner must separately identify their own residence and status, and the partnership's BN is used for income tax remittance, GST/HST, payroll, and other program accounts.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canadian income tax law does not provide a formal 'check-the-box' regime analogous to the U.S.; however, a limited partnership may, by filing Form RC1 and executing a partnership agreement, elect to be treated as a partnership for both income tax and GST/HST purposes, which is the default pass-through treatment. Under Part X of the Excise Tax Act, a partnership may elect to have certain qualifying members or the partnership itself become the registrant for GST/HST. The CRA may, on application, disregard the pass-through status in certain circumstances under the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR); otherwise, the partnership remains fiscally transparent under section 96 of the Income Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For GST/HST, a person (including a non-resident carrying on business in Canada) must register once the worldwide taxable supplies (arising in Canada) exceed $30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in any 12-consecutive-month period; this threshold also applies to a limited partnership as a separate registrant for GST/HST where it is considered a person for registration purposes. HST provinces (Ontario, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island) add a provincial component (total 13–15%) but the registration threshold remains the federal $30,000 test.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods exported from Canada when the supplier is registered for GST/HST and has retained documentary evidence that the goods have been exported as part of the sale, pursuant to section 1 and Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. For offshore services and intangible supplies, zero-rating applies under sections 7 to 10 of Part V of Schedule VI where the recipient is a non-resident, the service is not performed wholly in Canada (for certain personal services), and the supply is not related to real property in Canada. For the royalty-financing fund, a non-resident royalty licensee receiving cross-border services and royalty flows may claim zero-rating if the service is supplied to the non-resident and the other statutory conditions (e.g.,, intellectual property, consultancy, financial service) are met, provided the supplier is a GST/HST registrant and the non-resident's identity and location are evidenced by a declaration of non-residency.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Form B13A / B13B or electronic export declaration as specified in D21-1-21)
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent transportation document (e.g., air waybill, ocean bill, or truck manifest)
  • Commercial invoice or sales contract showing the goods/services, value, destination, and consignee
  • Proof of export (CBSA export verification certificate or carrier documentation)
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank transfer record evidencing receipt of payment from the non-resident buyer/licensee
  • For services and intangible property: written non-residency declaration, contract with non-resident, and documentation of the place-of-use or supply

No federal real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory digital stamping regime exists in Canada as of the current statutory framework. The CRA accepts electronic invoices in standard XML/EDI/JSON formats, but submission to the CRA is not required for normal B2B transactions. The pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework (based on the PEPPOL network) is being phased in; mandatory e-invoicing for large enterprises is expected no earlier than 2027, with consultation on specific timing. For GST/HST returns, mandatory electronic filing (File via GST/HST NETFILE or GST/HST TELEFILE) applies to persons with annual taxable supplies exceeding $1.5 million, and remittance must be made electronically. The CRA does not perform pre-clearance stamping of invoices; invoices must simply contain specified GST/HST information.

Where the CRA challenges a zero-rating claim, the onus is on the registrant to provide contemporaneous documentary evidence under section 169 and CRA Memoranda 17-10 (Proof of Export) and 17-13 (Intangible and Services). Failure to substantiate results in reassessment of the tax at full GST/HST rate, plus arrears interest and statutorily prescribed penalties. Civil penalties under Excise Tax Act section 280 (false or misleading statements) may equal the greater of $250 or 25% of the net tax understated; section 285 imposes a $25 per day failure-to-provide penalty. Repeated misstatements or false documentation attract criminal sanctions under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, with fines up to $100,000 and imprisonment for up to two years, or under the Income Tax Act where tax evasion is proven. The general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) permits the CRA to deny zero-rating transactions without substantive commercial and statutory purpose, with retroactive clawback plus interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted Article VIII, Sections 2, 3 and 4 of the IMF Articles of Agreement, confirming unrestricted convertibility for current international transactions. Capital-account transactions are also generally free of central-bank approval, although legal and regulatory gates exist under the Investment Canada Act, sanctions administration, FINTRAC reporting, Part XIII non-resident withholding tax, FATCA/CRS certification and provincial securities laws. Profit remittance by a Canadian limited partnership is not subject to exchange-control licensing or CAD conversion requirements; dividends, interest, royalties and partnership distributions may be paid in any currency, but treaty-based relief and withholding-tax compliance must be evidenced before funds leave the Canadian banking system.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio or time limit. Exporters, financing funds and LPs may retain foreign-currency proceeds freely in Canada or abroad. FINTRAC requires Canadian financial institutions to report cross-border electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more in one transaction, and the Income Tax Act requires the Canadian-dollar equivalent of FX transactions to be recorded at an appropriate exchange rate, commonly the Bank of Canada daily rate. No Bank of Canada approval, surrender certificate or export-license filing is required for receipt of export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) acts as the monetary authority and exchange-rate administrator; OSFI supervises federally regulated banks; and the Department of Finance Canada retains prudential and international-financial-policy oversight. Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority because the Canadian dollar is freely floating and exchange controls were abolished.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-Medium. Offshore digital EMI and neobank accounts may be opened remotely by a Canadian LP for low-value outbound disbursements in USD, EUR or GBP, and can function as ancillary settlement wallets, but they cannot serve as the primary escrow, capital custody or operating account for a Canadian royalty financing fund. They are not Schedule I chartered banks, are not CDIC members, do not have direct access to Payments Canada clearing systems, and may not lawfully hold themselves out as deposit-taking institutions in Canada. A foreign EMI serving Canadian customers must assess FINTRAC money-services-business registration and, once the legislative framework is fully effective, registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act. In every case, the EMI is subject to the same corporate KYC, UBO disclosure, sanctions screening and source-of-funds verification as a Canadian bank, and the high perceived fintech risk profile of a revenue-based financing fund can trigger enhanced due diligence. Legal constraint: full reliance on a non-Canadian EMI for treasury concentration is neither compliant nor prudent.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route all inbound capital as SWIFT MT103 wire transfers to the Canadian LP's Operational Account at the onboarding Schedule I bank. In field 57C include the beneficiary bank BIC and account number, and in field 59 include the LP legal name exactly as registered. Canada does not maintain a mandatory domestic purpose-code table, but field 70 should carry a consistent narrative such as 'CAP CALL NO. 001 - CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION - NON-RESIDENT SUBSCRIPTION' so that the receiving bank can classify the transaction for FINTRAC cross-border EFT reporting. For a single transaction of CAD 10,000 or more, the Canadian receiving bank is required to file a cross-border electronic fund transfer report. Prior to value date, deliver the bank a board-approved capital call notice, investor KYC package, source-of-funds statement and, where the investor is an affiliate, an executed intercompany or subscription agreement. CAD domestic clearing is effected through Payments Canada Lynx and the Automated Clearing Settlement System; USD inbound wires are cleared through the Canadian bank's U.S. correspondent network. Offshore digital EMI and neobank accounts may be opened remotely by a Canadian LP for low-value outbound disbursements in USD, EUR or GBP, and can function as ancillary settlement wallets, but they cannot serve as the primary escrow, capital custody or operating account for a Canadian royalty financing fund. They are not Schedule I chartered banks, are not CDIC members, do not have direct access to Payments Canada clearing systems, and may not lawfully hold themselves out as deposit-taking institutions in Canada. A foreign EMI serving Canadian customers must assess FINTRAC money-services-business registration and, once the legislative framework is fully effective, registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act. In every case, the EMI is subject to the same corporate KYC, UBO disclosure, sanctions screening and source-of-funds verification as a Canadian bank, and the high perceived fintech risk profile of a revenue-based financing fund can trigger enhanced due diligence. Legal constraint: full reliance on a non-Canadian EMI for treasury concentration is neither compliant nor prudent.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate rate (ITA s. 123(1)); the Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96, so no entity-level federal income tax is imposed on the fund; income is allocated to partners and taxed at the partner level.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII dividend withholding tax (s. 212). The LP does not customarily declare dividends: partner distributions are returns of capital (non-taxable), allocations of Canadian business income (Part I taxable, no Part XIII), or allocations of non-business/property income (Part XIII at 25%, treaty-reduced). If CRA recharacterizes fund flows as deemed dividends under s. 212(13.1) or GAAR, the full 25% rate applies to the deemed dividend paid to non-resident partners.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

T5013 return and slips must be filed within 5 months after the partnership's fiscal year-end (May 31 for a December 31 year-end); Part XIII amounts withheld are remitted by the 15th day of the month following the month of payment or crediting.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with any related non-resident triggers Form T106 filing; contemporaneous documentation is required for all reportable transactions under s. 247(4); proposed CRA rules mandate a Local File where reportable transactions aggregate CAD 10,000,000 or more and a Master File where the global group's consolidated revenue is at least CAD 1,000,000,000 (2023 draft legislation).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain an OECD-style Economic Substance Regulations statute at the federal level, but GAAR (s. 245) and treaty beneficial-owner/PPT tests require genuine economic substance: real employees, premises, risk management, and substantive decision-making in Canada. The fund must demonstrate active origination and servicing of revenue-based financing agreements, Canadian-based operational control, and a substantive Canadian general partner to sustain flow-through treatment and treaty access. Absence of substance invites GAAR reassessment, denial of treaty benefits, and recharacterization of distributions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Returns

Statutory sanction: Fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded (ITA s. 239(1)); gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax (s. 163(2)); arrears interest; criminal conviction with imprisonment up to 2 years on summary conviction and up to 5 years on indictment; CRA audit, search warrant execution, and public enforcement disclosure.

Director personal liability: The general partner and directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII and source deductions under s. 227.1; civil restitution to the Crown; in serious prosecutions courts may impose travel bans and the CRA may seek a ne exeat order; potential investor derivative claims and regulatory disqualification.
Shell Company / GAAR-Abusive Partnership Structure

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment (s. 245) cancels the tax benefit; GAAR penalty of 25% of the tax benefit (2024 Budget Implementation Act; formerly 15%); CRA may recharacterize distributions as deemed dividends under s. 212(13.1) with Part XIII at 25% and no treaty benefit where the recipient lacks substance; interest and penalties run from the original filing due date.

Director personal liability: Piercing the corporate veil: the general partner and its directors are held liable for the aggregate tax deficiency as directors of a deemed payer; personal civil restitution for distributions made to foreign partners; court-imposed travel restrictions in fraud-linked investigations; potential winding-up and permanent director disqualification.
AML / MSB Non-Compliance (FINTRAC)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 per violation for entities under the PCMLTFA; criminal fines and imprisonment up to 5 years for wilful contravention; forced removal from ACH/payment-rail networks; enhanced regulatory scrutiny of the fund and its banking correspondents.

Director personal liability: Senior officers and directors of the general partner may be personally named in FINTRAC enforcement for failure to supervise; individual fines, prohibition orders, travel restrictions attached to bail or criminal proceedings, and civil liability to merchant counterparties for losses caused by AML failures.
FATCA/CRS Reporting and Registrable Financial Institution Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA penalties of CAD 2,500 per failure to file FATCA/CRS information returns, additional CAD 100 per day for continued failure, and re-computation of the year's Part XIII exposure at 25% for any unreported passive income; automatic exchange of information with treaty partners triggers foreign tax audits of non-resident partners.

Director personal liability: Designated partners and directors are exposed to aggregate penalty assessments where the fund was reportable at year-end and due diligence was neglected; civil liability to investors whose foreign tax positions are compromised; removal from AEOI-compliant financial institution registries and reputational sanctions.
