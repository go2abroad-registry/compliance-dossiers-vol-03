Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/businesses/business-number.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 with CRA by mail, telephone, or online via the Business Registration Online portal. The limited partnership must register for applicable program accounts, including GST/HST (RT account) if making taxable supplies in Canada, payroll (RP) if employing staff, and corporate tax (RC) if an assessment is required. Non-resident limited partners should ensure compliance with section 229 of the Income Tax Regulations through a T5013 partnership information return and any required Form NR4 or Part XIII withholding filings.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible_Territorial_Scope

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box election for entity classification; a limited partnership is automatically fiscally transparent for federal income tax on a sharing basis. Non-resident partners may invoke treaty tie-breaker provisions under the Canada-US Tax Treaty and file a section 220 election to set out tax payment arrangements. Hybrid mismatch election or disclosure obligations under section 18.6 of the Income Tax Act may apply where an entity is treated as non-transparent in a foreign jurisdiction; a partnership may also elect under subsection 150(2) to file a partnership return for a non-calendar fiscal period.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 in worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter and over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (non-resident small-supplier test)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported by the supplier to a non-resident person may be zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier establishes exportation through documentary evidence. Services supplied to non-resident persons for use wholly or mainly outside Canada may also be zero-rated under Part V, subject to specific exceptions for Canadian real property, tangible personal property, and services rendered to non-residents physically present in Canada. For market research survey platform services involving cross-border consumer panels, the service must clearly be delivered to and used by non-resident clients outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13 or electronic declaration submitted through Canada Border Services Agency)
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill / Cargo Control Document
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Proof of Payment or Inward Remittance Certificate
  • Export records retained under section 286 of the Excise Tax Act

No mandatory electronic invoicing or real-time fiscal clearance regime under federal GST/HST. Electronic invoices are acceptable provided they are legible and retained in accordance with section 286 of the Excise Tax Act. There is no API-based customs clearance or digital stamping requirement for ordinary export transactions.

CRA may reassess and deny zero-rating if evidence is inadequate or false. The Excise Tax Act imposes a minimum penalty of CAD $5,000 under subsection 285(1) for false statements, and under subsection 285(2) a penalty of 50% of the excess refund or credit for gross negligence. Interest runs from the date of claim. Criminal prosecution is possible for fraudulent evasion, subject to fines up to CAD $500,000 and/or imprisonment, and such claims may also attract penalty tax and clawback of refunds with compounding statutory interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under both the current and capital account. The Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act impose no restrictions on cross-border capital flows, dividend remittances, or profit repatriation attributable to a limited partnership. Non-resident partners may receive their share of distributable profits from a Canadian LP via wire transfer without prior regulatory approval. No withholding tax treaty override applies at the federal level except standard Part XIII withholding considerations under the Income Tax Act (Canada).

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada has no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no minimum percentage of export receipts must be converted into CAD or retained domestically. Receipts may be held in any OECD currency and settled through ordinary banking channels.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Currency and Monetary Authority); OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) for financial institution regulation; no dedicated foreign exchange control authority as Canada maintains a liberalized capital account.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

High feasibility for select licenses only. Wise (Wake Forest, UK / EU EMI) and PayPal Mass Payout are acceptable for outward disbursements and cross-border payout rails but are not substitutes for a Canadian bank account in satisfying OSFI-regulated banking requirements. An unlicensed offshore EMI cannot provide a true Canadian clearing settlement account; any digital EMI offering CAD-denominated accounts must hold a Canadian MSB (Money Services Business) registration under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and FINTRAC compliance program. For institutional liquidity, a Schedule I Canadian bank remains mandatory.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocol: inbound capital contributions from non-resident limited partners must be remitted via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 to the Canadian LP's CAD/USD concentration account held at a Schedule I bank. The bank requires the beneficiary legal entity identifier (LEI) and partnership registration number. Because Canada has no foreign exchange declaration requirement, the remitter is not required to assign a unique purpose code; however, Canadian banks commonly request an internal reason code such as 'Capital Investment – Contribution to Partnership' for OFSI and financial reporting. Supporting documents include the executed capital call notice, the partnership agreement, and source of funds letter from each contributing partner. For EFT domestic wires, Canadian banks use the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) / RTGS equivalent, and US-dollar transfers clear via CHIPS/Fedwire with Canadian bank correspondent. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate rate is 15.0% after the 10% provincial abatement and 13% general rate reduction from the basic 38.0% statutory rate

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to non-residents; for the LP itself, distributions of partnership income are not dividends and are taxable at the partner level, but any distributions from a corporate partner with dividend characterization are subject to 25% before treaty relief.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no statutory monetary threshold for mandatory local or master file documentation in all circumstances, but contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation must be prepared for controlled cross-border transactions when the aggregate annual value exceeds CA$5 million to safeguard relief from the 10% transfer pricing penalty; country-by-country reporting applies where group consolidated revenue exceeds CA$750 million (Form RC4645).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) similar to Caribbean regimes; however, the CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule (section 245), common-law economic substance doctrines, and the transfer pricing provisions of section 247 to recharacterize facially artificial transactions. The Canadian limited partnership must demonstrate real business functions, personnel, assets, and risks located in Canada; a letter-box entity with no commercial substance cannot rely on treaty benefits and may have allocations reassessed as dividends or management fees.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, on indictment a fine of between 100% and 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to five years; on summary conviction a fine of 50% to 100% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to two years. Gross negligence penalty is 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and tax advisors who authorized or acquiesced in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for civil penalties and the evaded tax; courts may order restitution, prohibit travel pending trial, and impose prison sentences.
Transfer Pricing Misstatements

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CA$5 million or 10% of gross revenue for the year; interest compounds daily on the reassessed taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners can be held civilly liable for the resulting corporate tax debt if they were negligent or directed the non-compliant pricing; individuals who knowingly participate in pricing fraud are exposed to criminal sanctions and personal restitution orders.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may disregard the partnership under section 245 GAAR and reallocate income to the active Canadian source; treaty benefits may be denied; avoidance penalties of up to 50% of the tax benefit can apply, plus interest and exposure to gross-negligence penalties.
