E-government filing portal

(2) Apply to the CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and program accounts via BRO (Form RC1 for domestic entities; non-residents use the simplified non-resident registration route and Form RC348). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope: a Business Corporation is a mandatory separate taxpayer with no US-style pass-through election. Resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations are taxable only on Canadian-source income attributable to a permanent establishment in Canada. No corporate income tax 'tax-exempt zone' applies to Ontario manufacturing operations. CCPC status may reduce the federal/Ontario small-business rates on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income, but the entity remains taxed at the corporate level.

Available election forms

No US-style 'check-the-box' classification exists under Canadian law. Material elections for a cross-border enterprise: (i) subsection 219(1)/(2) election (branch-tax election) allowing a treaty-resident non-resident corporation to file Part I returns and compute the Part XIV 25% branch tax (treaty-reduced) only on after-tax Canadian business earnings; (ii) section 216 election for net-basis taxation of Canadian rental income; (iii) Excise Tax Act section 150 election treating taxable supplies between closely related corporations (90% common ownership) as made for no consideration (non-taxable); (iv) Excise Tax Act section 167 election to zero-rate the transfer of a business as a going concern; (v) hybrid-mismatch neutrality rules effective 1 July 2022 (Income Tax Act ss. 18.15 et seq.) deny deductions for hybrid financial-instrument and entity-mismatch payments — no elective carve-out permits a taxpayer-favourable hybrid classification; CRA may impose a conforming deduction-inclusion adjustment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in gross annual worldwide taxable supplies in the current and preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (Excise Tax Act ss. 240-241 small-supplier threshold). Once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory unless the supplier is a non-resident that does not carry on business in Canada and does not solicit orders in Canada. Voluntary registration is available, and in practice necessary, for an export manufacturer to zero-rate foreign sales and claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: (a) goods must be sold to a non-resident buyer, with delivery/transfer of title and risk (CIF/FOB/Ex-Works terms) to the export destination; (b) the exporter must cause the goods to be exported 'as soon as is reasonable after the supply' and not for consumption, use, or supply in Canada; (c) the supplier must obtain and retain prescribed export evidence (carrier waybill, bill of lading, export declaration, or CRA-accepted proof) within that period; (d) customs duties/regulatory controls must be satisfied (CBSA export report for goods above CAD 2,000 or controlled goods). Offshore services supplied to non-resident persons (e.g., design/engineering of custom keycaps) are zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V s. 7 where the recipient is a non-resident and not present in Canada at the time of performance. A failure to satisfy these conditions converts the supply to a domestic taxable supply subject to 13% HST.

  • Canada Export Declaration (B13A) filed electronically with the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting Program (mandatory for goods valued above CAD 2,000 or controlled goods destined for restricted end-uses)
  • Commercial invoice showing sale to the non-resident buyer, Incoterms, unit values, and Harmonized System classification (keyboard keycap components, e.g., HS 8473.30/3926.90 as advised by customs broker)
  • Bill of lading or air waybill (carrier documentation evidencing physical export from Canada to the foreign destination)
  • Courier/Canada Post export waybill and delivery confirmation for low-value direct-to-consumer mail shipments
  • Packing list (quantity, weight, marks) reconciling shipment contents to the commercial invoice
  • CRA 'evidence of export' file: carrier certificate, freight-forwarder certificate, or CRA-compliant alternative proof retained for each export transaction
  • Inward remittance certificate / bank settlement statement or payment-processor payout record (Shopify Payments/Stripe) evidencing receipt and FX attribution of export proceeds
  • Purchase order or marketplace order record evidencing the non-resident customer and contract of sale

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based invoicing validation. Electronic books and records must be retained in a CRA-accessible, machine-readable format. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework (Peppol BIS 3.0) is voluntary for B2B/G2B use. Under Excise Tax Act marketplace rules effective 1 July 2021, platform operators that control supplies made by non-resident sellers through the platform must collect, report, and remit GST/HST; third-party settlement organisations (e.g., Stripe, Shopify Payments) are subject to phased-in TPSO information reporting to CRA. GST/HST returns are filed electronically via NETFILE/My Business Account.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess within the normal 4-year limitation period; the period is unlimited where the return is false or fraudulent or where misrepresentation results from neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, permitting retrospective clawback of denied zero-rating, disallowed input tax credits, assessed 13% HST on reconsidered domestic supplies, and prescribed daily compound interest. Primary penalties: (i) gross negligence penalty under Excise Tax Act s. 285 equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the net tax understatement; (ii) failure-to-register/file penalties of 1% of net tax plus surcharges; (iii) wilful evasion prosecution under ITA s. 239 / ETA s. 327 with fines of 50-200% of the evaded amount and imprisonment up to 5 years. Documentary gaps (missing B13A, late dispatch, circular payment or unrepatriated proceeds beyond the 180-day benchmark) create a presumption of domestic supply and trigger full reassessment.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal and 11.5% Ontario. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation generally receives a combined rate of 12.2% on eligible active business income within the CAD 500,000 small-business limit: 9% federal and 3.2% Ontario. Manufacturing and processing income may qualify for reduced provincial rates, subject to statutory classification and eligibility requirements.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the recipient is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership thresholds and 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends; the exact rate depends on the recipient's residence, ownership, limitation-on-benefits provisions, and beneficial ownership.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any corporate tax balance is generally due two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for a qualifying CCPC meeting the statutory conditions. Instalments are due on the applicable monthly or quarterly due dates.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires taxpayers to make reasonable efforts to determine and use arm's-length prices for cross-border non-arm's-length transactions. The CRA may request documentation, which is generally due within three months of a written request. T106 reporting generally applies at an aggregate CAD 1,000,000 threshold for reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, translated under applicable rules. Canada does not impose a universal BEPS-style Master File filing threshold for all corporations, but OECD-style local-file and master-file information may be expected for large multinational groups.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a separate general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to the UAE or certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose and beneficial-ownership concepts where applicable, transfer-pricing rules, residence and central-management-and-control analysis, debt characterization, withholding rules, and deductibility requirements. A corporation should maintain Canadian commercial purpose, decision-making evidence, personnel or appropriately documented outsourced functions, premises or operational access, bank and accounting controls, inventory and manufacturing records, and risk-bearing capacity consistent with its claimed activities. A shell arrangement lacking commercial substance may face denial of deductions, treaty benefits, loss utilization, or other tax attributes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, repeated-failure penalties, and prosecution. Income Tax Act tax evasion can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. Criminal Code fraud may carry imprisonment of up to 14 years where the fraud exceeds CAD 5,000.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability for personal participation, authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness. Corporate tax debt does not ordinarily become a director's personal debt, but directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under director-liability provisions, subject to due-diligence defenses. Courts may order restitution or fines; a general automatic tax-debt travel ban does not apply, although release conditions, warrants, criminal bail orders, or immigration consequences may restrict travel.
Failure to remit payroll source deductions

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, penalties generally ranging from 10% to 20% depending on the failure, and possible criminal prosecution for wilful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions if the corporation fails to pay and the director cannot establish statutory due diligence. Personal assets may be pursued after the statutory conditions are satisfied.
GST/HST non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of net tax, interest, penalties for late filing or remittance, denial of input tax credits, gross-negligence penalties, and prosecution for fraud or tax evasion. Canadian resident businesses generally must assess the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold and applicable Ontario HST obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST under the Excise Tax Act, subject to statutory limitation periods and due-diligence defenses. Personal liability can include assessed tax, interest, and penalties.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing adjustments, secondary adjustments, imputed benefits, denial of deductions, withholding-tax assessments, contemporaneous-documentation penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjusted amount where statutory conditions are met, interest, and possible application of the general anti-avoidance rule.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil exposure for knowingly approving fraudulent records or unlawful distributions and criminal liability for intentional participation. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments normally remain corporate liabilities, but source-deduction failures can create personal director liability.
Unlawful profit distribution and capital-maintenance breach

Statutory sanction: Repayment or restoration claims, shareholder and director liability under the applicable federal or Ontario corporate statute, possible creditor remedies, tax reassessment of dividends or benefits, and interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors approving unlawful dividends may be required to indemnify the corporation and creditors. Shareholder recipients may also be required to repay distributions. The corporate veil is not routinely pierced, but fraud, bad faith, oppressive conduct, and statutory director-liability provisions can produce personal claims.
Shell company, sham residence, or beneficial-ownership concealment

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, GAAR reassessment, withholding-tax liability, denial of expenses or losses, corporate-record penalties, beneficial-ownership reporting consequences, and possible criminal charges where deception, laundering, or tax evasion is proved. A shell company is not independently illegal solely because it has limited operations.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and criminal exposure for false corporate records, fraud, tax evasion, or money laundering. There is no automatic imprisonment merely for low substance, but intentional concealment can support imprisonment, fines, restitution, asset seizure, and director-disqualification consequences.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties, account suspension, seizure and forfeiture, criminal money-laundering charges, and imprisonment. Proceeds-of-crime money laundering can attract imprisonment of up to 10 years for indictable offences, with higher consequences for certain aggravating circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, beneficial owners, and employees may be prosecuted individually. Corporate liability does not shield persons who direct, authorize, or knowingly facilitate laundering. Travel restrictions may arise from bail, warrants, forfeiture proceedings, or international cooperation.
Customs, import valuation, and product-origin violations

Statutory sanction: Additional duties, GST at import, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, importer-of-record sanctions, and prosecution for customs fraud.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly submit false customs declarations may face personal fines, prosecution, restitution, and forfeiture-related orders. Directors may also be liable where they authorized or acquiesced in the conduct.
Corporate-record, beneficial-ownership, and annual-return failure

Statutory sanction: Late fees, administrative penalties, loss of good standing, dissolution or cancellation proceedings, inability to access certain corporate services, and possible offence prosecution under the applicable corporate statute.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory fines and personal liability for knowingly false filings or failure to maintain required records. Dissolution does not automatically extinguish tax, creditor, or director-liability claims.
Product safety, consumer protection, and intellectual-property infringement

Statutory sanction: Product recalls, injunctions, seizure, administrative penalties, damages, accounting of profits, statutory copyright or trademark remedies, and criminal prosecution for counterfeit or deceptive conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable for knowing, directing, or participating in infringement, deceptive marketing, or unsafe-product conduct. Corporate indemnification may be unavailable for fraud or bad faith.
