Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the CRA Business Registration Online service, My Business Account portal, and GST/HST administration. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers commercial import and export customs processes. Ontario corporate income tax is generally administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial tax administration framework. Ontario separately administers certain provincial taxes through the Ministry of Finance.

E-government filing portal

A corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number (BN), generally through Business Registration Online, telephone registration, or incorporation-related registration where available. The corporation must register for the applicable CRA program accounts, including an RC corporate income tax account and an RT GST/HST account where registration is required or elected. A non-resident corporation must provide legal name, jurisdiction of incorporation, registered address, Canadian business details, authorized representative information, and supporting incorporation documents. The CRA may require a Canadian address, books and records, security, or additional identity verification depending on the activity. Commercial importers and exporters must obtain a CBSA importer-exporter program account and comply with the Canada Border Services Agency Assessment and Revenue Management framework. A Canadian corporation does not generally receive a separate provincial corporate income tax number for Ontario; its BN and CRA program accounts are used for federal and Ontario corporate income tax reporting. A foreign corporation carrying on business in Canada may also require a T2 corporate income tax return and must assess permanent establishment, treaty residence, branch tax, and withholding obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no United States-style check-the-box election for ordinary corporations. A corporation incorporated in Canada is generally a separate taxable person subject to federal and applicable provincial corporate income tax. A non-resident corporation may be treated as carrying on business in Canada based on Canadian domestic law, subject to applicable treaty protection and permanent-establishment rules. Partnerships are generally fiscally transparent, while trusts and certain other entities have separate statutory classification rules. Available elections are transaction- or regime-specific, including eligible capital reorganizations, amalgamations, wind-ups, section 85 transfers, section 86 share exchanges, foreign affiliate elections, GST/HST registration and filing-frequency elections, and certain GST/HST elections. Canada has anti-hybrid mismatch rules that can deny deductions or impose income inclusions for specified hybrid arrangements; these are not elective entity-classification rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, for a single person in one calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, subject to the detailed small-supplier rules and associated-person aggregation. A supplier may voluntarily register below the threshold. The CAD 30,000 threshold generally does not apply in the same manner to charities, for which a CAD 50,000 threshold generally applies. A non-resident may be required to register where it makes taxable supplies in Canada in the course of commercial activity, even if it has no Canadian corporation. Registration, collection, filing, and place-of-supply analysis must be assessed separately for goods, services, digital supplies, and imported goods.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST/HST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied and the supplier maintains adequate evidence that the goods were exported or delivered for export in accordance with the Excise Tax Act and related regulations. Typical evidence includes the commercial invoice, purchase order, shipping documents, carrier records, customs export or accounting records, proof of delivery outside Canada, and payment records. Goods shipped to a non-resident purchaser may qualify where the supplier delivers the goods to a destination outside Canada or otherwise satisfies the prescribed export rules; a non-resident customer's status alone is insufficient if the goods remain in Canada. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated where the applicable exported-service rules are met, commonly requiring that the recipient be a non-resident and that the service not relate to Canadian real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or other statutory exclusions. Services performed in Canada, or services concerning goods used in Canada, require separate analysis. A GST/HST registrant may generally recover eligible GST/HST incurred in commercial activities through input tax credits, including where output supplies are zero-rated. A cash refund may be available where input tax credits exceed net tax, subject to filing, documentation, and CRA verification. Dental ceramic blocks and milling discs must be separately reviewed for any domestic medical-device zero-rating provisions; export zero-rating remains dependent on the export and documentary rules.

  • CBSA export declaration or other applicable export-reporting record, including Canadian Export Reporting System or applicable commercial export documentation
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, purchaser, goods, value, currency, terms of sale, destination, and tax treatment
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier document showing shipment from Canada to a destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and proof of delivery or destination receipt, where applicable
  • Customs entry, import clearance, or destination-country import documentation where available
  • Purchase order, sales contract, and customer correspondence establishing the export transaction
  • Proof of payment, including bank transfer or other settlement records; an inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian statutory document
  • GST/HST return and general-ledger records reconciling zero-rated revenue to the export documentation
  • Certificate, permit, or product-specific regulatory documentation where required for dental devices, controlled goods, or the destination jurisdiction

Canada has no general national real-time electronic invoice clearance or mandatory tax-authority pre-approval system for ordinary B2B GST/HST invoices. A GST/HST registrant must issue compliant invoices or other supporting documentation showing prescribed information, including supplier identity and GST/HST registration number where applicable, date, description, consideration, tax rate, tax amount or a legally permitted indication, and recipient information where required for input tax credit support. Electronic invoices and electronic records are generally acceptable if their authenticity, integrity, accessibility, and readability are maintained. CRA electronic filing and payment requirements apply to specified registrants and filing situations. Exporters must retain customs, shipping, commercial, accounting, payment, and customer records sufficient to establish zero-rating and input tax credits, generally for six years from the end of the relevant year or longer where an objection, appeal, or statutory extension applies. PEPPOL or other e-invoicing networks are not generally mandatory for private-sector Ontario manufacturers, although public procurement or contractual requirements may apply.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated to Canada within 180 days. The stated 180-day value is therefore not an applicable Canadian mandatory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline. Canada generally permits foreign-currency receipts to remain offshore, subject to tax reporting, transfer-pricing, anti-avoidance, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, customs, and foreign-affiliate rules. CRA scrutiny may test whether the goods actually left Canada, whether the supplier was the exporter, whether the transaction was completed with the claimed recipient, whether services met the exported-service conditions, and whether supporting records are contemporaneous and internally consistent. If zero-rating is denied, GST/HST may be assessed retroactively with applicable interest. Input tax credits or refunds may be denied or recovered where documentation is deficient. Administrative penalties may apply for false statements, omissions, failure to file, failure to remit, or failure to maintain records; gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, subject to statutory rules. Customs non-compliance may result in CBSA assessments, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, interest, and reassessment under the customs legislation. Deliberate tax evasion or fraudulent documentation may result in prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. The normal reassessment period is generally four years for GST/HST returns, subject to extended periods for misrepresentation, fraud, failure to file, or other statutory circumstances.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts under a free-floating exchange-rate regime. Canada maintains no exchange-control statute, no central-bank conversion permit, and no repatriation limit on dividends, interest, royalties, service fees, or branch profits; such outbound amounts are instead subject to Income Tax Act Part XIII withholding (25% statutory rate, reduced by treaty, e.g., 5%–15% on dividends under the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention). Inbound direct investment in a new Ontario manufacturing subsidiary is unrestricted for banking and FX purposes; only acquisitions of control of an existing Canadian business trigger Investment Canada Act notification or net-benefit review at indexed thresholds, plus a separate national-security review. All cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC, but this is an informational obligation, not a capital-control mechanism.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no foreign-exchange surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory repatriation deadline on export proceeds. The Ontario manufacturer may invoice in CAD, USD, EUR, or CNY, retain export receivables in multicurrency operating accounts at a Schedule I bank, and convert at commercial discretion. Exports of dental zirconia blocks are zero-rated for GST/HST, which requires a CRA business number, commercial invoice, and export documentation; large foreign-currency receipts remain subject to FINTRAC EFT reporting thresholds, but no percentage of proceeds must be surrendered to the Bank of Canada.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority and operator of the Lynx RTGS settlement account; prudential regulation of Canadian banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision and cross-border EFT reporting by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); securities and derivatives oversight for any capital-raising by the Ontario Securities Commission (OSC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low as a primary treasury rail; Medium as an ancillary sub-CAD 10,000 remittance corridor. An offshore digital EMI (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer) cannot lawfully act as the principal settlement bank for a Canadian manufacturing taxpayer unless registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and, once fully in force, compliant with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); unregistered offshore EMIs are typically refused by correspondent banks and expose the corporation to CDIC deposit-insurance gaps, custodial trust-risk, and limited enforceability of funds in foreign insolvency proceedings. Use EMIs only for fragmented trade receipts or refunds; route payroll, GST/HST remittances, documentary trade, capex, and intercompany flows exclusively through a Schedule I bank.
