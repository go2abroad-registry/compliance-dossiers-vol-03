Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — GST/HST registration and filings via CRA My Business Account portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must register for GST/HST through the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service or by completing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). Registration requires legal entity name, Canadian business address or authorized representative, description of activity, and a non-resident registration may be obtained even without Canadian residency if the entity conducts a taxable supply in Canada. Upon registration, a 9-digit Business Number (BN) plus an RT/RM/RC program account suffix is assigned. A Limited Partnership that is not itself a taxpayer must nevertheless register for the GST/HST where it makes taxable supplies in Canada; the partnership is the registrant and the partners are jointly and severally liable for its GST/HST obligations in certain circumstances. Where the LP has no permanent establishment but sells digital or intangible services to Canadian customers, it may trigger the place-of-supply rules and be required to register under the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration regime.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box election. A Canadian Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act: the partnership itself is not a taxpayer, and each partner computes its share of partnership income as if earned or incurred directly. Available elections include an election under subsection 216(1) of the Income Tax Act for a non-resident partner to file a Canadian return on net taxable Canadian rental income instead of the gross Part XIII withholding; an election under section 217 for non-resident employees; and a partnership may elect to have a non-calendar taxation year under subsection 249.1(4) in limited circumstances. Canada has not enacted domestic hybrid mismatch election rules; BEAT or equivalent domestic anti-hybrid legislation is not in force, though the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 remains available to challenge abusive hybrid mismatch arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (small supplier threshold) in worldwide taxable supplies other than financial services over the preceding four calendar quarters; once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. Non-resident vendors supplying digital services or intangibles to Canadian consumers may be required to register under the simplified regime, with the same CAD 30,000 threshold applied to Canadian-source taxable supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the exporter produces satisfactory evidence of export, including that the goods were exported by or on behalf of the supplier, ownership transfer occurred outside Canada, and the exporter retained records proving export within a reasonable time. Services supplied to a non-resident are generally zero-rated if the service is provided to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST, and the service is not performed in Canada in a manner that is part of the non-resident's Canadian business or relates to Canadian real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. For a kernel-level anti-cheat gaming middleware provider, zero-rating applies to annual licence fees or subscription fees for software and middleware charged to non-resident gaming operators or publishers, provided the service is not deemed supplied in Canada under the place-of-supply rules and adequate supporting documentation is retained.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency B13A or approved electronic equivalent)
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill or shipping documents demonstrating physical export from Canada
  • Inward remittance certificate, bank wire confirmation, or other financial proof of payment from the non-resident purchaser

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance regime, no mandatory digital stamping, and no statutory electronic invoicing authentication mandate for GST/HST. E-invoices are not legally required, although they are recognized as valid documentation if they contain the prescribed information. Registrants with annual taxable sales exceeding CAD 1.5 million must file GST/HST returns electronically using CRA's GST/HST NETFILE, EDI, or My Business Account APIs; no government pre-clearance or real-time API approval of individual invoices is required.

The CRA requires exporters to retain adequate documentary evidence of export for six years; failure to maintain substantiation can result in reassessment of the zero-rated supply at 5% GST and denial of related input tax credits. Civil penalties under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act may apply to false statements or omissions, up to 25% of the amount misstated. Where fraud or wilful evasion is established, prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act exposes the taxpayer to a penalty of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years. Retrospective clawbacks are available under the normal reassessment period, generally four years after the reporting date, which is extended without limitation where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default is shown.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates under a free-floating and fully convertible currency regime for both current and capital account transactions; no exchange controls are currently imposed under the Currency Act, and no prior approval is required for foreign direct investment, capital contribution, dividend, or profit remittance by a non-resident limited partner. Remittances are nonetheless subject to Canadian tax compliance, including Part XIII withholding tax on non-resident distributions or royalty payments at the statutory rate of 25%, potentially reduced under the relevant Canada Income Tax Convention. Any financial institution executing such flows remains subject to FINTRAC large-value transaction and cross-border reporting obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender requirement or mandatory conversion ratio applies in Canada; software and middleware exporters may retain foreign currency earnings offshore or domestically in foreign currency accounts. However, cross-border movements of currency or monetary instruments equal to or exceeding C$10,000 must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency (Form K217), and international electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary and financial stability authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as the prudential regulator of federally incorporated banks under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46); Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the AML/CFT supervisor and beneficiary reporting authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Moderately feasible with legal constraints; offshore digital EMIs are not members of Payments Canada and cannot directly access the Lynx high-value payment system or CAD clearing settlements. A foreign EMI may operate only through a registered Canadian Money Services Business (MSB) or a sponsor arrangement with a Schedule I bank, subject to FINTRAC registration, Canadian funds safeguarding rules, and OSFI outsourcing expectations. Stripe is viable as a merchant payment acquisition rail for subscription and usage-based billing, but the legal entity treasury accounts should be held with a domestic Schedule I bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate rate on taxable income of a resident corporate partner; the limited partnership itself is fiscally transparent and bears no entity-level federal Part I tax

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII on Canadian-source dividends paid or credited to non-resident shareholders; under the Canada-US treaty the rate is 5% for a US corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the voting shares and 15% in all other ordinary cases. For a Canadian LP, commercial profit distributions are withdrawals of previously allocated partnership income, not dividends; the character of the underlying income governs the Canadian tax treatment.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 must be filed for each non-arm's length non-resident person when the aggregate fair market value of all specified transactions with that person in the taxation year exceeds CAD $1,000,000; at that level contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation satisfying ITA section 247(4) must exist. Canadian law does not separately prescribe OECD 'Local File' or 'Master File' forms, but CRA administrative practice expects OECD-aligned documentation for material controlled transactions and an OECD Master File for multinational groups with consolidated revenue above CAD $100,000,000; for the gaming middleware LP, all intercompany software license, distribution, and service arrangements above the CAD $1,000,000 threshold should be benchmarked annually and updated to reflect the most recent fiscal period.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer a discrete Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to the UAE 'ESR' framework, but the same design principle is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA section 245), the common-law sham doctrine, and the substance-over-form doctrine. A Canadian LP is respected only if it has real economic substance: enforceable partnership agreement, genuine capital contributions, active management by the general partner, business premises, employees and a commercial purpose other than tax savings. CRA may recharacterize artificial allocations, deny the partnership's existence, apply GAAR, impose gross-negligence penalties, and deny treaty benefits where an LP is a shell or is used to shift Canadian gaming-security software profit to a low-tax non-resident affiliate without operational substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion / false information return

Statutory sanction: Gross-negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax under ITA section 163(2); criminal prosecution under ITA section 239(1) with fines of 50% to 200% of evaded federal and provincial tax and imprisonment up to two years; prescribed daily compound interest applies to all reassessed balances.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be liable under ITA section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions or Part XIII withholding; in fraud cases courts may pierce the corporate veil to reach the general partner, its directors and controlling beneficiaries; criminal tax convictions render non-resident principals inadmissible to Canada and subject to travel restrictions in connection with extradition and enforcement proceedings.
Artificial loss allocation / shell LP / sham entity

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under GAAR invalidating the avoidance transaction, denial of loss and deduction claims, imposition of the 50% gross-negligence penalty under section 163(2), and possible de facto recharacterization of the arrangement as a taxable corporation or association; CRA may also impose T106 penalties and Part XIII withholding liabilities.

Director personal liability: The corporate general partner and its directors can be jointly and severally assessed for the resulting tax and withholding obligations; the partnership veil will not shield participants where the LP is a mere instrumentality, sham or fraudulent conveyance, and directors who knowingly participate in tax avoidance may face criminal liability.
