Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal at canada.ca/business-registration; Ontario Ministry of Finance (33 King Street West, Oshawa, ON) for Ontario provincial tax registrations, including the Ontario Employer Health Tax (EHT) and Ontario retail sales tax (PST) account administration.

E-government filing portal

For a federally or provincially incorporated Business Corporation (Inc./Corp.) in Ontario, obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by completing the Business Registration Online (BRO) application or filing Form RC1 after incorporation. The corporation must supply its registered office address, directors' names and residential addresses, jurisdiction of incorporation, and a description of commercial activities. CRA then issues the 9-digit Business Number and program accounts: RC (Corporate Income Tax), RT (GST/HST), RP (Payroll), and RD (supplier). Non-resident corporations without Canadian incorporation but carrying on business in Canada must use the same BRO/RC1 process, provide a Canadian agent for service, and obtain the relevant program accounts; Ontario-specific accounts are then registered through the Ontario Ministry of Finance (ONe-key portal).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box pass-through election. Available elections include: (i) a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada may elect under subsection 219(2) of the Income Tax Act to compute tax on Canadian business income on a net basis in lieu of the branch tax; (ii) a non-resident owner of Canadian real property may elect under section 216 of the Income Tax Act to be taxed on net rental income; (iii) closely related corporations may elect under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act to treat qualifying supplies as non-taxable for GST/HST; (iv) a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may forgo the small business deduction by filing an election under subsection 125(1) or comply with associated-company deeming rules; and (v) hybrid mismatch rules are applied under section 18.4 and Part XVI.1 of the Income Tax Act, but no elective pass-through status exists.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (for resident suppliers, worldwide taxable supplies; for non-resident suppliers, taxable supplies made in Canada, excluding supplies of capital property and financial supplies). Exceeding this threshold mandates GST/HST registration; below it, registration is voluntary subject to the small supplier rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods are zero-rated (0% GST/HST) where the goods are exported by or on behalf of the vendor under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act and the goods are delivered to an address outside Canada. For color Doppler ultrasound consoles (ISIC 3250 / NAICS 334510), the manufacturer must ship the goods to a foreign buyer, file a valid export declaration, and retain proof of export evidencing movement from Canada to the foreign destination. Services performed outside Canada or relating to exported goods may also be zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST. The vendor must be a GST/HST registrant to claim input tax credits (ITCs) attributable to zero-rated exports; recovery of ITCs is the primary 'refund' mechanism.

  • Customs Export Declaration (electronic eManifest filed with the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) via the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA))
  • Commercial invoice / Canada Customs Invoice (CCI) showing the foreign purchaser, HS classification (9018.12 for ultrasound equipment), unit prices, and terms of sale (Incoterms)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (air) issued by the carrier, plus a non-negotiable copy for the exporter's records
  • Freight forwarder's packing list and carrier receipts showing export from a Canadian port
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank transfer confirmation evidencing receipt of export proceeds in Canadian dollars or converted foreign currency
  • Purchase order, proforma invoice acceptance, and final invoice demonstrating the commercial sale to the foreign buyer

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API pre-approval, or digital stamping regime applies in Canada. Electronic invoices are accepted provided the supplier has the recipient's consent and the invoice contains the prescribed information under section 221.1 of the Excise Tax Act (supplier name, GST/HST registration number, invoice date, recipient name, description of supply, consideration, and tax charged). Records must be retained for six years in PDF, EDI, XML, or similar machine-readable formats. A future PEPPOL-based national e-invoicing framework remains voluntary at this stage.

The CRA and CBSA conduct post-assessment audits, cross-check export declarations against trade data, and share information under international mutual-assistance agreements. If zero-rating is claimed without valid proof of export, the CRA may reassess within the four-year normal reassessment period (or longer in cases of misrepresentation or fraud) and apply GST/HST at 13% to the full selling price, disallow input tax credits, and add statutory interest and penalties. False or fraudulent export claims attract penalties under section 285 and 327.4 of the Excise Tax Act (25% of the false amount, or 50% for gross negligence), and deliberate tax evasion under subsection 327(1) can lead to criminal fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. Income tax consequences under subsections 163(2) and 163(3) of the Income Tax Act may also apply for false statements or wilful omissions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a full free-floating currency regime with no exchange controls or restrictions on current or capital account transactions under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible for all residents and non-residents. Profit remittances, dividends, interest, and royalties may be paid abroad freely through authorized financial institutions; however, all transactions are subject to FINTRAC reporting thresholds and anti-money-laundering (AML) vetting. Ontario-specific thin capitalization and withholding tax rules apply to cross-border payments, not exchange-control authorization. For repatriation of export sales, no separate exchange control permission is required. The federal government may impose administrative sanctions only under trade or trade-sanctions law, not under monetary conversion rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory obligation to surrender export proceeds to the central bank, no fixed conversion ratio, and no mandatory repatriation timeline under Canadian federal or Ontario provincial law. Exporters may retain export receivables in foreign currency in domestic or offshore accounts. However, for corporate tax and GST/HST compliance, export revenue must be recognized on the accrual basis and may need to be translated to CAD in accordance with International Financial Reporting Standards and Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) guidelines. If a foreign-currency bank account is held in Canada, FINTRAC reporting requirements apply for transactions above CAD 10,000. For outbound payment execution for import settlements, banks will ask for appropriate purpose-of-payment codes, though Canadian law does not mandate a specific surrender dossier.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank); Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank regulation; cross-border transaction monitoring remains within Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and Department of Finance Canada policy remit

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Low-to-Medium. Cross-border Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) headquartered in the EU or UK are not chartered banks in Canada and cannot independently provide Canadian domestic clearing accounts or process CAD-denominated wires as core banking activity without entering into a sponsorship or partnership with a Schedule I Canadian bank. An EMI may be used as an auxiliary payment engine for foreign-currency receipts only if funds are settled through a Canadian bank partner and subject to OSFI guidance on third-party payment service providers. The Canadian Payment Clearing and Settlement Association (Payments Canada) does not grant direct clearing membership to non-bank EMIs. Legal constraints include the Bank Act (Canada), Payment Clearing and Settlement Act, and provincial consumer protection laws; non-bank EMIs must be registered as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC if operating in Canada. For a medical-device exporter, a federally regulated Schedule I bank account is superior and mandatory for letter of credit issuance, trade finance, and multi-currency treasury management.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections from a non-resident shareholder to the Ontario operating company must be routed through a Canadian Schedule I bank account using a standard SWIFT MT103 wire transfer. The originating bank must include a clear payment purpose code, typically a remark such as Outbound trade disbursements for purchase of components, contract manufacturing, freight, and settlement of commercial invoices must be executed by SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfer through the corporate bank's cross-border disbursement desk. For larger supply contracts and to maintain purchase-protection controls, a documentary Letter of Credit (LC) should be opened as a Commercial LC (Irrevocable, possibly confirmed) using SWIFT MT700. The payment rails for trade settlements include: (i) SWIFT MT103 for open-account payments; (ii) SWIFT MT700 for issuing an LC; (iii) SWIFT MT710/MT720 for amendment/transfer; and (iv) CAD-dollar automated clearing settlement system (ACSS) for domestic pre-settlement clearing of CAD leg before conversion of FX leg. Treasury must pre-clear all outbound payments with the bank's compliance desk, attaching the underlying invoice, export-import documentation, and shipping evidence for transactions above CAD 10,000. FINTRAC reporting thresholds require retention of originator and beneficiary name, account, and address; wire to or from Ontario must quote the full legal entity name exactly matching the incumbency certificate. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate cross-border non-arm's length transactions per fiscal year triggers the need for contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation satisfying ITA s.247(4) and CRA Information Circular 87-2R. Consolidated multinational group gross revenue exceeding C$1.07 billion requires Country-by-Country Reporting, Master File, and Local File documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a separate Gulf-state-style Economic Substance Regulation; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under ITA s.245 denies tax benefits arising from transactions or series lacking genuine business purpose and economic substance. Canadian source attribution rules also require an Ontario permanent establishment to have real operational authority over sales, inventory, and contract risk; shell arrangements without employees or decision-making are vulnerable to GAAR, treaty abuse principles, and CRA amplification.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Criminal Fraud

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.239, summary conviction fine of 50%-100% of evaded tax and/or 2 years imprisonment; indictment fine of 100%-200% of evaded tax and/or up to 5 years imprisonment. CRA may also impose a civil gross-negligence penalty of 50% of net understatement plus daily arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who participate in or authorize fraud are personally and jointly and severally liable; the corporate veil is pierced for fraudulent conduct, and criminal proceedings may include travel bans, asset freezes, and restitution orders against individuals.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA may reprice the transactions under s.247(2), assess a 10% transfer pricing penalty on the net adjustment when documentation obligations are not met or a gross valuation misstatement exists, and add prescribed daily interest on the additional corporate tax.

Director personal liability: Directors can be assessed for unremitted withholding taxes or GST/HST that arise from fabricated transfer pricing entries if they knew or ought to have known; deliberate participation in false documentation subjects them to criminal prosecution and personal civil liability.
Shell Company / Abuse of Treaty or GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR denial of tax benefit, reassessment at full 25% non-resident withholding rate, gross-negligence penalty of 50%, and interest; under CbCR rules, substantial misrepresentation may trigger competent-authority examination and increased audit exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly form or maintain a sham corporation may be liable in civil damages; Canadian courts may pierce the corporate veil for intentionally fraudulent or unconscionable acts, and statutory directors' liability notices may name them personally for corporate tax debts.
AML / Proceeds of Crime and FINTRAC Violations

Statutory sanction: ITA s.227.1 and ETA s.323 permit CRA to assess directors personally for 100% of unremitted source deductions and GST/HST; CRA may file liens, garnish receivables, require security, and compound daily interest until amounts are paid.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable if they knew or ought to have known of the failure to remit; the due-diligence defence is narrowly interpreted, and a CRA director's liability notice can be enforced personally without piercing the corporate veil.
False Incentive Claims / Regulatory Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: CRA disallows all claimed credits, revokes carryforward entitlements, assesses gross-negligence penalty of 50% of overstated amounts, and demands arrears interest; deliberate false statements can trigger Criminal Code fraud penalties and regulatory license suspension.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve T661 SR&ED claims without genuine substantiation face personal liability for restitution and could be prosecuted for fraud; if the corporation is deemed insolvent through non-payment, directors' statutory liability follows.
