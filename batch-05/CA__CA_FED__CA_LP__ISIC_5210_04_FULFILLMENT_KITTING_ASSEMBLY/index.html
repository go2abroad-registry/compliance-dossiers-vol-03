Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

Provincial limited partnership registry (ServiceOntario – Ontario Business Registry) for LP registration; Corporations Canada / Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) for the NUANS federal name search; Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for the federal Business Number.

Governing statute

Canada is a federal state; limited partnerships are constituted under provincial statutes, not under the Canada Business Corporations Act. Primary statute: Limited Partnerships Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. L.2, ss. 2, 3, 9 and 10 (formation, registration, and extra-provincial registration). Federal layer: Business Number and program accounts under the Income Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.), s. 248(1). The Canada Business Corporations Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-44 does not apply to partnerships.

Formation / constitutional filings

Certificate of Limited Partnership issued under the Limited Partnerships Act upon filing of the Form 1 Declaration; CRA Business Number confirmation letter.

Incorporation processing time

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed; capital contributions are fixed solely by the Limited Partnership Agreement, and no government escrow account is required by the registry or the CRA.

Funding & unlock path: No federal or provincial civil defense permit applies to warehousing and kitting; the facility must maintain a fire safety inspection certificate under the National Fire Code as adopted provincially, a building occupancy permit confirming structural load ratings for racking systems, and workplace compliance with the Canada Labour Code Part II if federally regulated or with provincial occupational health and safety legislation.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada); registration portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca/business-registration, also accessible through provincial/territorial one-stop business registries

E-government filing portal

A foreign limited partnership must first complete extra-provincial registration in the province where it carries on activity or has a place of business, then obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) online via BRO, by mail, fax, or through the provincial registry. The BN is the partnership-level statutory tax identifier. The partnership must also open program accounts as applicable, notably the GST/HST RT account for any registrable commercial activity, the payroll RP account if employees are engaged in Canada, and may need an import/export RM account for customs. Non-resident partners are not separately registered for the BN solely because of partnership activity; the partnership files an information return (T5013) and allocates income to partners.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a US-style check-the-box or elective entity classification regime. A Canadian limited partnership is mandatorily fiscally transparent for federal income tax: the entity itself is not subject to Part I income tax, and partnership income is computed and allocated to limited partners. Limited elections may be made, including a partnership fiscal-period election under subsection 249.1(1) in restricted circumstances, and anti-hybrid rules under sections 18.4 and 18.5 of the Income Tax Act can neutralize certain hybrid mismatch outcomes without an elective opt-in. No statutory election is available to reclassify the LP as an entity-level taxpayer for Canadian federal purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000: a person making taxable supplies in Canada is a small supplier and is not required to register until its total worldwide taxable supplies (excluding financial services and certain supplies) exceed C$30,000 over any four consecutive calendar quarters; registration becomes mandatory once the threshold is exceeded, subject to special non-resident and related-party rules

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act. For goods, a supply is zero-rated where the supplier exports tangible personal property and delivers it to a destination outside Canada by common carrier, courier, mail, or as accompanied baggage, or exports it in accordance with regulatory evidence requirements. For services relevant to fulfillment kitting and assembly, zero-rating may apply where the service is rendered to a non-resident person and is not performed in respect of real or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is supplied; certain services performed on goods intended for export may also qualify when the goods are subsequently exported and direct evidence of export is maintained. The supplier must prove that the supply is zero-rated by retaining documentary evidence of the foreign destination and the export transaction, otherwise the supply is subject to 5% GST.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form G13) or CADEX electronic export report
  • Commercial invoice showing the foreign buyer, full description, quantities, values, and HS tariff classification
  • Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, or carrier tracking manifest evidencing delivery to a destination outside Canada
  • Inward remittance certificate, foreign bank transfer advice, or settlement records evidencing cross-border payment
  • Export contract, purchase order, and any certificate of non-residency for the overseas recipient

No federal real-time fiscal clearance, electronic invoicing clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory government API validation mandate currently applies under the Excise Tax Act for GST/HST. CRA accepts electronic invoices and records provided they are complete, legible, and retainable, but does not require a fiscal authorization code or vendor-certified E-invoicing platform. Registrants must file GST/HST returns electronically (NETFILE/EFILE) above prescribed annual revenue thresholds and retain source records, but issuance of invoices in paper or PDF remains legally sufficient.

The CRA audits export zero-rating claims through issuer-origin matching, foreign exchange monitoring, and documentary evidence inspection. If the required export evidence is absent or insufficient, the supply is re-characterized as taxable at 5% GST/HST, and the taxpayer is reassessed for unremitted tax, arrears interest, and penalties. A false statement or omission in a return or exemption claim may incur a penalty equal to the greater of C$100 and 25% of the tax sought to be evaded under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act; fraudulent claims can trigger gross-negligence penalties, denial of input tax credits, loss of registration privileges, and referral to the CRA Criminal Investigations Program for prosecution.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a free-floating currency regime with full convertibility on both current account and capital account. There are no statutory exchange-control restrictions, no foreign-investment approval obligations for ordinary LP capital contributions, and no prohibitions on profit repatriation. Non-resident partners of a Canadian limited partnership may remit CAD or foreign currency in and out freely, subject only to standard anti-money-laundering reporting thresholds. Distributions to non-residents remain subject to Canadian Part XIII non-resident withholding tax where applicable, but no tax- or FX-related repatriation permit is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no mandatory surrender, forced conversion, or prescribed holding period on export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained in any currency, held offshore, or converted at market rates. However, any international electronic funds transfer of C$10,000 or more must be reported by the financial intermediary to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and evidence of underlying export transactions should be preserved for compliance inquiries.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential regulation overseen by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and financial-intelligence reporting administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium-High conditional feasibility. Offshore digital EMI platforms such as Airwallex can serve the Canadian LP only if the provider is properly registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC or operates through a partnership with a Schedule I Canadian bank. A wholly offshore, unlicensed EMI lacking Canadian MSB registration and a local settlement agent cannot hold client funds or conduct Canadian payment services. Cross-border viability is strongest when the EMI (i) holds a Canadian MSB registration, (ii) uses SWIFT or LVTS via a Canadian bank sponsor, and (iii) maintains direct USD and CAD accounts to avoid forced conversion.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal Part I statutory rate 38.0%; after the 10% provincial abatement and the 13% general rate reduction, the net federal rate on ordinary active business income is 15.0% for non-CCPC corporations. A limited partnership is fiscally transparent: Part I tax is assessed on the partners, not on the LP itself.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident. Because a limited partnership does not pay dividends, allocations and distributions to partners are not subject to Part XIII dividend withholding; the partner is instead taxed under Part I on the allocated Canadian-source business income. Remittances from a Canadian corporate operating partner of after-tax profits are subject to the 25% statutory dividend withholding rate unless a tax treaty reduces it.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

No later than five months after the partnership's fiscal year-end; for a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is May 31. A Canadian-resident corporate partner must report its allocated share on its own T2 Corporation Income Tax Return due six months after that corporation's taxation year-end.

Late and incomplete T5013 information returns: section 162 penalty of C$25 per day, minimum C$100 and maximum C$2,500. Gross negligence penalties under section 163(2) and criminal prosecution under section 239 can apply to deliberately false T5013 or T2 filings.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1 million in gross fair market value of reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions in a fiscal year is the general threshold for filing Form T106 and maintaining contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation. Canadian ultimate parent entities within multinational groups having consolidated group revenue of C$750 million or more must file a Country-by-Country Report and should maintain Master File and Local File data accessible to the CRA upon audit request.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations of the Gulf Cooperation Council type. However, economic substance is decisive under the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245, the common-law sham doctrine, the permanent establishment attribution analysis, and the OECD Multilateral Instrument principal-purpose test. The LP must maintain real staff, physical premises, warehouse operations, functional decision-making, and identifiable business records in Canada to avoid being characterized as a conduit or shell arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239: on summary conviction, a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for up to 2 years less a day; on indictment, imprisonment for up to 5 years. The gross negligence penalty under section 163(2) may also apply at 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the corporate general partner who signed or authorized false returns are not shielded by the corporate veil. They can be prosecuted as parties to the offence, ordered to pay restitution, frozen out of Canada through restraint orders, and subjected to criminal travel bans while charges are pending.
Gross Negligence, Non-Filing, and Incomplete Reporting

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty under section 163: the greater of C$100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credits. Late filing penalties under section 162 accumulate daily; CRA may also revoke tax benefits and disallow expenses without an adequate due-diligence defence.

Director personal liability: Under section 227.1, directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for the partnership's unremitted payroll source deductions and GST/HST amounts. Directors who knowingly permit non-filing can be assessed personally and have their personal assets seized.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing reassessment to the arm's-length price; section 247(3) penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of C$5 million and 10% of gross revenue. Understatement interest accrues from the original filing date. GAAR under section 245 may apply to a circular arrangement lacking economic substance.

Director personal liability: Directors who certify incomplete transfer pricing records or implement sham cost-plus arrangements can be personally liable as transferees under section 160 if property or benefits are transferred to them. Willful participation in a false TP document may invoke criminal liability under section 239.
Shell Company / Sham and GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR under section 245 denies the tax benefit and imposes interest and penalties; sham transactions are void against the CRA. Treaty benefits may be denied under the OECD MLI principal-purpose test. Gross negligence penalties under section 163(2) and Part XIII withholding tax can apply to disguised distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner who manage a sham LP can be assessed personally for unpaid taxes under section 160 as transferees or benefit recipients. Courts may pierce the veil and impose liability on individuals who control the operations and personally benefit from the arrangement.
Money Laundering and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code section 462.31: laundering proceeds of crime is an indictable offence punishable by up to 10 years' imprisonment with asset forfeiture of tainted property. Non-compliance with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act attracts administrative monetary penalties imposed by FINTRAC and possible suspension from regulated financial rails.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners who knowingly participate in laundering transactions face personal criminal liability as principals or accomplices. Compliance officers and senior managers can be fined and imprisoned, and the court may order travel bans, seizure of personal assets, and lifetime disqualification from financial-sector roles.
