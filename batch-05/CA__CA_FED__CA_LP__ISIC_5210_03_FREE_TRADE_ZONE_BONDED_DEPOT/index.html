Competent tax authority

The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), principally through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) and the Customs Commercial System, administers customs, bonded-warehouse, export-reporting, and tariff-relief matters. Provincial or territorial authorities administer partnership registrations and applicable provincial sales taxes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A Canadian limited partnership has no general United States-style check-the-box election. Its Canadian classification follows Canadian partnership-law and federal income-tax principles, with income and losses generally computed at the partnership level and allocated to partners under the partnership agreement and applicable statutory rules. A partnership may be required to file a T5013 information return and issue partner information. Canadian-resident partners are generally taxed on their allocated shares; non-resident partners may be subject to Canadian tax on Canadian-source income and Part XIII withholding where applicable. A partnership can make specific elections under the Income Tax Act, including elections relevant to capital cost allowance, eligible capital or resource property, foreign-affiliate matters, and other transactions where statutory conditions are met; these are not entity-classification elections. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules may deny deductions or impose inclusion or withholding consequences for specified hybrid arrangements. Foreign legal classification does not automatically control Canadian classification, and a foreign tax-transparent vehicle should obtain a Canadian classification analysis before relying on treaty or flow-through treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies, measured under the applicable small-supplier tests over the relevant quarterly and annual periods. The threshold generally applies to a single supplier and associated persons as prescribed. Registration can be voluntary below the threshold, and mandatory registration can arise for non-residents carrying on commercial activities in Canada, making taxable supplies in Canada, or otherwise meeting the GST/HST registration rules. The CAD 30,000 threshold does not eliminate customs, import GST, provincial tax, or special non-resident obligations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada can generally be zero-rated where the statutory place-of-supply and export conditions are satisfied, including exportation by the supplier or by the recipient under the applicable GST/HST rules. Goods held in a customs bonded warehouse or other customs-controlled facility may receive GST/HST relief while under the applicable customs regime, but the precise treatment depends on importation, ownership, release, delivery, and subsequent export facts. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated where the service qualifies as an exported service under the Excise Tax Act; exclusions apply, including certain services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, litigation, and services performed in Canada in circumstances specified by law. Zero-rating requires contemporaneous evidence of the recipient's non-resident status, contractual scope, delivery or performance location, export or customs status, and payment and accounting records. Input tax credits or GST/HST refunds require valid supporting invoices, tax paid or payable evidence, commercial-use nexus, and retention of books and records for the statutory period. Export status does not automatically zero-rate domestic warehousing, handling, transportation, brokerage, or other services.

  • Canadian Export Declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where an export declaration is legally required
  • Commercial invoice or equivalent accounting document identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, value, currency, terms, and destination
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, cargo control document, or other carrier evidence of shipment
  • Proof of export and destination, such as carrier delivery records, customs exit evidence, shipping confirmation, or foreign import documentation
  • Purchase order, contract, warehouse release record, inventory ledger, and proof of delivery linking the goods to the zero-rated supply
  • CBSA customs accounting, bonded-warehouse, duty-deferral, and inventory records where goods enter or leave a customs-controlled facility
  • GST/HST invoices and input-tax-credit support meeting the prescribed documentary requirements
  • Inward remittance or foreign-payment evidence where commercially available; Canada generally does not impose a universal inward-remittance certificate requirement for GST/HST export zero-rating

Canada has no general nationwide real-time GST/HST e-invoice clearance or continuous transaction-control mandate for ordinary domestic B2B transactions. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices or other supporting documentation containing prescribed information, retain reliable electronic records, and substantiate tax charged, collected, input tax credits, zero-rating, and adjustments. Electronic invoices are generally acceptable if they preserve authenticity, integrity, readability, and audit accessibility. Export declarations are filed electronically through CERS where required, and customs-controlled warehouse operators must maintain prescribed inventory, movement, accounting, security, and reporting records. CRA portal registration and electronic filing requirements may apply to specific returns or registrant categories. Invoices for goods imported into Canada do not replace customs declarations, accounting documents, tariff classifications, or import records.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange-control rule requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within 180 days. The stated 180-day period is therefore not a general Canadian statutory deadline and must not be treated as a universal Canadian requirement. CRA may reassess GST/HST where zero-rating, input tax credits, refunds, or export evidence is insufficient, including tax that should have been collected, denied input tax credits, interest, and applicable penalties. Gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit or refund, subject to statutory limits; other late-filing, failure-to-file, failure-to-remit, and false-statement penalties may apply. Criminal prosecution may arise for fraud, tax evasion, false records, or obstruction. CBSA may reassess duties and taxes, impose Administrative Monetary Penalty System sanctions, suspend or revoke customs privileges or warehouse authorizations, seize goods, and pursue penalties or prosecution for false declarations, diversion, or non-compliance. Recovery may extend to prior reporting periods within the applicable reassessment and limitation periods.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada generally permits non-resident ownership, capital contributions, dividend and partnership-profit remittances, loan repayments, interest payments, royalty payments, and repatriation of capital, subject to contractual banking controls, sanctions, anti-money-laundering requirements, tax withholding, transfer-pricing rules, partnership tax allocation, beneficial-ownership verification, and source-of-funds review. Canada does not generally require prior central-bank approval for inbound or outbound foreign-currency payments. A Canadian limited partnership should maintain a documented capital-account ledger and partnership agreement because distributions may be subject to Canadian withholding tax, Part XIII withholding where applicable, non-resident tax reporting, treaty relief documentation, and tax filings relating to Canadian-source income. Payments involving sanctioned persons, restricted jurisdictions, trade-controlled goods, or unexplained third-party funding may be delayed, rejected, frozen, or reported. Foreign investment review under the Investment Canada Act may apply depending on ownership, control, enterprise value, national-security considerations, and the nature of the warehouse and logistics operations. A bonded warehouse operator must separately satisfy CBSA authorization, customs-security, inventory-control, and accounting requirements; those customs requirements do not constitute general currency controls.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general deadline requiring a Canadian exporter or warehouse operator to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars or remit them through a domestic bank. Export receipts may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the holder's discretion, subject to bank controls, sanctions screening, accounting records, tax reporting, customs documentation, and contractual settlement terms. Export and import reporting, commercial documentation, valuation, tariff classification, origin, and customs-accounting obligations are administered by CBSA and, where applicable, the Canada Border Services Agency's CARM system. Payment reporting may arise under Canada's statistical reporting framework, tax legislation, anti-money-laundering legislation, or the Bank of Canada's reporting programs, but these requirements are not currency-surrender rules. Large-value cash or monetary-instrument movements involving Canada are subject to FINTRAC reporting requirements, including cross-border currency or monetary-instrument reporting where the applicable CAD 10,000 threshold is met. Banks may request invoices, bills of lading, customs records, contracts, and proof of beneficial ownership before releasing or converting export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing exchange controls or mandatory currency surrender. Relevant regulatory authorities also include the Department of Finance Canada, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and applicable provincial securities, corporate, partnership, and financial-services regulators.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment initiation, foreign-exchange execution, collections, and treasury technology; Low as a replacement for a principal Canadian commercial-bank relationship. Canada does not have a single statutory electronic-money institution passport equivalent to the European Union framework. A foreign fintech or EMI must establish the applicable Canadian legal basis, which may include registration as a foreign money services business with FINTRAC, provincial money-services-business registration, partnership with a Canadian financial institution, or authorization under the Retail Payment Activities Act administered by the Bank of Canada. Activities involving deposit-taking, payment accounts, settlement, custody of client funds, lending, securities, derivatives, or trust services may require additional federal or provincial authorization. A non-bank provider cannot represent customer balances as insured Canadian deposits unless legally eligible for Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation coverage. Offshore EMI balances may be operationally useful for non-resident collections or FX, but Canadian banks may require a domestic account, transparent safeguarding arrangements, direct contractual evidence, payment-flow diagrams, sanctions controls, and confirmation that the provider is not conducting unlicensed deposit-taking or money transmission in Canada.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The preferred route is a wire from an account held in the legal name of the contributing partner or its disclosed parent into the LP's Canadian commercial-bank account, with the payment reference stating the LP name, capital contribution or partner loan designation, and executed agreement reference. The LP should pre-clear the transaction with its relationship manager where the amount is material or the remitter is offshore. The bank file should include the partnership agreement, capital-call notice or loan agreement, partner resolution, ownership chart, source-of-funds evidence, ultimate-beneficial-owner evidence, expected-use schedule, and any applicable tax or foreign-investment analysis. Canada does not use a universal statutory FX purpose-code system equivalent to some controlled-currency jurisdictions; any bank-selected payment-purpose, regulatory, or statistical code must accurately describe the transaction. The recipient should avoid third-party funding, unexplained split payments, personal remitters, and circular transfers. For large-value payments, use SWIFT payment messaging through a regulated bank and obtain the incoming funds confirmation, payment reference, and reconciliation entry. Capital contributions, partner loans, and service payments must be recorded separately because their legal, tax, withholding, accounting, and repayment consequences differ. Funds should not be released to operations until the bank's sanctions, AML, and source-of-funds review is complete.Use Canadian domestic clearing for CAD payments to Canadian suppliers and regulated-bank SWIFT transfers for foreign suppliers, customs brokers, freight providers, insurers, and overseas customers. High-value domestic payments may use Lynx, Canada's high-value payment system, subject to bank eligibility and transaction limits; routine CAD payments may use EFT, electronic funds transfer, or other bank-supported domestic rails. Foreign-currency wires are generally sent through SWIFT using an MT103-equivalent customer credit transfer message or its ISO 20022 successor, with complete originator, beneficiary, bank, invoice, goods, and purpose information. MT103 is a payment message and not an independent settlement system. For higher-risk or documentary trade, use a commercial letter of credit, preferably subject to the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600), with an issuing and advising or confirming bank acceptable to the parties. Documentary requirements should cover commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, certificate of origin, customs documents, insurance document, inspection certificate where required, and sanctions or end-use certifications. Documentary collections may use URC 522 where commercially appropriate. All payments should be matched to approved purchase orders, contracts, invoices, customs records, delivery evidence, and vendor master data. Customs duties and taxes should be paid through authorized CBSA and broker channels, including CARM processes where applicable, rather than through informal third-party settlement. Outbound distributions, partner payments, interest, royalties, and management fees require separate tax, withholding, transfer-pricing, partnership-account, and sanctions review.Deliverable foreign-exchange forwards entered into with a regulated Canadian bank or appropriately authorized dealer to hedge documented CAD, USD, EUR, or other currency receivables and payables. The LP should maintain a hedge designation or treasury memo, exposure forecast, counterparty limit, confirmation, settlement instructions, and accounting treatment.Non-deliverable forwards may be used for currencies with limited convertibility or delivery restrictions, subject to dealer availability, applicable provincial derivatives rules, sanctions screening, counterparty documentation, and evidence that the notional amount corresponds to a genuine commercial exposure. NDFs do not create a right to receive the restricted currency and settle by reference to the contractually specified fixing rate.Foreign-exchange swaps and cross-currency swaps may be used for short-term liquidity management or hedging of identified currency cash flows, subject to ISDA documentation, collateral and margin terms, counterparty credit limits, applicable Canadian derivatives regulation, and board-approved treasury authority.Currency options and option structures may be used to protect budget rates while retaining upside, provided premiums, maximum loss, settlement terms, suitability, accounting treatment, and counterparty authorization are documented. Avoid leveraged, naked, or speculative structures outside the LP's approved risk mandate.The LP should use written treasury limits covering permitted currencies, hedge ratios, tenor, counterparty credit quality, collateral, valuation, early termination, netting, and prohibited speculative positions. Canadian derivatives obligations may vary by entity status, notional exposure, counterparty, product, and province; legal and regulatory classification should be confirmed before execution.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for payment initiation, foreign-exchange execution, collections, and treasury technology; Low as a replacement for a principal Canadian commercial-bank relationship. Canada does not have a single statutory electronic-money institution passport equivalent to the European Union framework. A foreign fintech or EMI must establish the applicable Canadian legal basis, which may include registration as a foreign money services business with FINTRAC, provincial money-services-business registration, partnership with a Canadian financial institution, or authorization under the Retail Payment Activities Act administered by the Bank of Canada. Activities involving deposit-taking, payment accounts, settlement, custody of client funds, lending, securities, derivatives, or trust services may require additional federal or provincial authorization. A non-bank provider cannot represent customer balances as insured Canadian deposits unless legally eligible for Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation coverage. Offshore EMI balances may be operationally useful for non-resident collections or FX, but Canadian banks may require a domestic account, transparent safeguarding arrangements, direct contractual evidence, payment-flow diagrams, sanctions controls, and confirmation that the provider is not conducting unlicensed deposit-taking or money transmission in Canada.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate (applicable to corporations; limited partnership is a flow-through entity, partners taxed at their own rates)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

0% for partnership distributions (not dividends); however, non-resident partners may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax on certain income (e.g., management fees, interest, royalties) at 25% unless reduced by treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for services transactions; CAD 5 million for goods transactions with non-residents (per CRA guidance).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a specific Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) like the UAE, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles apply. A limited partnership must demonstrate real business activity and economic substance to avoid recharacterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal penalties under the Income Tax Act: fines up to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may be personally liable for unpaid taxes and penalties if they knowingly participate in evasion; travel bans and asset seizure possible.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, reallocate income, and impose penalties under GAAR (up to 50% of tax benefit).

Director personal liability: Directors may be held jointly and severally liable for corporate tax debts if they fail to remit withholdings or knowingly facilitate a shell arrangement.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: fines up to CAD 2 million for corporations, criminal charges for individuals, and suspension of licenses.

Director personal liability: Directors can face personal fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for willful non-compliance; travel bans may be imposed.
