Competent corporate registry

Provincial limited partnership registrar (for Ontario: ServiceOntario – Companies Section, Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery); federal tax and Business Number registration: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) Business Registration Online portal.

Governing statute

Canada has no federal limited partnership statute; the Limited Partnership is constituted under the applicable provincial Limited Partnerships Act (reference: Ontario Limited Partnerships Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. L.8, ss. 2, 3 and 9), with the general partner's duties supplemented by the Partnerships Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. P.5. Federal tax registration is effected under the Excise Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. E-15, s. 240 (GST/HST) and the Income Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985 (5th Supp.), c. 1, ss. 96 and 102.

Formation / constitutional filings

Form 1 – Declaration of Limited Partnership (Ontario), accompanied by the Limited Partnership Agreement and NUANS Name Search Report; federal BN and GST/HST registration via CRA Business Registration Online.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal tax authority responsible for federal income tax, Goods and Services Tax/Harmonized Sales Tax (GST/HST), and Business Number (BN) registration. Official portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html) and GST/HST NETFILE.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident limited partnership carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada (including specified digital services) must apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account (RT). Registration may be completed online through CRA's Business Registration service or by paper Form RC1. The limited partnership is registered as a separate 'person' for GST/HST purposes under the Excise Tax Act, despite being fiscally transparent for income tax purposes. The partnership must also file Form T5013 (Partnership Information Return) where it carries on a business in Canada. Foreign entities should first confirm whether they have Canadian permanent establishment or Canadian nexus; if the partnership is registered, program accounts RC (payroll), RP (income tax withholding), and RD (import/export) may be added as needed.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — A Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent for income tax purposes: income, loss, and capital are allocated to partners under Income Tax Act section 96. For GST/HST purposes, the partnership is itself a registrable 'person' distinct from its partners. Non-resident partners deriving income from a Canadian-source business through the partnership may be subject to Canadian Part I tax and Part XIII withholding on certain distributions, unless reduced by applicable income tax treaty.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists under Canadian law. Partnerships allocate Canadian-source income under the partnership agreement consistent with ITA section 96; the CRA generally respects bona fide allocations. Available statutory elections include: election among closely related registrants under ETA section 273 to treat qualifying supplies as made for nil consideration; partnership depreciation and cost allowance elections under Regulations 521 and 522; and hybrid-mismatch related adjustments under ITA sections 18.4 and 18.5 for foreign tax credits and deductibility where the partnership is a reverse hybrid in another jurisdiction. No general entity-classification election is available to convert the limited partnership into an entity-level taxpayer.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (C$30,000) — the small supplier threshold based on total taxable supplies made in Canada during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and the current quarter. Effective July 1, 2021, non-resident suppliers of specified digital services (SDS), including API access, data services, and cloud-based platform services, are required to register under the simplified GST/HST framework when annual taxable SDS revenues from Canadian recipients exceed CAD 30,000. Registration may also be mandatory without regard to the threshold where the partnership otherwise 'carries on business' in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of commercial web-scraping, proxy API gateways, and related digital data services are zero-rated under the Excise Tax Act (Schedule VI Part VII) when supplied to a non-resident recipient, provided the supply is not for consumption, use, or enjoyment in Canada and does not relate to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. Where the supply is characterized as intangible personal property (API licenses, data access rights), zero-rating may also be claimed under Schedule VI Part V, including technology and know-how provisions. The supplier must obtain documentary evidence of the recipient's non-resident status, the contractual terms, the place of use of the services, and the foreign settlement of payment. Physical goods exported by the partnership are zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V where the goods are exported as soon as reasonably possible following delivery, with proof of export retained.

  • Customs export declaration (B13A or approved CBSA/GCA electronic export declaration) for physical goods
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, or other transportation document proving delivery outside Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/financial institution FX settlement statements evidencing payment from the non-resident recipient
  • Commercial invoice or service agreement identifying the non-resident recipient, description of the API/data supply, price, and contractual place of consumption
  • Evidence of non-resident status of the recipient (foreign business registry extract, tax residency certificate, or equivalent)
  • Technical records demonstrating delivery and use outside Canada (server response logs, IP routing records, license key issuance, user access logs)
  • GST/HST zero-rated invoice and supporting general ledger entries

Canada does not operate a national real-time e-invoicing clearance, fiscal stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) regime. CRA does not require a specific invoice format; however, registrants must maintain books and records in an accessible electronic or paper format and must retain sufficient transactional detail to support zero-rated export treatment. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or an approved EDI gateway once registrant status applies. Canada has voluntarily adopted the PEPPOL standard for business-to-government e-invoicing in certain procurement processes, but there is no mandatory federal digital clearance mandate applicable to the commercial web-scraping API sector.

CRA may reassess zero-rated export claims within the normal four-year limitation period, extended to seven years where a partner or the partnership has made a misrepresentation or omission attributable to negligence, carelessness, or willful default. If the partnership cannot substantiate that the recipient was non-resident or that the supply was not used in Canada, the supply will be re-rated as taxable at 5%–15% GST/HST depending on the province where the recipient is located, with tax, compound daily interest, and penalties assessed retroactively. Culpable conduct penalties of 25% of the deficient net tax may apply under Excise Tax Act subsection 285(1), and false statements or omissions in filings expose the partnership and its partners to the 50% gross-negligence penalty under Income Tax Act subsection 163(2). Knowingly making a false or deceptive export declaration may also lead to criminal prosecution for tax evasion, fines, seizure of assets, and imprisonment. CRA strictly scrutinizes export refund claims involving digital services and may require production of underlying contract, payment, and usage records before releasing any refund or input tax credit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full de facto and de jure convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions. The Canadian dollar is freely tradable and there are no surrender or conversion requirements, no exchange-rate approvals, and no restrictions on inbound capital subscriptions, service receipts, loan repayments, or outbound distributions. Legal hurdles are limited to tax and sanctions: (i) business income of a Canadian limited partnership allocated to non-resident partners is subject to Canadian Part I tax on Canadian-source income, requiring T5013 filings and a Canadian income-tax return by the non-resident partner; (ii) Part XIII withholding tax applies to certain amounts paid by the limited partnership to non-residents (interest, rents, royalties, and management fees), but not to ordinary business-income allocations; (iii) USDC receipts are treated as payment in the form of a commodity and must be measured at fair market value for income tax and GST/HST purposes; (iv) OFAC, UN, EU, and Canadian autonomous sanctions are screened at the bank-wire level. No central bank authorization is required for partnership distributions, profit remittances, or capital repatriation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts or cross-border service-proceeds. Foreign-currency proceeds may be retained in any jurisdiction and in any currency. The controlling obligations are reporting and tax rules: (a) foreign-currency receipts must be translated into Canadian dollars for income computation and GST/HST returns, generally using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate or the CRA prescribed convention; (b) if the limited partnership is a specified taxpayer owning specified foreign property (including foreign bank deposits or directly held USDC) with a cost amount exceeding CAD 100,000 at any time during the year, Form T1135 must be filed; (c) monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more transported across the Canadian border must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency under the cross-border reporting regime of the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; (d) a functional-currency election may be available under section 261 of the Income Tax Act for a qualifying entity operating predominantly in a designated currency, but partners must still compute their Canadian tax in Canadian dollars; professional tax advice is required before election.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority and, since the phased commencement of the Retail Payment Activities Act, the supervisory authority for registered retail payment service providers; there is no dedicated exchange-control department because Canada abolished general exchange controls in 1951. Foreign-investment approval and sanctions screening are respectively administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) under the Investment Canada Act and by Global Affairs Canada under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act, while anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing supervision is carried out by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary collection and FX engine; Low-Medium as the sole primary banking relationship. Canadian law does not recognize the European electronic-money-institution (EMI) licence as a distinct charter; foreign digital banks and EMIs (for example, Mercury, Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex) can support USD/CAD collections, disbursements, and conversion, but they are not clearing members of Payments Canada and cannot settle directly through Lynx or the Real-Time Rail. An offshore EMI that is not registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act and is not a FINTRAC-registered money services business exposes the limited partnership to regulatory and operational risk. The recommended architecture is a tiered structure: a Schedule I domestic bank as the primary liquidity, tax-clearing, and relationship bank; Stripe as the online-PSP gateway for card and API subscription collections; and a FINTRAC-registered virtual-currency payment processor (or Canadian MSB exchange) for USDC merchant settlement, with immediate liquidation into CAD unless the treasury policy deliberately retains USDC as a hedge.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary collection and FX engine; Low-Medium as the sole primary banking relationship. Canadian law does not recognize the European electronic-money-institution (EMI) licence as a distinct charter; foreign digital banks and EMIs (for example, Mercury, Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex) can support USD/CAD collections, disbursements, and conversion, but they are not clearing members of Payments Canada and cannot settle directly through Lynx or the Real-Time Rail. An offshore EMI that is not registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act and is not a FINTRAC-registered money services business exposes the limited partnership to regulatory and operational risk. The recommended architecture is a tiered structure: a Schedule I domestic bank as the primary liquidity, tax-clearing, and relationship bank; Stripe as the online-PSP gateway for card and API subscription collections; and a FINTRAC-registered virtual-currency payment processor (or Canadian MSB exchange) for USDC merchant settlement, with immediate liquidation into CAD unless the treasury policy deliberately retains USDC as a hedge.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to federal corporate income tax. Taxable income is allocated to the partners. The general federal corporate income tax rate is 15% after the general federal abatement; the federal small business rate is 9% for an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), subject to the small business limit. Provincial and territorial income taxes apply separately. A non-resident corporate partner is generally taxable in Canada on its allocated Canadian-source income and may be subject to branch or permanent-establishment rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The general Canadian Part XIII withholding rate is 25% on taxable dividends paid or credited to non-residents. Treaty rates commonly reduce the rate to 5% where the recipient is a qualifying corporate beneficial owner meeting ownership thresholds, and 15% in other qualifying cases, but the exact rate depends on the applicable treaty. An LP does not ordinarily pay dividends; distributions are generally partnership distributions, while the underlying income retains its tax character.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 return is generally due within five months after the partnership's fiscal period-end. T5013 slips and partner information must generally be provided by the same deadline. A partnership with a fiscal period ending December 31 normally files by May 31 of the following year. Corporate partners generally file Form T2 within six months after the corporation's taxation year-end, with any balance generally due two months after year-end, or three months for qualifying CCPCs.

Deadline: · Frequency:

A corporation files within six months after its taxation year-end. Corporate tax balances are generally due two months after year-end, with a three-month balance-due period available to qualifying CCPCs meeting the statutory conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian transfer-pricing documentation is required under Income Tax Act section 247(4) when a taxpayer or partnership is engaged in non-arm's-length transactions with a non-resident and the statutory conditions apply. The commonly applied documentation threshold is based on gross revenue exceeding CAD 10 million, subject to the statutory exceptions and regulations; the T106 reporting threshold is generally more than CAD 1 million of reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report may be required for members of an applicable multinational enterprise group, with the CbC threshold generally based on consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Documentation should nevertheless be prepared below thresholds where material risk exists.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone United Kingdom-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian authorities examine legal and economic substance through the arm's-length principle, transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership requirements, interest limitation rules, thin-capitalization rules, principal-purpose analysis, and judicial substance-over-form principles. A data-scraping or proxy-API LP should maintain genuine Canadian business functions where claimed, including personnel or contractors, decision-making, operational controls, data governance, cybersecurity, customer contracts, service capacity, and commercially supported risk allocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Partnership Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, unreported-income penalties, and prosecution under the Income Tax Act. Tax evasion on conviction can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to five years; indictable fraud-related conduct may attract more severe Criminal Code consequences.

Director personal liability: General partners and persons who direct or authorize the conduct may face criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, and personal assessment for certain unremitted source deductions or GST/HST. Directors of a corporate general partner may face derivative statutory liability after the corporation's assets and limitation periods are considered. CRA may seek a departure prohibition order in qualifying serious tax-debt circumstances; there is no automatic general travel ban for ordinary tax debt.
Improper Use of Limited Partnership or Sham Entity

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization under partnership, income-tax, GAAR, beneficial-ownership, or agency principles; denial of deductions or treaty benefits; reassessment of partners; interest; civil penalties; and possible dissolution or court remedies under applicable federal or provincial partnership law.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is not automatically pierced, but individuals may be personally liable for fraud, knowing participation, torts, statutory trust amounts, or acting as an undisclosed general partner. A general partner normally has unlimited liability for partnership obligations.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Primary transfer-pricing adjustment, secondary adjustment or withholding consequences where applicable, contemporaneous-documentation penalties, T106 penalties, interest, GAAR application, and denial of deductions. Repeated or deliberate failures may support gross-negligence or tax-evasion charges.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face personal exposure for knowingly approving false records, fraudulent tax planning, source-deduction failures, or statutory trust breaches. Ordinary arm's-length pricing disputes do not by themselves create personal criminal liability.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: Assessment for the unremitted tax, arrears interest, a 10% failure-to-remit penalty for a first failure and 20% for repeated failures, late-filing penalties, and possible director liability for amounts held in trust.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate payer can be assessed personally under the director-liability provisions after statutory conditions are met. General partners may be directly exposed. Wilful failure or falsification may also result in criminal prosecution.
GST/HST Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of uncollected tax, interest, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, denial of input tax credits, gross-negligence penalties, and prosecution for fraud or false statements under the Excise Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted GST/HST held in trust where statutory conditions apply. Individuals may also be personally liable for fraud, intentional misrepresentation, or tortious conduct.
Anti-Money Laundering, Sanctions, and Cryptocurrency Controls

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties can reach statutory maximums based on the violation category and compliance history; serious criminal laundering offences under the Criminal Code can result in substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to ten years for certain laundering offences. Sanctions violations may attract administrative and criminal penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and compliance personnel may be personally prosecuted for knowing participation, authorization, counselling, or wilful blindness. Corporate liability does not protect individuals from criminal, proceeds-of-crime, sanctions, or confiscation consequences.
Unauthorized Computer Access, Privacy, and Data Misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential Criminal Code unauthorized-use offences, civil claims for breach of contract, trespass to chattels, conversion, misappropriation, or injunctions; federal or provincial privacy investigations and orders; statutory damages where applicable; and contractual termination or platform blocking.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct unauthorized access or knowingly misuse personal information may face criminal prosecution and personal civil liability. Corporate status does not immunize the actual actor from Criminal Code or privacy-law exposure.
Beneficial Ownership, Corporate Transparency, and Recordkeeping

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, refusal or suspension of financial services, corporate-registry enforcement, tax reassessment, AML escalation, and criminal sanctions for knowingly false or misleading information where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and authorized representatives may be personally liable for knowing false statements, obstruction, or participation in concealment. Beneficial owners may be directly investigated and prosecuted.
Payment-Processor and Banking Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Account termination, reserve withholding, civil recovery, contractual damages, suspicious-transaction reporting, AML investigation, tax reassessment, and potential fraud or proceeds-of-crime prosecution.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be personally liable for fraudulent representations, unauthorized transactions, conversion, or knowing participation. Directors and general partners may face personal exposure for restitution and criminal sanctions.
