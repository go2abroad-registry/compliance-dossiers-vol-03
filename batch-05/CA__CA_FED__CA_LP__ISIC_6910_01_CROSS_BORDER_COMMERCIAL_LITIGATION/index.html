Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA/ARC); digital portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html) and GST/HST NETFILE

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity (including a non-resident limited partnership) that begins carrying on business in Canada or earns Canadian-source income must obtain a Canadian Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The BN is the statutory tax identifier, and a separate GST/HST program account is required if the partnership is not a 'small supplier' under the Excise Tax Act. Registration can be completed online through CRA My Business Account, by postal application using Form RC1, or by FAPI through the CRA's 'Register for a business number' onboarding process. Non-resident partners without permanent establishment must still satisfy partnership information return obligations (Form T5013 and related schedules). The partnership's BN application includes a legal entity profile; foreign partnerships must provide full details of each non-resident partner, the partnership's Canadian address for service, and the partnership agreement, and may be required to appoint a Canadian resident representative for GST/HST if the partnership has no place of business in Canada and makes taxable supplies here.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under subsection 102(2) of the Income Tax Act (Canada), a partnership in which all of the partners are non-resident persons may elect to be deemed to be a corporation for Canadian income tax purposes; this is the primary Canadian statutory entity-classification election analogous to a 'check-the-box' in other jurisdictions. A limited partnership that is a 'Canadian partnership' as defined under subsection 102(1) may also elect on Form T5023 to treat itself as a separate taxpayer for certain GST/HST and income tax attribution purposes, subject to CRA administrative position. No general federal 'check-the-box' regime exists; however, anti-hybrid mismatch rules under section 12.2 of the Income Tax Act and the relevant Multilateral Instrument provisions limit mismatches arising from foreign entity classification elections, and a foreign partnership may be recharacterized as an entity for Canadian purposes where it has more than five members or a degree of central management. For GST/HST purposes, a partnership and its members are treated as separate supplies and the partnership may elect to have a 'partner representative' handle all obligations on behalf of the partners.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (the 'small supplier' threshold); registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded, but non-resident persons making taxable supplies in Canada may be required to register regardless of the threshold if they are providing services in Canada or carrying on business in Canada. Voluntary registration is generally available for Canadian and non-resident partnerships that supply exempt or zero-rated services, including cross-border legal services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for: (a) supplies of goods exported from Canada by the supplier, provided the exporter retains evidence of export and exports the goods within 60 days of delivering the invoice; (b) supplies of services rendered to a non-resident person, if the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of supply, and not an advisory, consultancy, or inspection service performed physically in Canada (except certain excluded services); and (c) supplies of legal services in connection with litigation or arbitration relating to real property or goods situated outside Canada, provided the services are rendered entirely offshore and the client is a non-resident. To claim zero-rating, the law firm must ordinarily obtain and keep a signed declaration from the non-resident client confirming the client's status as non-resident and the off-shore nature of the services, contemporaneously with the supply.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CERS/Form B13A) confirming actual export for goods; for pure offshore legal services, the executed engagement letter and client non-residency declaration serve as the export evidence
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent shipping document (for goods) / Written evidence that the service was performed wholly outside Canada or in respect of foreign property (for legal services)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate and bank wire confirmation (SWIFT MT103) showing payment received from the non-resident client in foreign or Canadian currency

Canada does not currently impose a government-approved real-time electronic invoicing or tax clearance platform at the federal level. GST/HST returns and information returns must be filed electronically through CRA's GST/HST NETFILE service or by prescribed machine-readable format when required. Businesses must retain electronic and paper source documents, including invoices, receipts, and contracts, and CRA may require conversion of electronic records to a readable format on audit. Fiscal-clearance 'digital stamping' is not a legal requirement in Canada; however, the CRA publishes technical requirements for Business-to-Business e-invoicing using Pan-Canadian PEPPOL specifications, and all registrants must maintain contemporaneous electronic accounting records capable of supporting the GST/HST audit trail.

CRA may audit all export zero-rating claims retrospectively, generally within the normal reassessment period of four years (or longer in cases of misrepresentation). If export documentation is incomplete or the zero-rating test is not met, the supplier is liable for the full GST/HST that would otherwise have been charged, plus late payment interest and penalties. Applying zero-rating without proper substantiation constitutes a false statement under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act, exposing the registrant to penalties of up to CAD 25,000 or 25% of the net tax amount (gross negligence), and to criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Income Tax Act and section 239 of the Excise Tax Act if fraud is established. Directors and partners of the partnership may also be jointly and severally liable for unpaid GST/HST under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act. Reassessments for an export zero-rating claim can claw back refunds, reduce future input tax credits, and forfeit the right to voluntary disclosure protection if the claim is found to be willfully blind.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full IMF Article VIII current- and capital-account convertibility; there are no exchange controls, no prior central-bank permits, and no restrictions on profit remittance, dividend distribution, management-fee payment, or capital repatriation. Non-resident partners exiting a disposition of taxable Canadian property may need a section 116 Income Tax Act clearance certificate, and Canadian withholding taxes apply at statutory treaty rates, but currency conversion is unrestricted at commercial bank rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada has maintained a zero-surrender, zero-mandatory-conversion regime since 1951; exporters are not required to repatriate, convert, or surrender foreign-currency receipts within any statutory timeline, and may lawfully hold foreign-currency bank accounts domestically.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential oversight and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF supervision.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium (restricted). Canada has no EU-style EMI passport; a non-resident digital bank or fintech EMI must register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), maintain Canadian agent oversight, and may require a provincial money-services licence in Quebec (Autorité des marchés financiers). A purely offshore EMI without FINTRAC registration cannot lawfully operate a Canadian-facing settling account and is not accepted as a clearing channel by Canadian Tier 1 banks or commercial counterparties. Furthermore, the label 'IOLTA Trust Account' is a US construct; in Canada, segregated client trust accounts ('en fidéicommis') are reserved for licensed lawyers and notaries under provincial Law Society rules, so a commercial litigation limited partnership typically routes client funds through its supervising law firm's trust account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal base rate of 38% less 10% federal abatement, producing a 28% federal rate before reductions; after the general rate reduction, the effective federal rate is 15%. Combined with provincial general corporate rates (ranging from 8% to 16%), the combined statutory headline rate generally falls between 23% and 31%, depending on province and corporate status. Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) benefit from a reduced federal rate of 9% on active business income up to CAD 500,000, yielding a combined federal-provincial effective rate typically between 9% and 13%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% of the full amount paid or credited to a non-resident person as a dividend or deemed dividend from a corporation resident in Canada. For a limited partnership, non-resident partners are generally not subject to dividend withholding on distributions that constitute partnership profits; however, if a partnership interest is structured as equity of a fiscally transparent entity, the CRA may treat certain distributions as dividends. The 25% statutory rate may be reduced under an applicable tax treaty. For example, under the Canada-United States Tax Convention, a U.S. resident partner receiving a dividend from a Canadian corporation may have a reduced rate of 5% (or 0% in some cases) if certain ownership thresholds are met. The Canada-Canada administrative guidance also provides for a 15% reduced rate in limited circumstances. The partnership must withhold and remit Part XIII tax on any payments that are deemed to be dividends under the Income Tax Act.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T5013 must be filed within five months after the partnership's fiscal year-end. A T2 corporate return must be filed within two months of the fiscal year-end for large corporations, and within three months for CCPCs. Tax instalments are generally due quarterly for corporations. Non-resident partners with Canadian-source business income may be subject to additional Part XIII withholding remittance deadlines.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian transfer-pricing documentation requirements are triggered under the Income Tax Act section 247. A T106 form is required for non-arm's length transactions with non-residents where the total value of reportable transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000. Contemporaneous documentation (Local File and Master File) must be prepared before the tax return filing deadline and provided to the CRA within three months of a request. The CRA's TTP (Transfer Pricing Review) manual specifies that a Master File is required for multinational groups with total gross revenue exceeding CAD 750 million (or as published). Local File is required for transactions exceeding the CAD 1,000,000 threshold. Country-by-country reports (CbCR) must be filed by ultimate parent entities resident in Canada with consolidated group revenue of CAD 750 million or more. For a limited partnership, if the partnership itself is not an MNE, each corporate partner may need to comply with documentation duties.

Economic substance scope: Under Canadian tax law, economic substance is not a standalone statutory doctrine but is embedded in the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR). A transaction or series of transactions that results in a tax benefit and is not primarily for bona fide business purposes may be recharacterized under subsection 245(2). In Canada (National Revenue) v. Gescan Ltd., 2023 FCA 7, the Federal Court confirmed that the GAAR applies where the transaction lacks economic substance and is designed to avoid tax. The commercial litigation services industry is subject to scrutiny for 'shell' partnerships used primarily to access treaty benefits. CRA also may apply the doctrine of 'sham' at common law. Economic substance is assessed by examining whether the partnership has employees, books, records, physical premises, and business operations that actually generate the income. For limited partnerships, the CRA may look through to the general partner's substance and the limited partner's participation in decision-making. Tax treaty relief may be denied where the principal purpose of a structure is treaty shopping, as codified by the Multilateral Instrument (MLI) Article 29 (LOB) for Canada's treaty network. Therefore, the limited partnership must demonstrate real profit-generating activities, including negotiation, management, delivery, and assumption of risks, to satisfy the economic substance requirement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and willful omission of income

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, tax evasion is subject to a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. The gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) is 25% of the additional tax payable. CRA may also seize books and records, impose a requirement to pay, and issue a 'Director's Liability' assessment under section 227.1.

Director personal liability: Directors or senior officers who knowingly participate in filing false returns are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid taxes, unless they exercised reasonable diligence. Personal civil liability may include restitution orders, criminal forfeiture, and travel bans under a court order. Directors may be personally assessed for Part XIII withheld taxes under section 227.1 if the partnership fails to remit.
Shell company and sham partnership structure

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize the entire partnership arrangement under the sham doctrine or GAAR, nullifying treaty benefits and penalizing 25% of any additional tax. The partnership may be disregarded for tax purposes, and all income allocated to the non-resident partner may be treated as taxable Canadian business income without treaty relief. Penalties of up to CAD 100,000 under section 163.2 may apply to false statements made by the firm/organizer.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner may be personally liable for unpaid taxes of the partnership if it is found to be a sham. Civil courts may pierce the corporate veil and order directors to repay amounts extracted from the partnership. The Minister may certify the amount and obtain a Federal Court judgment allowing execution against personal assets. Travel bans may be imposed to prevent directors from leaving Canada, and criminal prosecution for conspiracy to defraud can carry imprisonment.
AML and proceeds of crime (FINTRAC non-compliance)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) up to CAD 1,000,000 for a partnership (and up to CAD 500,000 per officer/director). Criminal charges under the Criminal Code for money laundering may result in up to 10 years' imprisonment and fines of up to CAD 1,000,000. FINTRAC may also issue a compliance order iterating direct sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner or the law firm are personally liable for monetary penalties when they knowingly carry out or fail to prevent the violation. A court may order payment of enforcement costs, disgorgement of fees received, and travel bans pending further investigation. Directors who facilitate a money-laundering transaction face criminal charges with a maximum penalty of life imprisonment where the proceeds exceed CAD 1,000,000 (Criminal Code s. 462.31).
Failure to file T5013/T2 and failure to remit source deductions

Statutory sanction: Late filing penalty (subsection 162(3)) of 5% of any balance due plus 1% per month up to 12 months, and a minimum CAD 100. For failure to file information return (section 162(7)), CAD 100 per day for each day after the deadline, up to CAD 2,500. Gross negligence penalty (section 163(2)) of 25% of any additional tax. If no tax was owed, a flat penalty of CAD 500 per partner may apply. CRA may also apply interest at the prescribed rate plus 10%.

Director personal liability: Section 227.1 imposes liability on directors of corporations, and section 227.1(3) may apply to directors of a corporate general partner if the partnership fails to remit withheld amounts. Directors who cannot demonstrate reasonable diligence are personally liable for the full amount of unremitted Part XIII, payroll, and GST/HST. Directors may be ordered to make payment to the Crown as restitution; prolonged absences can trigger travel bans. The CRA can assess the director personally and seek registration of the certificate with the Federal Court.
Unauthorized practice of law / IOLTA trust account mismanagement

Statutory sanction: Provincial law society acts (e.g., Law Society Act of Ontario) impose fines and imprisonment for unauthorized practice. A partnership convicted of unauthorized practice may be fined up to CAD 50,000 for the first offence, CAD 100,000 for subsequent offences. Trust account mismanagement may lead to a requirement to remit funds plus interest and penalty. The partnership may be disgorged of fees earned from unlicensed activity.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly facilitate unauthorized practice or who sign documents as legal counsel without licence are personally liable for civil damages and may be ordered to pay restitution to the client. They may be prohibited from holding any trust and ordered to pay the Law Society's costs. Criminal contempt of court can result in imprisonment. Travel bans may be issued if the director flees to avoid restitution.
Transfer-pricing documentation failures

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3), a penalty equal to 10% of the amount of adjustment (i.e., the gross understatement or overstatement) may be imposed if contemporaneous documentation is not provided within three months of the CRA's request. Failing to file T106: for each failure, the greater of CAD 2,000 and the sum of CAD 100 per day for each day after the deadline, up to CAD 50,000 per failure. CRA may also adjust the transaction at fair market value.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or acquiesce in the filing of incomplete or false transfer-pricing forms may be liable for negligence penalties under section 163.2. They may be subject to a penalty of CAD 100,000 where the balance of penalties exceeds CAD 5,000 due to knowing, or grossly negligent conduct. Civil liability includes restoration of any corporate capacity. Travel bans can be sought where the director is a non-resident and owes penalties.
