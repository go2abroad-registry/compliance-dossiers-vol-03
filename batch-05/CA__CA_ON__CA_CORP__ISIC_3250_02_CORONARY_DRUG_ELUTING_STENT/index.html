Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — excise/GST/HST and corporate income tax administration; CRA 'My Business Account' portal is the primary digital service channel, with separate registration for customs and import/export accounts through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) system.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a Canadian corporation must incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincially under the Ontario Business Corporations Act, obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Form RC1 or online registration, and then register for GST/HST, payroll, and import/export accounts under that same BN. A non-resident corporation without Canadian incorporation that is carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada may also apply for a BN and GST/HST registration directly using Form RC1 or RC1A. The BN serves as the statutory TIN for tax, customs, and indirect-tax purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a check-the-box classification regime; corporations are automatically taxable entities under the Income Tax Act, subject to worldwide taxation if resident, and non-resident corporations are taxed only on Canadian-source income. Relevant elections include the family of corporate tax elections under the Income Tax Act (e.g., section 216 election for non-resident rental income, section 217 elections for certain Canadian-source income), the GST/HST section 156 election for closely related corporations and non-corporate entities, and the section 167 election to apply GST/HST to certain sales of a business. Hybrid mismatch rules apply through the Income Tax Act and the OECD Multilateral Instrument, but no elective hybrid classification exists.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada over a single calendar quarter or over the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters, unless the supplier qualifies as a small supplier; additional special registration rules apply to non-resident suppliers making taxable supplies in Canada, including mandatory registration in certain e-commerce and marketplace scenarios.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of qualifying medical devices, including coronary drug-eluting stents and bioresorbable polymer scaffolds, are zero-rated under the Excise Tax Act if the goods are exported by or on behalf of the supplier and are not acquired for consumption, use, or supply in Canada before export. The supplier must hold visible proof of export in a form that satisfies the CRA's prescribed evidentiary requirements, including a customs export declaration, commercial invoice, bill of lading, and evidence of payment in a foreign currency. Services that are performed entirely outside Canada or that are directly attributable to exported goods may also qualify for zero-rating where the recipient is not in Canada and the service does not relate to real property in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Export Data System / CBSA declaration and corresponding Customs RMD return),
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or truckwaybill showing the consignee outside Canada and the port/place of export),
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent wire transfer/SWIFT payment evidence confirming the foreign purchaser's payment in convertible foreign currency

No real-time fiscal clearance, API-based e-invoicing mandate, or digital stamping regime is currently in force in Canada. Vendors may issue electronic invoices and all prescribed GST/HST invoice content must be retained in an auditable format. The CRA supports electronic data interchange (EDI), XML, and PDF invoice storage, but does not operate continuous transaction controls. A GST/HST registrant must keep adequate books and records containing copies of invoices, customs documentation, and proof of export for a minimum of six years, and CRA may conduct electronic audits through business systems.

The CRA may reassess GST/HST returns and deny zero-rating claims where the exporter fails to maintain sufficient documentary proof of export, and may recover input tax credits and charge interest from the original due date. Deliberate false statements or omissions in a GST/HST return may attract a penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded, and gross negligence penalties apply to the refund claim amount. For wilful evasion, the Excise Tax Act authorizes summary or indictable proceedings with fines of up to 200% of the tax or an amount that covers 100% of the evaded tax, plus the possibility of imprisonment. Further, the Income Tax Act imposes general anti-avoidance and false-tax-return penalties in cases involving sham export transactions or fabricated outward remittances.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely floating, fully convertible on both current account and capital account, with no exchange control restrictions on acquisition, holding, or sale of foreign currency. A Canadian federal or Ontario business corporation may remit dividends, repay intercompany loans, and repatriate paid-up capital without Bank of Canada or OSFI approval. Sole residual hurdles are FINTRAC reporting of electronic fund transfers of C$10,000 or more, tax gross-up obligations on non-resident dividend withholding under the Income Tax Act, and enterprise-level national security screening under the Investment Canada Act for large inbound direct investments.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada abolished mandatory surrender and repatriation of export proceeds in 1951. For hospital or distributor export receivables (CAD, USD, EUR, or CNH), there is no statutory conversion ratio, no time limit for collection or retention of foreign currency, and no obligation to convert export proceeds into Canadian dollars. Only transfer-pricing and export documentation rules under the Income Tax Act and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) trade data requirements apply to export sales.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting with the Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no standalone foreign exchange control administration or exchange control permit authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium feasibility with material constraints. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, and Nuvei are viable for low-value supplier disbursements, collections from distribution partners, and payroll routing; however, they are not licensed as banks, may not issue import commercial letters of credit, are not CDIC-insured deposit takers, and when serving Canadian residents they must register with FINTRAC as money services businesses and, once fully in force, with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act. Use EMIs solely as secondary clearing pinch-points and cap treasury balances at less than 30 days of operating cash.

Statutory Canadian withholding tax rate is 25% on dividends paid or credited by a corporation resident in Canada to a non-resident person (Part XIII tax), unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty; Canada-US DTA typically reduces the rate to 5% on dividends paid to a US corporation owning at least 10% of the voting shares and 15% in other portfolio cases; the UK treaty provides 5% for a company controlling at least 10% of the voting power; the parent-subsidiary directive-like relief does not apply from non-EU/EEA countries; for a shareholder resident in a treaty jurisdiction that does not reduce the rate, the statutory 25% rate remains

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File / Master File preparation is required under section 247 of the Income Tax Act and Information Circular IC 87-2R for transactions between the Canadian entity and non-arm's-length foreign affiliates exceeding CAD 1,000,000 in gross annual transaction value; a full contemporaneous documentation package is mandatory if the total value of reportable transactions with all non-resident related parties exceeds CAD 1,000,000, and a Master File / Country-by-Country report may be required when the consolidated group revenue in the immediately preceding fiscal year exceeds CAD 1 billion (CbC threshold); Ontario-specific transfer pricing documentation follows the federal requirement; a corporation engaged in drug-eluting stent manufacturing with cross-border purchase of drug coatings or polymer materials from a foreign affiliate must maintain a CRA Form T2086 (Rescheduling of Transfer Pricing Adjustments) if the taxpayer proposes to make a transfer pricing adjustment

Economic substance scope: The Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as applied in Canadian domestic law are grounded in section 245 of the Income Tax Act (general anti-avoidance rule, GAAR), which denies a tax benefit from an avoidance transaction that is not primarily based on legitimate business, commercial, or family purposes; the OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) framework and the OECD Model Tax Convention Article 9 are incorporated by the CRA through transfer pricing guidelines; Canadian jurisprudence, including Canada v. Alta Energy Luxembourg S.A.R.L., clarifies that a Luxembourg-resident shareholder may claim treaty benefits despite Canadian GAAR and substance requirements; for a Canadian medical device manufacturing entity that is a subsidiary of a foreign parent, the economic substance doctrine requires that the Canadian entity has real decision-making authority, manufacturing capacity, physical premises, qualified employees, risk control, and business records in Ontario; an artificial allocation of stent intellectual property to a no- or low-tax jurisdiction without meaningful functions, people, assets, and risks may be challenged under the OECD's 'demand-side' royalty withholding, Canadian sourced income rules, and GAAR; Ontario's provincial tax enforcement does not impose an independent separate ESR regime but relies upon federal transfer pricing rules and the Ontario Corporations Tax Act's reasonableness requirements; the active financing and corporate reorganisation exemptions under the Ontario act require substantial presence and substance

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 327(1) of the Excise Tax Act and section 238 of the Income Tax Act, a person who evades tax is liable on summary conviction to a fine of not less than 50% of the tax evaded and not more than 200% of that amount, or both fine and imprisonment for a term not exceeding 24 months on summary conviction; on indictment, the penalty is a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded (no statutory minimum) plus imprisonment for a term up to 5 years; in addition, the taxpayer is liable for the unpaid tax, gross negligence penalties of 100% of the understated tax, and interest under section 161(1); Ontario Corporations Tax Act mirror penalties apply; Voluntary Disclosure Program relief is not available if the disclosure is initiated after CRA enforcement contact

Director personal liability: Under section 160 of the Income Tax Act, a person receiving assets from a taxpayer for less than fair market value is jointly and severally liable for the unpaid tax liability; directors are assessed under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for the failure of the corporation to remit source deductions (payroll, withholding) and GST/HST, and the director's liability is absolute without a defence of good faith, but a due diligence defence exists for directors who exercised reasonable care; the CRA may obtain a director's personal asset seizure, garnish bank accounts, and register a lien against personal property; serious tax evasion leads to criminal prosecution of the directing mind and can include a travel ban under bail conditions; Canada's Income Tax Act provides for 5-year prison sentences and personal liability; Ontario's Securities Commission or the federal Director under the Canada Business Corporations Act may issue a prohibition order barring a director from serving as a director for up to 5 years if the corporation engages in flagrant tax evasion
Shell Company

Statutory sanction: The CRA can disregard the corporate entity under the GAAR (subsection 245(2)) and the 'substance over form' doctrine; all income earned through the shell is reattributed to the taxpayer who economically controls it, creating additional tax, penalties, and interest; under the Canada Business Corporations Act, a court may order the winding-up of a corporation if it is insolvent or if it was incorporated for a fraudulent or unlawful purpose; a shell company that fails to file its annual corporate return and annual information return (Form 3443 or the Ontario Annual Return) for two consecutive years may be dissolved administratively by the Ontario Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery; if a shell entity is used to obtain a treaty reduction on Canadian dividends by a resident of a treaty country that lacks economic substance, the tax authority may deny treaty relief and impose Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on all dividends, plus interest on deficient withholding and penalties for false certificates; the CRA's aggressive GAAR assessments can result in a corporate tax reassessment for the entire value of revenue channelled through the shell, and a criminal charge of counselling tax evasion may be brought against the shareholder if they knowingly used a shell to avoid tax

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a shell corporation can be held personally liable for a corporation's unpaid taxes to the extent that they directed funds to themselves or related parties under the shareholder-liability rules in section 160; a court can pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a mere facade or agent, enabling the CRA collection to recover unpaid tax from the directors' personal property; the Ontario Business Corporations Act permits a court to order restitution and a director may be subject to a criminal conviction for fraud or fraudulent conveyance; travel bans under the Criminal Code can be imposed when an accusation of tax fraud and money laundering is made against a non-resident director; imprisonment of up to 2 years on summary conviction or 5 years on indictment may apply for the intentional use of a shell company to evade corporate tax; the Civil Remedies Act of Ontario allows a court to freeze a director's assets and order disgorgement of profits derived from a sham structure
AML

Statutory sanction: The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) may levy an administrative monetary penalty (AMP) of up to CAD 500,000 for an entity with a schedule I bank and up to CAD 150,000 for other money services businesses; for a medical device manufacturer, the penalty for failure to report a large cash transaction or electronic funds transfer is up to CAD 100,000 for a first contravention; a corporation that is not registered under the PCMLTFA as a money service business but acts as an unregistered MSB by wire-transferring funds is subject to criminal fines of up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment of up to 5 years for officers; the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act also authorizes a freeze order by the Minister of Finance for assets associated with money laundering; convicted entities may be added to the Canadian trade blacklist and barred from holding a Health Canada Class III medical device establishment licence; CRA and FINTRAC share information and can impose a joint tax and AML compliance order requiring a total restitution and civil penalty

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who were involved in or failed to take reasonable measures to prevent money laundering are criminally liable under section 82 of the PCMLTFA; they may be prosecuted and face imprisonment for up to 10 years if the offence is done knowingly for the purpose of evading the act; directors who manage the company's payment rails (Letters of Credit, Swift Wire) and deliberately sign false transactional records may be sued by the Crown for civil recovery under the Ontario Civil Remedies Act; FINTRAC can issue a compliance order against a director to implement required anti-money laundering policies; personal asset freeze and travel bans under the Criminal Code section 83.3 may be ordered where there is reasonable grounds to believe that a director will leave Canada to evade prosecution; a conviction leads to a personal criminal record and prohibition from serving as an officer of any publicly traded or licensed company for 10 years
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Under section 247(2) of the Income Tax Act, the CRA may adjust the transaction price to the arm's-length amount, resulting in an upward correction of taxable income and an assessment of Part XIII withholding tax on any deemed dividend or royalty; a transfer pricing penalty under section 247(3) applies at 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts to establish a documented arm's-length price, and no reasonable efforts defence is available for taxpayers that did not prepare contemporaneous documentation; the penalty is in addition to back taxes, but not subject to gross negligence penalty; if the adjustment produces a dividend deemed under section 214(3), additional Part XIII withholding at 25% applies with no corresponding foreign tax credit; interest on the transfer pricing adjustment accrues from the date the tax should have been paid; re-assessments may be made for up to 7 years after the initial assessment if the taxpayer made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect or willful default

Director personal liability: The transfer pricing adjustment attaches to the corporation, but directors may be liable for the corporation's tax debt under the CRA's director liability regime if the corporation fails to collect or remit Part XIII withholding taxes; a director who knowingly certified a false transfer pricing report may be guilty of tax evasion under section 239, leading to a fine and imprisonment up to 5 years; the CRA can pursue a director's personal assets where the corporation is stripped of its assets by a non-resident parent and left unable to pay the tax; individuals involved in a transaction that artificially reduces Canadian taxes through transfer pricing can be reassessed as transfer pricing beneficiaries under subsection 247(8); criminal liability also arises for false statements to a public official under section 122 of the Criminal Code; a court may order the director to repatriate the profit earned from the transfer pricing scheme under equitable remedies
