Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and the non-resident registration channels. Corporate and partnership legal registration is administered federally by Corporations Canada where applicable or by the relevant provincial or territorial authority; the CRA administers federal income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and information-return accounts.

E-government filing portal

Apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) and the required program accounts using Business Registration Online where eligible, or Form RC1, Request for a Business Number, and the applicable non-resident registration process. 3. Register for a GST/HST account where the partnership is required or elects to register; provide effective date, worldwide and Canadian taxable revenues, commercial activity, partners, authorized representative, banking and contact information, and supporting legal documents when requested. 4. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes: the partnership computes income or loss, and partners are taxed on their allocated shares under the Income Tax Act. Canada has no general U.S.-style check-the-box election for partnerships. A partnership may have statutory choices affecting fiscal periods, method and timing of particular income items, and designated partnership or information filings where applicable, but these do not generally convert the LP into a corporation. A non-resident partner may be subject to Canadian tax on income from a business carried on in Canada and may have Canadian filing, withholding, instalment, or treaty obligations. Section 85 rollover elections can apply to qualifying transfers to a Canadian corporation, but they are transaction-specific and do not constitute entity classification elections. Hybrid-entity and hybrid-mismatch outcomes must be analyzed under Canadian foreign-affiliate, treaty, interest-limitation, anti-avoidance, and cross-border rules; no general elective partnership-to-corporation classification election is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding specified supplies and subject to the applicable small-supplier rules, measured over the relevant single-quarter and consecutive-four-quarter periods. A person exceeding the threshold must generally register and begin charging GST/HST from the applicable effective date. Voluntary registration is generally available before the threshold is exceeded. The threshold does not eliminate registration requirements arising under special non-resident rules or other statutory provisions. HST rates vary by participating province: 13% in Ontario, 14% in Nova Scotia, and 15% in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, and Prince Edward Island. The applicable rate depends principally on the place-of-supply rules and the province of supply.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Qualifying exported goods are generally zero-rated where the goods are supplied for export and the statutory conditions under the Excise Tax Act are satisfied, including delivery or transfer in prescribed circumstances and documentary evidence of export. Goods exported from Canada by or on behalf of the supplier may qualify where the supplier retains evidence such as customs records, bills of lading, carrier confirmations, purchase orders, contracts, and proof of delivery outside Canada. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where a specific statutory provision applies. General consulting, management, repair, towage, salvage, transportation, and other maritime services require a transaction-specific place-of-supply and zero-rating analysis. Certain services physically performed in Canada, services in respect of real property or tangible personal property in Canada, and services relating to goods situated in Canada may remain taxable. Marine towage or salvage performed in Canadian waters, involving Canadian property, or supplied to a person in Canada cannot be assumed to be zero-rated merely because the customer is foreign or payment is received offshore. A GST/HST registrant may claim input tax credits for GST/HST incurred in commercial activities, subject to documentary evidence, attribution rules, restrictions, and any applicable non-resident rebate regime. Exporters should retain contracts, customer residency evidence, voyage and port records, vessel logs, bills of lading, salvage documentation, customs records, invoices, proof of payment, and proof of foreign delivery or performance.

  • CBSA export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where required
  • Commercial invoice and contract or purchase order identifying supplier, customer, goods or services, value, destination, and delivery terms
  • Bill of lading, sea waybill, cargo manifest, vessel records, or carrier confirmation evidencing export and foreign destination, where goods are exported
  • Proof of delivery or receipt outside Canada, where relevant
  • Customs broker records and export accounting records, where a broker is used
  • Foreign customer residency and business-purpose evidence for services supplied to non-residents
  • Banking and payment records supporting the commercial transaction; Canada does not generally require an inward remittance certificate as a condition of GST/HST zero-rating
  • Service-performance records, voyage logs, port records, crew and vessel documentation, and salvage or towage reports for maritime services
  • GST/HST invoices and input-tax-credit documentation supporting refund claims

Canada has no general federal real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance or electronic-invoicing mandate for ordinary commercial transactions. Registered suppliers must issue documentary evidence meeting GST/HST information requirements, retain adequate records, and provide prescribed invoice information for input tax credit support, with requirements varying by transaction value. Electronic records and invoices are generally acceptable if they preserve integrity, accessibility, and auditability. GST/HST returns are filed electronically where mandatory under applicable CRA rules and may be filed through CRA digital services, but invoices are not generally submitted to the CRA for pre-clearance. Commercial exporters must use the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) electronic export declaration system, currently the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where an export declaration is required. Marine cargo, customs, and reporting requirements may also apply through CBSA systems, but no universal digital GST/HST stamp is required.

Canada generally has no statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated to Canada within 180 days, and no general inward-remittance certificate is required to establish GST/HST zero-rating. The supplier must substantiate the export or statutory zero-rating conditions and retain records for the applicable retention period. If zero-rating is unsupported or incorrectly claimed, the CRA may assess the supplier for GST/HST that should have been collected, deny related input tax credits or rebates, assess interest, and apply applicable failure-to-file, false-statement, gross-negligence, or other administrative penalties. A registrant that knowingly makes false statements or participates in fraud may face civil penalties, criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under the Excise Tax Act and related legislation. Refunds or input tax credits obtained on the basis of false, incomplete, or fabricated export evidence may be reassessed and clawed back with interest and penalties. Customs violations may separately result in administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, reassessment of duties or taxes, and prosecution under the Customs Act. The CRA may audit contracts, customer status, vessel and port records, CERS declarations, bills of lading, delivery evidence, payment trails, subcontractor invoices, input-tax-credit records, and the actual place and nature of towage or salvage performance.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada does not operate general exchange controls or restrictions on capital or current account convertibility. The Canadian dollar is fully convertible, and there is no central bank approval requirement for inward capital contributions, outward trade payments, or profit remittances. Non-resident partners of a Canadian limited partnership are subject to Part XIII withholding tax on Canadian-source partnership distributions, reduced where a tax treaty applies. Profit repatriation is further subject to income tax compliance, FINTRAC reporting for cross-border transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, and sanctions/OFAC screening obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds or to convert foreign currency into Canadian dollars. Exporters may retain foreign currency in authorized accounts. All income for tax purposes must be reported in Canadian dollars, and cross-border payments exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported in accordance with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). No prescribed conversion ratios or repatriation timeline apply to Canadian export receipts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating jointly with the Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for monetary and prudential supervision; anti-money laundering oversight is exercised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility is Low-to-Medium for offshore digital EMIs. An offshore EMI lacking a Canadian chartered bank licence cannot hold Canadian-dollar deposits or operate as an authorized deposit-taking institution. A fintech can act only as a payment service provider and must register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC. For maritime salvage and towage contract flows, including escrowed Lloyd's Standard Form settlements, Tier-1 chartered bank rails remain the compliance baseline; fintech EMIs may be used solely for ancillary vendor payments after full licensing, sanctions-screening, and AML validation.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inward routing uses SWIFT MT103 wires with explicit beneficiary details at a Tier-1 Canadian bank. The remitter must state a purpose code consistent with a capital contribution or investment to a Canadian limited partnership, e.g. 'Capital Contribution' or 'Investment-Distribution'. No Bank of Canada exchange-control permit is required. For CAD-denominated wires, the Canadian clearing route is the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) or the Real-Time Rail (RTR) where available; for USD-denominated wires, the Canadian bank's US correspondent channel is used. The bank will require the LP's account number, branch/transit number (for CAD), and full legal entity name. FINTRAC reporting applies to every cross-border transfer of CAD 10,000 or more, and the originating bank must pass through sanctions and AML screening; source-of-funds documentation is mandatory for first-time capital injections.Trade and salvage settlements are executed using one or more of the following rails: SWIFT MT103 for high-value single-payment wire disbursements; MT202 COV where a cover payment is required between correspondent banks; Commercial Letters of Credit for documentary trade settlements in which the Canadian bank is the confirming or advising bank; and Lloyds Standard Form wire settlement through the designated escrow agent. CAD payments settle through LVTS or ACSS depending on size, while USD cross-border payments are routed through the Canadian bank's US clearing correspondent. Outbound payments must include the same purpose/remittance details, and must be screened against Canadian economic sanctions and FINTRAC reporting thresholds.
