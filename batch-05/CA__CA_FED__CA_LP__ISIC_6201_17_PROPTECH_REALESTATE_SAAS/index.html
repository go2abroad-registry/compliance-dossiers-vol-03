Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

For a federal CBCA limited partnership (and the general partner), a registered office must be physically located in Canada and be a street address where process can be served during ordinary business hours. A virtual office address (mail forwarding, non-territorial digital mailbox, or co-working reception address) is permissible only if the address is a real Canadian street address, the general partner maintains a human agent of process present or a designated agent with a fixed Canadian address, and the registered office cannot be solely a P.O. Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital for a Canadian limited partnership; capital commitments are set by the partnership agreement and may be funded by the limited partners on agreed terms. No escrow or bank verification is required under federal or provincial limited partnership law.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — administered via canada.ca/taxes, Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca/business-registration, and My Business Account; GST/HST filings made through GST/HST NETFILE.

E-government filing portal

The Limited Partnership, acting through its general partner, obtains a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a 15-character GST/HST Program Account (RT reference number) by (i) applying online through Business Registration Online (BRO), (ii) submitting Form RC1 by mail/fax, or (iii) telephoning 1-800-959-5525. The application must state the LP's legal name, provincial or federal registration particulars, principal business activity (NAICS 531390), Canadian business address, and the identity and residence of each partner. Registration is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000; voluntary registration is available. For a non-resident partnership, the same procedure applies, a Canadian representative must be designated for service of documents, and the CRA may require security under the Excise Tax Act.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act; the LP files an information return (Form T5013) and allocates income to partners without entity-level federal income tax. Territorial_Scope: Canadian-resident partners are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident partners are taxed only on Canadian-source income allocated from the LP, subject to Part I and Part XIII withholding rules. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act; the LP files an information return (Form T5013) and allocates income to partners without entity-level federal income tax. Territorial_Scope: Canadian-resident partners are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident partners are taxed only on Canadian-source income allocated from the LP, subject to Part I and Part XIII withholding rules. For GST/HST purposes the LP is a separate 'person' and must itself register, collect, and remit.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box election; partnership flow-through status is mandatory. Available elections include: (i) non-calendar fiscal period election under ITA s. 249.1(4); (ii) GST/HST reporting-frequency and filing-period elections (monthly, quarterly, annual); (iii) GST/HST closely related corporation election under ETA s. 156, available to corporate partners; (iv) partnership capital-cost-allowance and method elections; and (v) tax treaty elections for non-resident partners to claim reduced withholding rates. Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4 to 18.6, implementing BEPS Action 2) apply mandatorily with no elective opt-out.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 148(1); for non-residents, the threshold is computed on worldwide taxable supplies, and foreign suppliers of digital services to Canadian consumers are subject to the same CAD 30,000 Canadian-revenue simplified registration threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 of the Excise Tax Act zero-rates a supply of services made to a non-resident, subject to carved-out supplies: services rendered to an individual physically present in Canada, services in respect of real property situated in Canada, services in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada, and agency/courier services. Cloud-based SaaS, rental-screening analytics, and software subscriptions supplied to non-resident recipients qualify for 0% GST/HST where no carve-out applies. The real-property carve-out is material for this PropTech platform: SaaS supplied to a non-resident landlord relating to Canadian real estate (e.g., Canadian apartments under management) may be taxable at 5% GST rather than zero-rated. The supplier must document the recipient's non-resident status (ordinary residence, foreign address, and, where present, the non-resident's GST/HST registration status) and evidence that the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada at the time of supply.

  • Customs Export Declaration filed through the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) — applicable only to tangible media containing the software; not applicable to pure cloud/SaaS delivery
  • Signed SaaS master subscription agreement or service contract with each non-resident customer, including governing law and delivery-territory clauses
  • Commercial invoices bearing a 0% zero-rating indicator and the non-resident customer's foreign billing address
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill — required only where physical media or hardware is exported
  • Payment and FX repatriation evidence: bank confirmation, SWIFT MT103 advice, or Stripe/ACH payout records; the Inward Remittance Certificate is not a Canadian statutory instrument, and bank or payment-processor advices serve as the evidentiary equivalent
  • Service delivery and consumption logs (server access logs, IP geolocation records, login history) demonstrating that the service is delivered to and consumed outside Canada

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance system, mandatory digital stamping, or an API-based tax-authority clearance hub. The Pan-Canadian e-Invoicing initiative is voluntary and interoperable with the Peppol network. Registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE; all registrants must retain invoices and records for six years under ETA record-keeping requirements. Stripe Billing and ACH Direct payment rails do not trigger additional invoicing clearance obligations.

The CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period, extended to ten years where misrepresentation is attributable to carelessness, neglect, or wilful default. If zero-rating is denied, the supplier is liable for the unremitted GST/HST plus daily compounding interest at the CRA prescribed rate, and the ETA s. 280 penalty applies (greater of CAD 250 and 5% of the error amount; 10% for a repeated error within five years). Wilful evasion is an indictable offence under ETA s. 327, exposing fines up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment of up to five years. Canada imposes no statutory deadline for repatriation of export proceeds; the 180-day benchmark reflects standard MNE transfer-pricing documentation and audit expectations, and delayed repatriation may trigger transfer-pricing adjustments under ITA s. 247.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible on both current and capital accounts under IMF Article VIII. Current-account profit remittances are not subject to exchange-control restrictions but are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (generally 25%, treaty-reduced). Capital inflows and outflows require statistical and transactional reporting to Statistics Canada, FINTRAC, and, where applicable, Investment Canada Act notification or net-benefit review for above-threshold non-Canadian acquisitions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose statutory export-proceeds surrender rules, mandatory conversion ratios, or repatriation timelines for export receipts. Exporters may maintain foreign-currency accounts in Canada and convert freely. Applicable obligations are limited to tax filings, CBSA export declarations, and standard anti-money-laundering recordkeeping.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator; Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign-exchange control agency, with FINTRAC and the Canada Border Services Agency performing related reporting functions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium for Canadian-regulated fintechs and MSB-licensed electronic money institutions; Low for offshore EMIs lacking Canadian registration. An offshore EMI must either obtain authorization as a foreign bank branch under the Bank Act, register as a money services business with FINTRAC, or otherwise meet provincial MSB licensing requirements before providing cross-border clearing or holding Canadian corporate funds. Stripe Treasury and ACH-oriented fintech rails are viable for domestic SaaS collections but require companion bank accounts for FX clearing and regulated hedging.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate on Canadian-source active business income earned by a corporation; for a Limited Partnership, the entity is fiscally transparent and income flows through to partners, who are subject to tax at their applicable rates. The top combined federal-provincial corporate rate for an ordinary active business in Ontario is approximately 26.5%, but the LP itself is not a taxpayer.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder under subsection 212(2) of the Income Tax Act. A Canadian Limited Partnership is normally not a withholding agent for the partnership income itself; each partner pays tax on its share. If the LP earns Canadian property income (rent) or royalties and distributes net cash to a non-resident partner, the distributions are not dividends but are subject to Part XIII withholding on the underlying source income (25% statutory rate for rents and royalties; 15% for royalties and interest under the Canada-U.S. treaty, 10% for interest under Canada-US treaty if arm's length and fully exempt in the U.S., and 15% for dividends under treaty, reduced to 5% if the recipient owns at least 10% of the voting shares of the corporation paying the dividend).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T5013 return: due within five months after the partnership's fiscal year-end. T2 corporate return: due within six months after the corporation's taxation year-end. For a calendar-year partnership, the T5013 is due by May 31. Quarterl installments of corporate income tax may be required for corporations with large tax liabilities, but the LP itself is not subject to those installments; partners are liable for their own quarterly installments if they are corporations. Non-resident payees subject to Part XIII withholding taxes remit the withheld amounts monthly.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under section 237.5 of the Income Tax Act and CRA Information Circular 87-2R, a Canadian taxpayer is required to prepare and contemporaneously update a transfer pricing document (Local File) for each taxation year in which transactions with non-arm's length non-resident persons exceed CAD 500,000 in gross revenues or cost/expenses. A Master File must be prepared if the total amount of intercompany transactions exceeds CAD 10 million, or if the taxpayer's Canadian gross revenue exceeds CAD 50 million and the taxpayer is part of a multinational group. The Canada Revenue Agency's Transfer Pricing Review Manual also requires a country-by-country report (CbCR) for fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2016, for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of EUR 750 million or CAD 1 billion. The Limited Partnership is a pass-through, but each corporate partner that is a constituent entity of a multinational group must comply with the CbCR obligations if the group threshold is met. Failure to prepare and retain the documentation can trigger a penalty of CAD 1,000 per failure for each documentation requirement (up to CAD 5,000) for large entities.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) akin to the UAE, but the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act and the substance-over-form doctrine deny tax benefits from transactions that lack bona fide economic substance. For a Limited Partnership, the CRA will examine whether the partnership is carrying on a real business and whether the limited partners' capital and management are at risk. The limited partnership itself is a legal arrangement, but the partnership agreement must demonstrate active commercial activity: actual SaaS platform development, real estate database management, rental screening algorithms, third-party contracts, employees, and office or cloud infrastructure. A mere passive investment in a partnership that holds, but does not operate through, business substance can be recharacterized as a trust or an association taxable as a corporation. Under the Canadian limited partnership legislation (e.g., Ontario Limited Partnerships Act), a limited partner who takes part in the control of the business forfeits limited liability, which affects economic substance analysis. Furthermore, the CRA's audit policy under Form T5013 requires that the partnership allocate income in line with the real contributions of capital and services; an allocation that shifts income to a low-tax non-resident partner without business purpose and economic substance may be attacked under the GAAR or the sham doctrine.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Non-Filing

Statutory sanction: On summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years under subsection 238(1) and 239(1) of the Income Tax Act; on indictment, a fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalties include the gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated amount, plus interest and late-filing penalties. The CRA may also apply a penalty of CAD 100 per partner per day for the T5013 late filing, and the minister may suspend the partnership's business number.

Director personal liability: General partners are jointly and severally liable for all debts, obligations, and liabilities of the limited partnership, including tax liabilities and penalties, under partnership law and subsection 226(1) of the Income Tax Act. Directors of a corporate general partner may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII withholding taxes under section 227.1; the CRA may assess directors for amounts not remitted (including 10% penalty and interest) regardless of corporate veil. A travel ban may be issued under section 180(2) of the Income Tax Act for individuals who refuse to comply with a demand for information or payment, and the CRA may require the Minister of Public Safety to deny a passport. For large-scale fraud, criminal prosecution and orders of restitution to the Crown are available.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Under the GAAR, the income tax benefit may be denied and tax reassessed with interest; a penalty of 25% of the tax benefit can apply under the revised GAAR rules proposed in Budget 2024 (currently under draft legislative proposals). The partnership may be treated as a sham and dissolved vis-à-vis the CRA. The CRA may impose transfer pricing penalties: the greater of 10% of the net adjustment arising from a transfer pricing mispricing and CAD 1,000. If the partnership's general partner is a shell company in a no-tax jurisdiction, the CRA may deny the partnership's active business status and recharacterize all income as gross Canadian-source royalties subject to Part XIII withholding at 25%, leading to non-resident penalties of 10% and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners have personal exposure for unpaid corporate income tax, unremitted withholding taxes, and transfer pricing penalties under section 247(15) and 227.1 of the Income Tax Act. In a debt-piercing analysis, courts will look to the totality of the arrangement: if the LP is merely a facade, a creditor or the CRA may pierce the limited liability of the limited partners under the 'instrumentality' doctrine articulated in Transamerica Life Insurance Co. of Canada v. Canada Life Assurance Co. (1996). Personal civil liability includes restitutionary damages to the CRA for the amount of tax avoided, plus costs. Under the United Nations Against Corruption and other treaty obligations, directors convicted of tax fraud may face international travel restrictions and extradition in egregious cross-border cases.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the taxable income of the Canadian partner to the arm's length price; the resulting tax difference attracts a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment amount (subsection 247(3)), in addition to interest. For failing to produce or provide documentation, a penalty of CAD 1,000 per failure can be imposed for each partner with T5013 filing obligations, and if the taxpayer fails to comply with a demand, the penalty is CAD 1,000 per month of continuance. For a taxpayer that fails to file the T5013 within 12 months, the penalty is CAD 500 per partner, up to CAD 5,000. A court may also impose a punitive sanction for non-cooperation under the Income Tax Act if the audit was deliberately obstructed.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner who knowingly participate in transfer pricing mispricing can be held liable for penalties up to the amount of the corporation's unpaid Part XIII and source deduction remittances (section 227.1). They may also be culpable for an offence of evasion if they direct the mispricing, giving rise to imprisonment up to 5 years and personal fines up to 200% of the tax evaded. In civil proceedings, directors are jointly and severally liable for the return of any tax benefit obtained via the artificial arrangement, and the court may order accountings and disgorgement of profits.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, penalties range from CAD 1,000 to CAD 2,000,000 per violation for entities; egregious violations are prosecuted as offences subject to fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years. FINTRAC may impose an administrative monetary penalty (AMP) of up to CAD 2 million for non-reporting of suspicious transactions. The CRA will also deny loss claims and expense deductions associated with illegal laundering activities. In cases where the partnership is found to be a vehicle for money laundering, the Attorney General may seek forfeiture of the platform's assets and all profits traceable to the activity.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers are personally liable for every failure of the entity to comply with the PCMLTFA if they knew or were grossly negligent in not knowing of the violation (section 74.1). Penalties can aggregate to the greater of CAD 2,000,000 per breach, and officers can be imprisoned for up to 5 years. The CRA and FINTRAC have reciprocal information-sharing protocols: personal bank account freezes, travel bans to prevent flight risk, and personal asset seizure proceedings may be applied. In extreme cases, directors can be extradited under the Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties on money-laundering charges.
Non-Resident Withholding and Remittance Failures

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent (the partnership) is liable for the full amount of the unwithheld tax plus interest at the prescribed rate from the date the tax should have been remitted. A penalty of 10% of the unremitted amount is imposed if the amount was not remitted within the required period; if a third assessment for failure to remit has occurred, the penalty can rise to 20%. The CRA may also assess non-resident partners directly for the tax, but the partnership is not exculpated. Repeated failures can trigger criminal liability under subsection 235(2) and imprisonment for up to 2 years.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for the entire unremitted Part XIII withholding tax, penalties, and interest, as if they had collected it under subsection 227.1(1) of the Income Tax Act. They cannot discharge liability by resigning: a director is liable for amounts accruing while they were a director and for amounts the CRA could not collect after the resignation if no reasonable steps were taken. The CRA may issue a director liability assessment and file it as a judgment; personal assets, including home equity and pensions, may be seized. A travel ban under the Income Tax Act can be imposed on a director who fails to pay the assessed amount and attempts to left Canada.
