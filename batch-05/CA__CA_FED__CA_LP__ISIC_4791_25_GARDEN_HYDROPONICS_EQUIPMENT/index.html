Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – GST/HST and federal income tax administration; online portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-business-number-registry.html)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident vendor must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) and separate GST/HST program account. Application is made by completing Form RC1 or using CRA Business Registration Online, providing legal entity type, partnership details, expected Canadian supplies, and non-resident contact information. The CRA issues a 9-digit BN with program account suffixes (e.g., RT for GST/HST).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible – A Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent for federal income tax purposes: the LP itself does not pay federal income tax; each partner reports its share of partnership income. For GST/HST, the partnership is generally treated as a separate person for registration and collection unless a qualifying joint election is made.

Available election forms

Canada does not have a U.S.-style check-the-box regime. A limited partnership may use Income Tax Act s. 96(3) for deemed disposition upon partner changes; for GST/HST, a joint election under ETA s. 273(1)(b) can attribute partnership supplies to partners. Hybrid mismatch and foreign entity recharacterization rules apply under ss. 12.6–12.72, 18.4–18.5, 19.1, 20.11, and 94–94.3. A non-resident partner may also elect under s. 216.1 to compute Canadian-source business income on a net basis.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide gross revenues from taxable supplies in a calendar quarter or across four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration applies regardless of Canadian residency.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under ETA Sch. VI applies to supplies of goods exported by the supplier or supplied to a non-resident who exports the goods as commercial cargo; goods physically delivered outside Canada; services supplied to non-residents and intangibles supplied to non-residents when not performed in Canada or related to Canadian real property. The supplier must maintain evidence of export and that the supply was made in the ordinary course of business.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Canadian Export Reporting System / Canada Border Services Agency) and export reference number
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill showing foreign destination and consignee
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order/contract identifying goods, value, and parties
  • Proof of payment / inward remittance (bank wire/MT103) from non-resident buyer
  • Carrier or freight forwarder certificate confirming export
  • Shipping logs and inventory records substantiating movement out of Canada

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, API-based e-invoicing, or digital stamping for B2B e-commerce. Taxpayers must maintain electronic sales logs and issue tax-compliant invoices (simplified invoice for B2B under CAD 150). GST/HST returns are filed electronically via the CRA portal, with remittance deadlines based on the registrant's reporting period.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess net tax if documentary evidence is incomplete. Penalties include ETA s. 280(1) failure to remit, s. 285(1) failure to file, and s. 327 false statements. Gross negligence penalties under Income Tax Act s. 163.2 and ETA s. 327.1 may reach 50% of understated tax; criminal prosecution is possible for fraudulent GST/HST refund claims with fines and imprisonment. Interest accrues retroactively on all denied refunds and clawback assessments.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible; current-account and capital-account transactions, including equity subscriptions, intercompany loans, and profit repatriation, are unrestricted under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. Repatriation of LP profits to a non-resident partner is generally subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, treaty-reducible, e.g., 15% under the Canada-United States tax treaty for dividend-type distributions where applicable). No prior exchange-control approval, registration, or repatriation license is required for inbound capital or outbound distributions, provided cross-border movement of CAD 10,000 or more in currency or monetary instruments is reported to CBSA/FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, forced conversion, or repatriation timeline on export proceeds. Exporters of garden hydroponics equipment (not an Export Control List item) must retain books and records for CBSA reporting purposes; any physical or electronic cross-border transfer of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to CBSA/FINTRAC. Foreign-currency export receipts may be held freely or converted at commercial bank rates; banks apply their own FX account and conversion policies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); FX and AML supervision co-administered with FINTRAC, OSFI, and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). Canada operates without a postwar exchange-control authority; the Bank of Canada issues currency and administers the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration regime for payment service providers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium. Offshore digital EMI or PSP accounts are not prohibited, but a foreign EMI providing retail payment services to Canadians must register as a payment service provider with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and carry a FINTRAC AML program. Foreign EMIs cannot access Payments Canada clearing rails (Lynx/ACSS/RTR) directly without a sponsoring Canadian Schedule I bank; a practical structure is a sponsor/sponsored arrangement or use of a Canadian-registered PSP and MSB (e.g., Wise Canada or Payoneer Canada) for USD/CAD payouts, with all CAD clearing ultimately intermediated by a Canadian bank.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate; a Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to corporate income tax. Taxable income and losses are generally allocated to partners. A corporate partner may be subject to federal tax at 15% generally or 9% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation small-business income, before applicable provincial or territorial tax.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax generally applies to taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident. A limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend; its Canadian tax treatment follows the underlying income and the partnership legislation. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation, the principal threshold is generally gross revenue of at least CAD 10 million for the taxpayer, together with non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. The documentation must support the nature and terms of transactions, functional analysis, economically relevant characteristics, selected method, comparables, adjustments, and results. T106 reporting may apply separately to reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions. Canada does not generally impose a standalone mandatory Master File and Local File filing regime equivalent to jurisdictions that require annual submission of both files; Canada requires contemporaneous documentation, and country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates or certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty purpose, transfer-pricing rules, residency and central-management analysis, and permanent-establishment rules. A Canadian LP conducting online retail should maintain evidence of real commercial operations, inventory or fulfillment arrangements, personnel or outsourced operational control, Canadian banking and accounting records, customer contracts, payment-processing records, decision-making, and risk assumption. A treaty benefit may be denied where an interposed entity lacks commercial purpose or does not control and economically enjoy the relevant income.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include tax owing, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and other statutory penalties. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years; aggravated offences can attract additional consequences under the Income Tax Act and Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where statutory director-liability conditions are met. Criminal conviction may result in imprisonment, fines, restitution, probation, and asset orders. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from bail, probation, immigration consequences, or court orders.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may remain liable for the full amount that should have been withheld, plus arrears interest and failure-to-deduct or failure-to-remit penalties. A payer generally cannot cure the liability merely by asserting that the recipient should have paid the tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally where corporate or partnership remittance failures satisfy the statutory due-diligence and director-liability rules. Deliberate diversion of withheld tax can support criminal prosecution and restitution.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust Canadian income under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny unsupported deductions, assess interest, and require amended information returns.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable solely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made. Personal exposure increases where directors knowingly approve false records, participate in evasion, breach source-deduction obligations, or use the entity to perpetrate fraud.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied under beneficial-ownership, treaty-purpose, anti-abuse, or principal-purpose principles. The GAAR may recharacterize or deny tax benefits, and the CRA may assess tax, interest, penalties, and withholding liability.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is not automatically pierced because a structure is tax-efficient. Personal civil liability may arise for fraud, knowing participation, breach of fiduciary duty, or statutory remittance failures. Criminal liability may include fines and imprisonment where conduct constitutes tax evasion or fraud.
GST/HST and Marketplace Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may assess unremitted GST/HST, deny input tax credits, impose failure-to-file, false-statement, and gross-negligence penalties, charge interest, and conduct audit or collection proceedings. Customs duties, import GST, provincial sales taxes, and Quebec-specific obligations may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts if statutory conditions are satisfied. Personal liability is not automatic for ordinary assessment disputes.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include transaction refusals, account freezing, suspicious-transaction reporting, administrative monetary penalties, seizure or forfeiture, prosecution, and imprisonment under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, Criminal Code, and applicable sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, facilitate, or knowingly ignore unlawful transactions may face personal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, asset forfeiture, director disqualification consequences, and court-imposed restrictions. Travel bans are not automatic but may result from criminal process or court orders.
Corporate and Partnership Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include reassessment, denial of deductions or losses, penalty assessments, cancellation or suspension of accounts, civil claims, and prosecution for fraud or false statements.

Director personal liability: Limited liability is not protection for fraud, intentional misconduct, breach of fiduciary duty, unlawful distributions, or statutory remittance failures. Courts may order restitution, damages, asset preservation, or other relief against responsible individuals.
Record Retention and Audit Obstruction

Statutory sanction: The CRA may impose record-keeping and information penalties, draw adverse factual inferences, reassess on the basis of available information, deny unsupported deductions or credits, and pursue compliance orders or prosecution.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct destruction or obstruction may face personal fines, prosecution, contempt-related consequences, and imprisonment where a court order is breached.
