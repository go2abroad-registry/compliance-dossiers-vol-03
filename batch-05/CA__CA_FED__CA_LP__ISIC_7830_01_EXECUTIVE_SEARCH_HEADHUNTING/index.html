Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada via My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html)

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities and non-resident Limited Partnerships carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) and, if registering for GST/HST, a separate GST/HST account number. Apply online through CRA Business Registration Online, or file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) for Canadian partnerships; non-resident applicants typically file Form RC459 (Request for a Business Number and GST/HST Registration for Non-Residents). CRA may require a security deposit under Excise Tax Act section 296 where the non-resident has no Canadian establishment. The LP itself is a taxable 'person' for GST/HST purposes and must provide its partnership declaration and constituent/partner identification; each partner derives income through the partnership for income tax purposes and is subject to the applicable Part I income tax rules.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible; Canada applies a flow-through/partnership model under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act; the Limited Partnership is not taxable at entity level for income tax, but is treated as a registrant/person for GST/HST purposes. Scope: federal residency-based taxation, with non-resident partners subject to Part XIII withholding on certain distributions and Part I tax on Canadian-source business income attributable to the partnership. — Foreign entities and non-resident Limited Partnerships carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) and, if registering for GST/HST, a separate GST/HST account number. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; Canada applies a flow-through/partnership model under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act; the Limited Partnership is not taxable at entity level for income tax, but is treated as a registrant/person for GST/HST purposes. Scope: federal residency-based taxation, with non-resident partners subject to Part XIII withholding on certain distributions and Part I tax on Canadian-source business income attributable to the partnership.

Available election forms

No 'check-the-box' election analogous to the United States exists in Canadian federal tax law. The LP is statutorily classified as a partnership. Relevant statutory elections include: (i) partnership fiscal-period election under subsection 249.1(1) of the Income Tax Act; (ii) GST/HST partnership election where one partner is appointed as registrar/representative on Form RC1 and accountable for the partnership's net tax; (iii) joint partnership filing elections for income allocation under Form T5013 Summary filing rules; and (iv) hybrid mismatch limitation mechanisms under Canada's anti-hybrid legislation which can recharacterize payments arising from dual-entity or branch mismatches for Canadian tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

30,000 CAD in annual Canadian taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) triggers mandatory GST/HST registration for a small supplier; no threshold applies for non-residents seeking voluntary registration, and mandatory registration may arise earlier for non-residents who solicit orders in Canada or supply advisory/headhunting services performed in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Executive search/headhunting services supplied to a non-resident client are generally zero-rated under section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act when the service is supplied to a non-resident individual/entity and is not supplied in connection with real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or a service rendered wholly within Canada on tangible personal property. Additionally, the supply must not be 'made in Canada' under section 142 of the Excise Tax Act. Eligibility requires the supplier to maintain records demonstrating the non-resident status of the recipient, the nature/place of the service, and the fact that the service was exported/consumed outside Canada. Where services are performed both in and outside Canada, a reasonable apportionment based on time/location of service activities is required.

  • Commercial invoice describing the executive search service and identifying the non-resident purchaser
  • Executed contract/retainer agreement with the non-resident client
  • Proof of non-resident status of the recipient (e.g., foreign tax registration or business address evidence)
  • Customs Export Declaration only if physical goods are exported; for services, a detailed service statement and engagement letter
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill where the service is bundled with export logistics
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or wire transfer/transaction record showing receipt of payment from outside Canada
  • T5013/Income tax records or TP-approved transfer-pricing documentation confirming cross-border service performance

No mandatory real-time e-invoicing or fiscal clearance/stamping regime exists at the federal level. CRA accepts electronic invoices (PDF, XML, EDI) provided the records are accurate, auditable, and retained under the Electronic Records and Electronic Books requirements (RC4058). Canada participates in Peppol interoperability by adopting pan-Canadian standards; however, issuance of invoices remains a commercial practice rather than a statutory real-time filing condition. GST/HST returns must be filed separately via CRA e-services, including GST/HST NETFILE or represent-a-client portals.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess net tax on the supply if the exporter fails to produce corroborative export documentation within the 180-day repatriation/receipt window. Penalties include: (i) unreported/understated GST/HST penalties under Excise Tax Act section 280 (5% plus 1% per month up to a maximum); (ii) false statement penalties under section 285.1 of up to CAD 25,000 and 10% of the aggregate of taxes sought to be evaded; (iii) full retrospective clawback with interest at prescribed rates; and (iv) criminal prosecution for tax evasion involving fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. For artificial schemes lacking economic substance, general anti-avoidance rules under Income Tax Act section 245 may also apply to deny partnership-level tax benefits.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. Canada maintains no general foreign exchange controls or restrictions on the repatriation of profits, dividends, interest, or capital. A non-resident limited partner may remit Canadian dollar funds in and out freely, subject only to ordinary tax withholding obligations on Canadian-source income and anti-money laundering reporting thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender or forced conversion requirements apply to export receipts or any other foreign currency proceeds. Canadian residents and non-residents may maintain foreign currency bank accounts and remit funds abroad without Central Bank approval. Export transactions are reported for statistical purposes only.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential oversight of banks and foreign exchange matters distributed between OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) and FINTRAC for AML/CTF supervision.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium. Internationally licensed digital EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex may offer Canadian dollar settlement accounts and B2B payment rails that are operationally viable for retainer collection and cross-border disbursements. However, they are not Schedule I chartered banks and typically do not hold deposits in Canada. Legal constraints include: (i) no CDIC deposit insurance; (ii) separate corporate onboarding may still require the underlying LP to open a bank account at a regulated Canadian financial institution; (iii) EMI KYC procedures may be more flexible but do not replace statutory obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Non-resident general partner or limited partner with no Canadian presence

Root cause: Non-resident general partner or limited partner with no Canadian presence

Defense evidence: Certified corporate certificates of incumbency, constitutional documents, register of directors and shareholders, passport copies, proof of address, fund source statements from the investing entity's bank, negative news screening report, and a written explanation of the LP's commercial purpose confirming that the executive search and headhunting activity is a genuine operational business.
Capital injection from an offshore jurisdiction or from a third-party payer not named as a partner

Root cause: Capital injection from an offshore jurisdiction or from a third-party payer not named as a partner

Defense evidence: Signed capital contribution agreement, duly notarized resolution of the general partner, bank statement showing the source account and transaction narrative, tax transparency declarations for the LP, and a sworn declaration confirming that the injected funds do not originate from proceeds of crime or unreported economic activity.
Unexpected wire frequency or invoice payments from low-correlation counterparties (headhunting clients)

Defense evidence: Complete client engagement contracts, retainer invoices, dated payment schedules, proof of placement deliverables, and a clear mapping of each inbound wire to a named fee-paying client and corresponding commission/refund obligations under ISIC 7830 executive search scope.
Beneficial ownership complexity of a limited partnership structure

Root cause: Beneficial ownership complexity of a limited partnership structure

Defense evidence: Full org chart covering general partner, limited partners, and ultimate beneficial owners; a written authorization naming all persons with more than 10% ownership interest; certified passports; tax identification letters; and a FINTRAC-compliant politically exposed persons (PEP) screening result for each officer and partner.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital contributions should be routed via SWIFT MT103/TT from the foreign partner's bank account to the LP's Canadian-dollar or US-dollar account held at a Schedule I bank. Remittance instructions should include the remitter's identity, the reference 'Capital Contribution – [LP Name]', and the specific purpose code required by the remitting bank (e.g., '1300 – Equity Capital' or '7020 – Other Domestic Equity'). The receiving Canadian bank will verify source-of-funds documentation under PCMLTFA and may request a capital contribution statement or notarized partnership resolution. No Bank of Canada approval is required, but transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 trigger FINTRAC large cash transaction reporting, and all wire transfers are reportable under FINTRAC electronic funds transfer rules.For operating expenditures and trade settlements, the LP should use the SWIFT MT103 wire rail for direct transfers and retainer fee settlements to international recruiters or service providers. Commercial letters of credit (LC) are permissible but unnecessary for low-risk executive search collections. CAD-to-foreign-currency disbursements are executed by the commercial bank at prevailing market rates; no Canadian exchange control license is required.
