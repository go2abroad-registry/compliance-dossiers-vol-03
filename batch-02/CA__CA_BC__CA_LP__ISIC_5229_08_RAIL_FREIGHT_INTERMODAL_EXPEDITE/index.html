Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — GST/HST and partnership registration via Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca/business-registration-online; BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administered separately by the Government of British Columbia, Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch (new PST e-registration portal as of April 1, 2025).

E-government filing portal

1) File the LP's extraprovincial registration with BC Corporate Registry (Victoria, BC) and appoint an attorney for service of process in BC. 2) Register on CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) to obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: 'RT' (GST/HST) and 'RC' (T5013 partnership information return/schedule 50). 3) Provide entity formation documents (foreign certificate of registration), full legal name, principal place of business in Canada, NAICS 488210, expected annual gross worldwide taxable supplies, and Canadian agent contact. 4) For BC PST, register as a 'vendor' through the BC Consumer Taxation portal (requires BCeID) if making taxable sales in BC (remote-seller threshold CAD 10,000). 5) The LP, although income-tax-transparent, registers under its own BN for GST/HST, T5013, and BC PST because a partnership is a separate 'person' for indirect-tax purposes under ETA s. 272.1(1).

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — 1) File the LP's extraprovincial registration with BC Corporate Registry (Victoria, BC) and appoint an attorney for service of process in BC. 2) Register on CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) to obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: 'RT' (GST/HST) and 'RC' (T5013 partnership information return/schedule 50). 3) Provide entity formation documents (foreign certificate of registration), full legal name, principal place of business in Canada, NAICS 488210, expected annual gross worldwide taxable supplies, and Canadian agent contact. 4) For BC PST, register as a 'vendor' through the BC Consumer Taxation portal (requires BCeID) if making taxable sales in BC (remote-seller threshold CAD 10,000). 5) The LP, although income-tax-transparent, registers under its own BN for GST/HST, T5013, and BC PST because a partnership is a separate 'person' for indirect-tax purposes under ETA s. 272.1(1).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no IRS-style 'check-the-box'; income-tax transparency is mandatory for LP partners under ITA s. 96. Available elections: (i) ITA s. 97(2) elective rollover on contribution of property to the LP; (ii) ITA s. 96(3) partnership fiscal-period election; (iii) ETA s. 156 'qualifying partnership' election to disregard certain supplies among partners; (iv) ETA s. 151/273 small-supplier voluntary registration; (v) for the non-resident partner, treaty-based allocation (e.g., Canada-US Treaty Art. IV) and US federal check-the-box election (Treas. Reg. 301.7701-3) to classify the Canadian LP as a C corporation or disregarded entity for US purposes, with hybrid-mismatch rules of ITA ss. 18.4-18.6 and ETA s. 383 applicable.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in worldwide gross taxable supplies (including zero-rated) in any single quarter or trailing four quarters, unless supplies are made entirely outside Canada; BC PST: remote sellers transacting CAD 10,000 or more in taxable BC sales per calendar year (no threshold for BC-resident vendors).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

0% GST applies to: (i) transportation of goods from a place in Canada to a place outside Canada (ETA Sch. VI Part V s. 7(a)); (ii) services reasonably ancillary to such export transportation, including forwarding, container handling, and documentation services (Sch. VI Part V s. 7(b)); and (iii) supplies made directly in respect of exported goods. The freight forwarder/LP must be a GST registrant, maintain satisfactory proof that the container block train physically left Canada (CBSA rail eManifest and outbound rail waybill), and invoice the supply to the party responsible for the export movement. Domestic rail legs within Canada are taxable at 5% GST; BC PST does not apply to transportation or freight-forwarding services.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CBSA CERS/eManifest export report for rail traffic (required for goods > CAD 2,000, controlled goods, or destined for certain jurisdictions)
  • Bill of Lading: Original or electronic through rail/intermodal Bill of Lading naming the container(s), port of exit, and foreign consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate: SWIFT or bank letter confirming receipt of foreign-currency payment from the non-resident principal, cross-referenced to the invoice
  • Commercial invoice, packing list, container number/terminal receipt, and CRA proof-of-export worksheet retained for 6 years

No real-time fiscal clearance, pre-clearance stamping, or mandatory B2B e-invoicing exists in Canada. GST/HST is self-assessed on the return; CRA accepts electronic records (EDI/XML/PDF). A voluntary Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework based on the PEPPOL/EN 16931 standard is being phased in but is not statutory. Mandatory electronic filing applies to all GST/HST registrants vs. CRA via GST/HST NETFILE/Representative Client; CERS/eManifest electronic export declarations are mandatory for rail container exports to CBSA.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada generally imposes no capital controls, exchange-control licensing, compulsory repatriation, or mandatory conversion of foreign currency receipts. A British Columbia limited partnership may receive foreign currency, maintain foreign-currency accounts, contribute or return capital, pay foreign suppliers, and remit distributable profits subject to partnership documentation, lender restrictions, sanctions, AML/KYC controls, tax compliance, and the receiving country's rules. Non-resident partners may generally receive partnership distributions without a Canadian exchange-control approval. Canadian tax consequences remain separate from currency convertibility: partnership income is generally allocated under the partnership agreement and applicable Canadian tax rules; non-resident withholding, information reporting, transfer-pricing, indirect-tax, customs, payroll, and permanent-establishment considerations may apply depending on the transaction. Canadian banks may require evidence of source of funds, beneficial ownership, commercial rationale, partnership authority, and expected transaction flows before processing large or unusual remittances.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory foreign-exchange conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline requiring exporters or freight-forwarding businesses to repatriate or convert export receipts. Export proceeds may remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the entity's discretion. The partnership should retain commercial invoices, freight contracts, bills of lading, customs records, customer payment evidence, and bank reconciliation records. Canadian tax returns, partnership information returns, GST/HST filings, customs declarations, payroll remittances, and any applicable non-resident withholding obligations must still be completed within their respective statutory deadlines. Financial institutions may impose contractual settlement deadlines, transaction-monitoring holds, or documentary requirements that are not statutory exchange controls.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no separate foreign exchange administration imposing general exchange controls. Anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls are principally administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada where applicable, the Canada Border Services Agency, and Global Affairs Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary payment operations and low-to-medium as the principal treasury bank. An offshore electronic-money institution is not automatically authorized to provide deposit-taking, payment, money-services, or foreign-exchange services in Canada. The partnership should verify the provider's home-jurisdiction authorization, Canadian licensing or registration status where required, safeguarding arrangements, sanctions controls, beneficial-owner transparency, Canadian payment-rail access, data location, insolvency treatment, and contractual recourse. A foreign fintech may be unable to provide Canadian-dollar domestic clearing, cash-management products, letters of credit, or regulated foreign-exchange dealing without an appropriately authorized Canadian or foreign institution. Canadian reporting entities must comply with applicable Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act obligations, and a payment intermediary may classify the partnership as a higher-risk cross-border logistics customer if payment flows involve sanctioned jurisdictions, nested intermediaries, or third-party collections.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Document the contribution under the limited-partnership agreement and obtain general-partner approval before remittance. Send funds from an account held in the contributing partner's or documented financing party's name to the partnership's Canadian bank account using SWIFT MT103 or the bank's equivalent wire format. Use a truthful payment narrative such as 'capital contribution to [legal name] under partnership agreement' and provide any bank-requested purpose classification; Canada does not impose a universal statutory FX purpose-code regime for ordinary commercial wires. Preserve the wire confirmation, remitter information, contribution agreement, source-of-funds evidence, beneficial-owner records, and bank reconciliation. Do not commingle capital contributions with customer collections without ledger segregation. For foreign direct-investment reporting, tax filings, securities considerations, or lender covenants, assess whether additional filings or approvals are triggered by the ownership, sector, transaction size, or investor jurisdiction. Screen all remitters, intermediaries, and beneficial owners against Canadian sanctions and applicable foreign sanctions before acceptance.Use Canadian bank domestic clearing for CAD payments and correspondent-bank SWIFT wires for foreign-currency payments. SWIFT MT103 or successor formats are appropriate for single commercial payments; structured remittance information should identify invoice number, shipment or booking reference, customer or supplier, currency, and purpose. Use commercial letters of credit, documentary collections, standby letters of credit, or bank guarantees where counterparty or transit risk justifies them. Letters of credit should specify the governing rules, normally UCP 600 for documentary credits or ISP98 for standby letters of credit, document requirements, presentation deadlines, permitted transport documents, sanctions clauses, discrepancy procedures, insurance requirements, and allocation of bank charges. Match each disbursement to an approved invoice, contract, purchase order, freight document, customs record, or payroll obligation. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate for a British Columbia corporation, comprising 15% federal tax and 12% British Columbia tax. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to ordinary corporate income tax; taxable income is allocated to its partners under the Income Tax Act and applicable partnership agreement.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic Canadian Part XIII withholding tax rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. Under the Canada-China income tax treaty, the rate is generally reduced to 10% where the beneficial owner is a company that directly or indirectly controls at least 10% of the voting power of the payer, and to 15% in other treaty-qualified cases. Other treaty rates depend on the recipient's residence, beneficial ownership, ownership threshold, limitation provisions, and applicable treaty article. A Canadian limited partnership does not ordinarily pay dividends; distributions are generally characterized by reference to partnership income, capital, debt repayment, or other legally supported components.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 return is generally due within 5 months after the end of the partnership fiscal period. A December 31 year-end therefore generally produces a May 31 filing deadline. Each partner reports its allocated partnership amounts in its own tax return for the relevant taxation year. A Canadian resident corporate partner generally files its T2 within 6 months after its taxation year-end. A non-resident corporate partner may be required to file a Canadian T2 where it carries on business in Canada, has a taxable Canadian presence, disposes of taxable Canadian property, or is otherwise required by the Income Tax Act.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not use an OECD-style statutory Local File and Master File nomenclature as a direct filing requirement. Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and must generally be prepared by the taxpayer's filing due date and provided to the CRA within 3 months of written demand. The commonly applicable gross-revenue threshold for relief from contemporaneous documentation is CAD 10 million, subject to statutory conditions; reliance on this threshold does not remove the arm's-length requirement, T106 filing, withholding, or audit obligations. Form T106 reporting generally applies to reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions and balances, commonly where the aggregate reportable amount exceeds CAD 1 million. A Canadian entity belonging to a multinational group may be expected to maintain a master-file-level package and country-by-country reporting analysis where the applicable Canadian CbC rules are met, including the CAD 750 million consolidated group-revenue threshold for CbC reporting.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the regimes of certain offshore jurisdictions. Nevertheless, Canadian tax outcomes are tested through the arm's-length principle, treaty beneficial-ownership and anti-conduit concepts, the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and factual-recharacterization doctrines, specific anti-avoidance provisions, interest limitation rules, thin-capitalization rules, and permanent-establishment principles. A partnership or intermediary with no personnel, decision-making authority, operational assets, commercial risk, or genuine control over freight and customer functions may be challenged as a conduit or agency arrangement. Substance must be demonstrated through contracts, personnel, premises, systems, risk control, board or partner decisions, bank activity, customer management, pricing functions, and contemporaneous operational records.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent reporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount, civil penalties for false statements, and criminal prosecution. Tax evasion convictions may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years under the Income Tax Act, in addition to repayment of tax and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal exposure for participation, authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness. A corporate general partner does not shield individuals from personal criminal liability. The CRA may pursue civil restitution and asset recovery; travel restrictions may arise through criminal process, bail, probation, or passport-related court orders, but a travel ban is not an automatic tax penalty.
Unremitted payroll, Part XIII, GST/HST, or other source deductions

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unpaid amount, arrears interest, remittance penalties, late-filing penalties, failure-to-deduct penalties, and potential criminal sanctions. The payer may be liable even where the recipient did not disclose the income or where the payer later disputes the recipient's residency.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules. Directors may also face prosecution for deliberate non-compliance. Limited partners generally are not personally liable solely by reason of status, but participation in management, guarantees, fraud, or statutory breaches can create direct liability.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and profit shifting

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing adjustment to the arm's-length amount, tax on the adjusted income, interest, possible transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, T106 penalties, and denial or limitation of deductions. Treaty relief may be unavailable where the arrangement lacks commercial substance or falls within anti-abuse rules.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be assessed or prosecuted where they knowingly participate in false documentation or evasion. Directors may face civil claims for breach of fiduciary duty and corporate losses. Mere approval of a defensible transfer-pricing policy does not ordinarily create personal tax liability.
Sham partnership, nominee structure, or beneficial-ownership abuse

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization of the arrangement, denial of treaty benefits, application of domestic withholding rates, reassessment of income and penalties, general anti-avoidance rule analysis, and possible partnership or corporate-law remedies. CRA may disregard labels and apply the legal and commercial substance of the transactions.

Director personal liability: The corporate general partner and responsible individuals may face civil restitution, breach-of-duty claims, fraudulent conveyance consequences, and criminal liability where documents are knowingly fabricated. Limited liability may be lost for a partner that exceeds statutory limits, gives guarantees, commits fraud, or participates in management contrary to the governing partnership statute.
Permanent establishment and unregistered Canadian business

Statutory sanction: Canadian corporate or partnership filing obligations, attribution of Canadian business profits, tax, interest, penalties, payroll and indirect-tax obligations, and potential failure-to-register consequences. Treaty protection depends on the actual permanent-establishment facts and profit attribution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may incur personal liability for unpaid source deductions and may be liable for knowingly false residency or permanent-establishment representations. Criminal exposure requires proof of the relevant offence; ordinary disagreement over treaty interpretation is not by itself criminal conduct.
Customs, sanctions, export-control, and trade-document fraud

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, denial of import or export privileges, customs reassessments, prosecution under Canadian customs, sanctions, and export-control legislation, substantial fines, and imprisonment depending on the offence.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, brokers, and compliance personnel may be personally prosecuted where they authorize, direct, or knowingly facilitate the conduct. Corporate indemnification may be unavailable for fines or criminal penalties. Civil restitution and asset forfeiture may apply; travel restrictions can be imposed by a court or arise from detention, bail, or probation conditions.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: Suspicious transaction reporting, recordkeeping and client-identification failures may lead to FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, enforcement disclosure, account restrictions, and prosecution. Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime offences may result in major fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal liability for counselling, aiding, abetting, or directing money laundering. Corporate status does not protect individuals who knowingly participate. Courts may order restitution, forfeiture, or release conditions that restrict travel; travel prohibition is fact-specific and not automatic.
Improper partner allocations and loss trafficking

Statutory sanction: Denial of allocations, restriction of losses, reassessment of partners, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and potential application of anti-avoidance or acquisition-of-control rules. Artificial allocations may also invalidate tax reporting and trigger information-return penalties.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly approve fabricated allocations may face civil claims by partners, director or officer liability, and criminal exposure for fraudulent tax reporting. Limited partners can be liable for their own false filings even when the partnership itself is fiscally transparent.
Corporate and partnership governance failure

Statutory sanction: Loss of limited-liability protection under applicable British Columbia partnership law, contract invalidity, civil damages, dissolution or court remedies, tax reassessment, and inability to substantiate deductions or partner allocations.

Director personal liability: A general partner bears primary partnership liability. Directors of a corporate general partner may be personally exposed for statutory source deductions, fraud, torts, breach of fiduciary duty, and knowing participation in unlawful conduct. Courts may order civil restitution and asset recovery; imprisonment requires a criminal conviction.
Recordkeeping and audit obstruction

Statutory sanction: Document-production orders, reassessment based on available evidence, denial of deductions, administrative penalties, audit-extension consequences, contempt proceedings, and criminal penalties for obstruction or destruction of evidence.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for obstruction or destruction of records and may face civil restitution for resulting losses. Courts may impose detention, probation, or travel conditions in criminal proceedings; a general tax audit does not automatically result in a travel ban.
