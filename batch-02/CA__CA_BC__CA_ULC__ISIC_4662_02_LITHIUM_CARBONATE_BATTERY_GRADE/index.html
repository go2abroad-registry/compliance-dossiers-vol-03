Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V when battery-grade lithium carbonate powder is exported by the supplier or recipient to a destination outside Canada, is delivered outside Canada at the recipient's direction, and title and risk of loss pass to a non-resident. The supplier must maintain contemporaneous evidence that the goods left Canada in a commercial export and that the sale was made to a non-resident for use outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration filed through CBSA Integrated Declaration System / Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS)
  • Commercial invoice and signed purchase contract identifying the non-resident buyer and export Incoterms
  • Bill of Lading or airway waybill evidencing foreign destination and actual shipment out of Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate and/or bank wire confirmation evidencing foreign currency repatriation into Canada within the prescribed deadline
  • Carrier certificates, customs broker export records, and warehouse/delivery receipts supporting the movement outside Canada

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamp, or API-based clearance mandate is currently in force in Canada. Registered persons whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD $300,000 must file GST/HST returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE; e-invoicing via CRA-supported PEPPOL is voluntary.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period, or without limitation where misrepresentation, fraud, or willful default is present, deny zero-rating retroactively, and claw back net tax refunds with interest. Gross-negligence penalties under Excise Tax Act section 285 can reach 50% of the improperly claimed amount; criminal prosecution under section 327 may result in fines and imprisonment. Late FX repatriation beyond 180 days, undocumented or recycled export reports, and non-arm's-length diversion schemes will attract enhanced audit scrutiny and retrospective refusal of refunds.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar ('CAD' or 'C$') is a freely convertible currency under a floating exchange-rate regime. Canada maintains no material restrictions on current-account transactions (trade in goods and services) or capital-account flows. The Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) establishes legal tender; exchange-rate policy is delegated to the Bank of Canada. Profit remittance from a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Company to non-resident shareholders is subject solely to statutory withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) (e.g., 25% default), and is generally not constrained by convertibility or exchange-control approval. There is no requirement to obtain central bank permission for investment repatriation or dividend remittance; however, all cash remittances exceeding the prescribed threshold must be reported to FINTRAC and are reviewable for compliance with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no prescribed timeline for repatriation or mandatory conversion into CAD. A resident corporation is free to hold export receipts in any currency in non-resident or resident bank accounts. Nevertheless, for regulatory clarity: (i) receipts exceeding C$10,000 in a single or linked transaction must be reported to FINTRAC; (ii) all cross-border transactions require a 'purpose code' designation in wire-transfer instructions, ordinarily 'S10' (trade in goods) for export payments; (iii) income tax filing obligations require accrual of foreign-currency amounts at the relevant exchange rate under the Income Tax Act, and any realized foreign-exchange gain or loss is included in business income.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the monetary authority; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the anti-money laundering supervisory body for cross-border fund flows; no separate foreign exchange control administration exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium, with material legal constraints. Canada does not recognise the 'electronic money institution' (EMI) licence. Global fintech platforms (e.g., Wise, Revolut Business) that hold EMI licences in the EEA or MSB licences in Canada may provide multi-currency settlement and foreign-exchange services; however, for wholesale commodity procurement they are not a substitute for a full-service clearing bank because: (i) the service provider must be registered under the PCMLTFA as a money services business (MSB), and may be subject to restrictions on accepting deposits or issuing Canadian-dollar 'deposit-like' instruments; (ii) funds held at non-deposit-taking institutions are not insured by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) and are exposed to counterparty risk in the event of insolvency; (iii) each Canadian-resident corporate customer is required to maintain a broad correspondent-banking relationship for trade-finance instruments (documentary credits, avalised bills) which EMIs typically do not support. Deployment is therefore confined to ancillary expense payments and foreign-currency collections, not for primary capital escrow or high-value trade settlement.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.00% combined federal and British Columbia general corporate rate (federal 15% after abatement and general rate reduction; BC 12%). A BC ULC controlled by non-residents is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation and therefore does not qualify for the small business deduction.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, imposed under ITA Part XIII section 212(2), absent treaty relief.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions triggers a T106 information return. There is no separate statutory Master File/Local File threshold in Canada, but CRA expects comprehensive TP documentation for all controlled transactions; the transfer pricing penalty does not apply where the aggregate adjustment does not exceed the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue and reasonable efforts to document and apply arm's-length pricing are established.

Economic substance scope: British Columbia does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to certain offshore centres, but Canadian law embeds substance requirements in the General Anti-Avoidance Rule at ITA section 245 and in transfer pricing rules at ITA section 247. The BC ULC must perform real procurement, trading, inventory risk, decision-making, and administrative functions in Canada; a passive interposition with no economic activity, no employees, and no management substance can cause CRA to recharacterize a distribution, deny treaty benefits, or reallocate income to the beneficial owner.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Returns

Statutory sanction: Under ITA section 239(1), a person convicted of tax evasion is liable to a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for up to 5 years. BC provincial penalties may be assessed in parallel, plus arrears interest and gross negity civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in or authorize evasion can be charged as parties to the offence, exposed to restitution orders, subjected to restraining orders and travel bans arising from criminal proceedings, and imprisoned. CRA may also disregard corporate separateness and pierce the veil to collect civil restitution from directors where the corporation is a mere façade.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize transactions, compute arm's-length prices under ITA section 247(2), impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue, and apply interest plus secondary penalties for gross negligence.

Director personal liability: Directors who supervise the preparation of false TP documentation or sign a misleading T2 can be held jointly liable under the reasonable-diligence test, subjected to civil assessments on a piercing-of-the-veil basis, and prosecuted if fraud is proven; travel restrictions may be imposed after criminal charges are laid.
Shell Company Artificiality and GAAR

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule to deny the claimed tax benefit, reassess the ULC on profits earned with a 25% gross-up penalty under ITA sections 245 and 160/163, and disallow treaty benefits through a Limitation on Benefits analysis.

Director personal liability: Directors who design or approve artificial interposition can be named in civil fraud and negligence actions, ordered to make restitution, have passports or travel documents restricted while under investigation, and face imprisonment for participating in a sham or fraudulent conveyance.
Anti-Money Laundering and Financial Offences

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties can reach CAD 1,000,000 or more for corporations; under Criminal Code section 462.31, laundering proceeds of crime is an indictable offence punishable by imprisonment for up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct or knowingly assist in money laundering are personally exposed to criminal fines and imprisonment, civil confiscation of assets, interprovincial and international travel bans pending prosecution, and personal liability for corporate penalties where the entity is a nominee or sham.
