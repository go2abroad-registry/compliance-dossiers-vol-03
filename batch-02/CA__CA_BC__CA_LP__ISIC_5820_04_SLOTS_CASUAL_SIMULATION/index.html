Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — For Canadian income tax purposes, the BC Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under Income Tax Act (ITA) s. 96: no entity-level federal or provincial income tax is imposed on the LP; each partner (individual, corporation, trust, or other partnership; resident or non-resident) reports its allocated share of income or loss in accordance with the partnership agreement and applicable income-splitting rules. Territorial scope: a BC LP with central management and control in Canada is a Canadian-resident partnership whose worldwide income is attributed to partners; non-resident partners are taxable under Part I (s. 2(3)) only on Canadian-source income, including business carried on in Canada and (depending on treaty) service income; a non-resident partner's share of a Canadian LP's business income is generally subject to Canadian tax based on the LP's permanent establishment in Canada and may also attract Part XIII withholding on certain distributions characterized as dividends/interest/rents/royalties. For GST/HST purposes, the LP is itself a separate 'person' (Excise Tax Act s. 123) and may be a registrant independent of its partners. BC PST applies at the retail point-of-sale and is not an entity-level tax.

Available election forms

Canada does not have a US-style check-the-box (Form 8832) election under domestic law; the LP's partnership status arises from the BC Registration and ITA s. 96 and is upheld through consistent documentation and filing. Relevant mechanisms include: (i) for US federal purposes, the Canadian LP may file IRS Form 8832 to elect corporate or partnership classification, which may create hybrid / reverse-hybrid mismatches subject to Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA s. 18(8), s. 212.3, and s. 247 for cross-border arrangements); (ii) election under ETA s. 273 for closely related corporations and partnership members to simplify GST/HST self-supply treatment; (iii) joint elections under ITA s. 96(8) regarding inclusion of partnership property at cost; (iv) under the digital-economy platform rules, a platform operator can elect not to be the deemed supplier if it does not control key elements (payment, delivery, or terms), thereby shifting GST/HST liability to the LP as the underlying seller; (v) BC PST does not provide elections, but exemptions certificates may be issued to qualified purchasers.

CAD 30,000 — the 'small supplier' threshold under ETA s. 148(1): a BC resident LP must register for GST/HST once taxable worldwide supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in any four consecutive calendar quarters; for non-resident suppliers of 'specified digital services' (downloadable software, in-app virtual goods, online gaming content) supplied to Canadian-resident consumers, registration is mandatory once revenues from Canadian customers exceed CAD 30,000 in a 12-month period (digital economy measures effective July 1, 2021). Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating (0% GST) is available under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. For tangible goods: exports are zero-rated where the goods are exported under customs authority permission within 60 days (or as permitted) and documentary evidence of export is retained. For digital services and intangible personal property (downloadable social-casino games, virtual slots, in-game virtual currency, licensing rights, platform access), a supply made to a non-resident recipient who is not in Canada at the time the supply is made is zero-rated unless the supply is excluded because it is in respect of real property situated in Canada, is a service performed on tangible personal property for a Canadian consumer, or the supply is a right/license limited primarily to use in Canada (ETA Sch. VI s. 7, s. 10). For the casual-simulation gaming model, the LP's sales to non-Canadian end-users through app stores are zero-rated exports of intangible digital property; the LP must maintain non-residency evidence, such as the customer's billing/storefront country, IP geolocation, device region, and payment instrument country. The LP's supply of its games to the Apple/Google platform operator (as deemed supplier) is also zero-rated as an export of intangible digital property to a non-resident when title/use occurs outside Canada. BC PST does not apply to exported digital property, and no PST is charged on sales to non-resident purchasers. Unclaimed input tax credits on Canadian GST-inclusive costs (hosting, development tools, Canadian marketing) can be recovered via the LP's GST/HST net-filing or by claiming a refund under ETA ss. 252/261 as applicable.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates an open-account regime with full convertibility of the Canadian dollar on both current-account and capital-account transactions. Neither the Bank of Canada nor any federal or provincial authority requires prior approval for inbound foreign investment, debt remittance, software-export proceeds, or the repatriation of limited-partner earnings. The CAD floats freely and there are no exchange-rate bands, surrender duties, or deposit/conversion quotas. Profit-remittance barriers are purely fiscal: (i) a non-resident limited partner is subject to Canadian income tax on allocated Canadian-source business income and must normally file a Canadian return or make a section 216 election to report the BC partnership income on a net basis; (ii) payments to non-residents of interest, royalties, or management fees are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at 25% unless a treaty or an exemption reduces the rate; (iii) returns of capital and dispositions of LP interests are not creditable capital distributions for withholding purposes but may attract Part XIII tax if the CRA recharacterizes the distribution as income. The Investment Canada Act may apply only to acquisition of control of a Canadian business, not to the operation of a social-casino publisher; no net-benefit review would be triggered at the expected capital levels.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for export-of-services receipts. The partnership may retain Apple App Store and Google Play settlement proceeds in CAD, USD, or any freely convertible foreign currency in its overseas or domestic accounts. Compliance duties are limited to (i) FINTRAC mandatory reporting of every single cross-border electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more, regardless of purpose code; (ii) accurate functional-currency calculations under subsection 261(2) of the Income Tax Act should the CRA allow CAD functional currency for designated foreign-currency business; and (iii) the partnership's bank contractual requirement to provide a written economic rationale, supported by platform payout statements, for any persistent foreign-currency balance exceeding liquidity needs.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for banking solvency and prudential supervision and with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for monetary reporting and cross-border wire surveillance. Canada maintains no dedicated exchange-control ministry; the Minister of Finance retains residual authority over payments-system legislation.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW for reliance on an offshore UK/EEA electronic money institution (EMI) as the primary treasury account because a foreign EMI cannot passport under the Canadian Bank Act, is not licensed to take Canadian deposits, and is ineligible for direct LVTS/ACSS clearing membership. Feasibility rating: MEDIUM as a secondary payout-only corridor, provided the EMI has registered with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and with FINTRAC as a money services business, and maintains a correspondent relationship with a Canadian Schedule I bank. Legal constraints: (i) the EMI must not hold or layer funds claiming to be deposits; account documentation must characterize balances as settlement funds; (ii) end-user safeguarding rules of the RPAA require segregation of Canadian end-user funds; (iii) the gaming-adjacent merchant profile will trigger enhanced due diligence and a 90-day enhanced monitoring period; and (iv) Apple IAP/Google Play Billing proceeds cannot be routed through the EMI because platform settlement rails are controlled by the app-store platforms and normally credit a designated corporate bank account chosen by the developer.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A British Columbia limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to Canadian income tax. Taxable income is allocated to partners. A Canadian-controlled private corporation generally has a combined federal and British Columbia rate of 11% on qualifying active business income within the applicable small-business limit and 27% at the general corporate rate. The federal general corporate rate is 15%; the British Columbia general provincial rate is 12%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. A partnership distribution is generally not a dividend; its Canadian and foreign tax treatment follows the nature of the underlying income and the partners' residence and tax status.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 is generally due by the last day of the fifth month after the partnership fiscal period ends. A partnership with a December 31 year-end generally files by May 31 of the following year. Each partner reports its allocated amount in the partner's applicable return. A corporation generally files its T2 within six months after its taxation year-end, with any balance generally due two months after year-end, or three months for eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting the statutory conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires reasonable efforts to determine and use arm's-length terms, and contemporaneous documentation must generally be prepared by the filing due date. Form T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 during the fiscal period. Canadian Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not use a separate statutory Master File and Local File regime identical to the OECD terminology, but CRA expects documentation broadly consistent with OECD concepts.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is assessed through transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, corporate-residence and central-management facts, partnership allocation rules, interest limitation rules, and specific anti-avoidance provisions. A Canadian LP should maintain genuine business activity, appropriate personnel or outsourced operational capability, commercial decision-making, books and records, functional control over software and marketing, documented risk assumption, and banking and contractual arrangements consistent with the claimed business model.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, reassessment, denial of deductions, interest, and collection enforcement. Criminal tax evasion may result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and controlling persons may face personal criminal liability where they participated in or directed the conduct. Directors may also face director-liability assessments for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST. Corporate limited liability does not protect fraud or criminal conduct. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from bail, court orders, or enforcement proceedings.
Unremitted payroll, GST/HST, or non-resident withholding

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, late-remittance penalties, failure-to-file penalties, gross-negligence penalties where applicable, and potential prosecution. Director liability can apply to source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally after statutory collection conditions are satisfied, including where the corporation failed to remit and collection from the corporation is unavailable. A general partner may have direct liability under partnership law and the partnership agreement. Imprisonment requires criminal prosecution and conviction; it is not an automatic consequence of an administrative assessment.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess income under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impose a transfer-pricing adjustment penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, and impose interest. Secondary adjustments, withholding-tax exposure, and treaty disputes may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face civil exposure for knowing participation in fraudulent records or breach of fiduciary duties. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessment does not automatically create personal tax liability or criminal liability absent intentional misconduct.
Artificial partnership allocation or sham arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize or disregard the arrangement, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, deny losses or deductions, reallocate income, assess withholding tax, and impose interest and penalties. Provincial tax consequences may also arise.

Director personal liability: General partners may have direct contractual and statutory liability. Persons who knowingly implement sham documentation may face civil claims, director or officer liability, and criminal charges for fraud or tax evasion.
Shell company, beneficial ownership, and economic substance failure

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include treaty-benefit denial, withholding at 25%, GAAR reassessment, denial of expenses, beneficial-ownership challenges, corporate registry compliance action, and suspicious-transaction reporting. There is no general Canadian offence solely for being a shell company, but associated fraud, tax evasion, or money laundering is criminal.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and beneficial owners may face personal civil and criminal exposure where they conceal ownership, launder proceeds, or direct false reporting. Court-ordered asset restraint, forfeiture, bail conditions, and travel restrictions may occur in criminal proceedings; they are not automatic consequences of a low-substance structure.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime violations

Statutory sanction: Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime offences can result in substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment under the Criminal Code and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties may apply to reporting entities for compliance failures.

Director personal liability: Individuals who participate, direct, counsel, or facilitate the conduct may be prosecuted personally. Conviction can result in imprisonment, criminal forfeiture, restitution, and court-ordered restrictions. Corporate limited liability does not protect knowing participants.
Unlicensed or misleading gaming activity

Statutory sanction: The business model may be subject to Criminal Code gaming provisions, provincial gaming laws, consumer-protection laws, advertising restrictions, app-store enforcement, payment blocking, fines, injunctions, and potential criminal prosecution. The stated absence of a statutory licence requirement must be validated against actual mechanics, users, prizes, jurisdiction, and monetization.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution where they authorize or knowingly permit unlawful gaming. Civil claims, regulatory orders, restitution, and asset seizure may follow. Imprisonment depends on the offence and conviction; travel bans are not automatic.
Intellectual-property and source-code compliance

Statutory sanction: Civil damages, injunctions, accounting of profits, statutory damages where available, delivery-up orders, contractual termination, tax deduction denial, and withholding or transfer-pricing reassessment may result. Wilful commercial infringement can create criminal exposure in limited circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for corporate infringement merely because they serve as directors, but personal liability may arise for authorization, inducing infringement, fraud, or breach of fiduciary duties.
Corporate registry and partnership governance failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, loss of good standing, cancellation or dissolution, inability to obtain certificates, enforcement against partners, and penalties under applicable British Columbia corporate, partnership, tax, and transparency legislation may apply.

Director personal liability: General partners may be personally liable for partnership obligations. Directors and officers may face statutory personal liability for specific remittances, fraud, oppression, or knowing contraventions. Dissolution does not extinguish tax debts or liabilities arising from misconduct.
