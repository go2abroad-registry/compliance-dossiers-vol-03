Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); online portal: CRA My Business Account (MyBA), Business Registration Online (BRO), and GST/HST NETFILE; customs and trade authorizations are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

E-government filing portal

A foreign limited partnership seeking to conduct investment or business activity in Canada must first determine its Canadian tax nexus. The partnership must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts' or by using the Business Registration Online service. If the partnership makes taxable supplies in Canada, a GST/HST account must be opened. The CRA will assign a federal BN and program accounts. For income tax purposes, a Canadian-resident or Canadian-carrying-on-business limited partnership files an information return (T5013) and each partner reports their allocation of income. The BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) account is registered separately through BC's eTax system, but the primary national tax identifier remains the CRA BN. Non-resident LPs must designate a representative for service in Canada if required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a check-the-box entity classification election. A limited partnership is statutorily treated as a flow-through partnership under the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, a partnership may be a registrant in its own right. No elective hybrid mismatch regime exists; Canadian hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss.18.2 and 18.3) operate mandatorily to deny deductions for certain payments to the extent of tax mismatch. Certain procedural elections, such as partnership fiscal-period elections, are available but do not affect entity classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual gross taxable supplies for the GST/HST small-supplier threshold; BC PST registration is required when a seller makes taxable sales or leases in British Columbia with no equivalent threshold exemption for non-residents engaged in commercial activity in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported from Canada are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, provided the exporter retains evidence of physical export and has no requirement to account for the goods in Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident are zero-rated under Part VI when the service is performed entirely outside Canada or the recipient's benefit is foreign in substance. Financial services supplied to a non-resident person who is not a GST/HST registrant may be zero-rated under Part VII of Schedule VI, subject to the 'in the course of' rule applicable to investment management and custody services for a non-resident single family office. All zero-rating claims require documentary substantiation and are subject to CRA audit.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or eManifest export transaction record)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill issued by the carrier evidencing shipment from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) issued by a Canadian financial institution or equivalent foreign bank settlement evidence (e.g., SWIFT MT103 or bank statement showing payment in convertible currency)

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory electronic invoicing regime. CRA accepts electronic invoices and records. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically by most registrants using GST/HST NETFILE or a service provider. Customs export declarations are submitted to CBSA through the Canadian Export Reporting System or the Automated Customs Declaration System; no digital stamping or blockchain fiscal validation is currently required.

CRA may reassess GST/HST within the normal four-year period, or longer in cases of misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default. A zero-rated export claim that lacks complete documentation will be reversed, with the registrant liable for GST/HST not remitted, arrears interest, and penalties. The gross negligence penalty is generally 50% of the understated net tax, and criminal tax evasion prosecution may result in significant fines and imprisonment for principals of the limited partnership. The 180-day documented repatriation deadline serves as a CRA administrative benchmark; absence of foreign exchange repatriation within that period will heighten audit scrutiny and may be treated as evidence of a domestic taxable benefit or potential anti-avoidance recharacterization.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a free-floating currency with full current- and capital-account convertibility, consistent with IMF Article VIII obligations since 1951. There are no statutory exchange-control approvals for capital injections, profit repatriation, or liquidation proceeds. The only constraints are indirect: (i) income tax compliance — non-resident partners of the BC LP are taxed under Part I of the Income Tax Act (via Canadian partnership return allocations) and may face Part XIII withholding at 25% (reduced by treaty) on certain distributions; and (ii) reporting thresholds under the Cross-border Currency and Monetary Instruments Reporting (CCMR) regime for physical cash/monetary instruments equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000. BC-specific corporate registry obligations require LP beneficial-ownership registers, but these do not restrict convertibility or remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada repealed the Exchange Control Act in 1951 and imposes no surrender, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds or any foreign receipts. The sole regulatory overlay is the PCMLTFA-based CCMR declaration to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for physical transit of currency/monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more, plus standard record-keeping and suspicious-transaction reporting to FINTRAC. For an SFO, no export proceeds arise; the relevant residual obligation concerns the reporting of large incoming international electronic funds transfers at the banks' discretion.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential banking regulation delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and AML/CTF supervision to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). For British Columbia, the BCFSA (British Columbia Financial Services Authority) oversees provincial financial-services conduct but holds no authority over federally chartered bank licensing or FX policy.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low-to-Medium. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise Business, Airwallex, Currencycloud resellers) are permissible for low-value operational payments and can hold USD subledgers, but they cannot provide: (i) regulated securities custody for portfolio holdings of an SFO; (ii) ISDA derivatives/hedging counterparty status; or (iii) prime-brokerage settlement. Under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), Canadian-destined PSPs must be registered with the Bank of Canada, and any institution taking Canadian client deposits must be authorized under the Bank Act (Schedule I or II). Legal-constraint summary: use an EMI strictly as a pre-funded disbursement rail for family-office expenses and international invoices, with daily sweeps to the Schedule I bank account; never hold LP committed capital in an uninsured EMI wallet.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident limited partners must remit via SWIFT MT103 using a Canadian Schedule I bank's US or overseas correspondent intermediary (e.g., RBC New York for USD). The instructed beneficiary must match the BC LP's name exactly as registered; the narrative line should read: 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION UNDER BC LP AGREEMENT DATED [DATE] — REF [LP account number]'. For USD wires to Canada, use beneficiary account type 'US Dollar Commercial Account' with a U.S. intermediary BIC; for CAD wires originating outside Canada, convert at the issuing bank or route via the Canadian bank's London/Frankfurt desk. FINTRAC cross-border reporting applies only to physical currency; no prior governmental authorization is needed for wire-based inflows. For amounts above CAD 250,000, submit a board-resolution extract and proof of source of funds to the receiving bank to avoid provisional holds.Domestic B2B trade settlements within Canada are cleared via Payments Canada: Lynx for high-value same-day CAD (Real-Time Gross Settlement) and the Real-Time Rail (RTR) for instant retail payments. For cross-border trade settlements, use SWIFT MT103 for open-account wire disbursements and SWIFT MT700 for documentary letters of credit (UCP600 governed), with MT202 on the cover side where applicable. For family-office acquisitions of foreign securities, settlement is channeled through the custodian's prime-brokerage network (Euroclear/Clearstream for EU, CNS/DTC for US) with funds wired via correspondent banking from the BC operational account. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the BC limited partnership level (fiscally transparent pass-through). Income/loss flows to partners; corporate partners resident in BC pay 27% aggregate (15% federal + 12% BC provincial corporate rate). Individual partners pay at graduated marginal rates.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% (Part XIII) on Canadian-source income allocations/distributions made to non-resident partners; reduced treaty rates apply (e.g., 15% for direct corporate partners under Canada-US DTA when holding at least 10% of voting shares; 5% for certain US parent corporations; no Canadian withholding on return-of-capital or partnership loan repayments).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Due no later than five months after the partnership's fiscal period end (e.g., December 31 year-end => May 31 filing deadline). Each partner must receive their T5013 slip by the same five-month deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 50,000,000 gross annual revenue (for the fiscal year) triggers mandatory Local File documentation under new Canadian TP rules (effective for fiscal years beginning after 31 December 2022). Where the partnership's consolidated group revenue exceeds CAD 1,000,000,000, a Master File is also required. For low-revenue family-office LPs, no mandatory documentation is required, but a contemporaneous TP memorandum is strongly advised if any intercompany transactions exist (management fees, guarantee fees, loans among family trusts corporations and the LP, cost-sharing of the SFO's investment staff and treasury functions).

Economic substance scope: Canada applies a GAAR-based economic-substance doctrine (s.245 of the Income Tax Act) plus the common-law 'substance over form' rule. For a BC LP-SFO, economic substance requires real investment-management activity conducted in BC: annual partner meetings, board/minutes of the general partner, maintenance of books and records in BC, investment discretion exercised by BC-based managers, and bank/custody accounts in the name of the LP. A pure 'mailbox' LP with a foreign single-family office controlling all investment decisions is vulnerable to: (1) GAAR recharacterization of income to the actual controlling partner; (2) deemed establishment of a Canadian permanent establishment for a foreign partner (attracting Part I tax on a net basis); and (3) application of Part XIII withholding on close to the full amount of income. BC also imposes the provincial capital tax on financial institutions only; no ESR statute applies to SFOs in Canada, but FINTRAC MSB registration and CRS obligations create substance-like compliance duties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion — Wilful Omission of Investment Income

Statutory sanction: Criminal liability under s.239(1) Income Tax Act: fine of 50%–200% of the tax evaded (minimum CAD 100,000 for large corporations; CAD 10,000 for individuals) and imprisonment up to 5 years; civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2): 50% of the understated tax; s.163(1): 10% repeated-failure penalty on undeclared income over CAD 500

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for tax withheld but not remitted (including Part XIII), plus interest and penalties — no essential 'reasonable care' defence where the director signed the T5013 knowing it false; fraudulent transfers to family members trigger s.160 joint-and-several liability against the transferee
Failure to File Information Returns (T5013, T1135, CRS)

Statutory sanction: T5013: CAD 25/day/partner (max CAD 2,500/partner; aggregate cap CAD 25,000 — cap raised to CAD 50,000 for later fiscal periods); T1135: CAD 25 per day (min CAD 100, max CAD 2,500 for 24 months) and an additional CAD 2,500 for gross negligence plus 5% of the cost amount of the foreign property (no maximum); CRS: CAD 2,500 per failure plus CAD 250 per occurrence

Director personal liability: The general-partner board is personally exposed where it authorized the non-filing; the CRA routinely assesses directors under s.227.1 for T5013 penalties and, for repeated behaviour, applies criminal sanctions under s.238 (imprisonment up to 2 years) on individuals
Non-Resident Part XIII Withholding Defaults

Statutory sanction: s.215(2): the partnership becomes absolutely liable for the full un-withheld 25% tax plus 50% gross-negligence penalty under s.227(9) where deliberate; daily compound interest at CRA's prescribed rate on all outstanding amounts; late-remittance penalty of 10% of unremitted amounts over 3 days overdue (additional 20% above CAD 1,000,000)

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner are personally liable for the full Part XIII deficiency with no minimum threshold (s.227.1(1)) — the due-diligence defense is unavailable where the director signed a management agreement delegating withholding oversight or where the withholding was consciously omitted to preserve a distribution; CRA travel bans can be issued against directors where unpaid arrears exceed CAD 50,000 (s.226 EIPA)
Shell-Like Substance Failure / GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR (s.245) — the tax benefit is denied and the income recharacterized; an additional penalty of 25%–50% of the tax benefit (s.163(7) for transactions disclosed/undisclosed); reassessment periods are extended by 3 years where GAAR specifically applies; foreign partner's share of income may be re-attributed to the foreign parent and taxed under Part I (net basis) with Part XIII on the distribution if the LP is disregarded for Canadian tax purposes

Director personal liability: Under s.237.1, a promoter/director of a 'tax shelter' arrangement not registered with the CRA is liable to a penalty of CAD 500 – 100,000 per plan; directors of the corporate general partner face personal Gross Negligence penalties of 50% where the GAAR transaction lacked commercial substance and the director signed the return with knowledge of the misstatement
AML/CFT Violations (FINTRAC) and Unlicensed Money Services

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA (2019 AML amendments): administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for entities (CAD 500,000 for individuals); criminal fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for wilful non-registration; asset seizure and freezing orders under the Criminal Code

Director personal liability: Directors/officers who directed, authorized, or assented to the MSB non-compliance are personally liable under the PCMLTFA (corporate veil-piercing for AML violations); a successful FINTRAC penalty leads to a conventional s.227.1 tax assessment where the underlying transaction generated unreported income; Bank Act and securities regulators additionally impose lifetime prohibition orders against directors of regulated financial entities
