Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — Business Registration Online (BRO); BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch, for British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST).

E-government filing portal

Apply online via CRA BRO or Form RC1 to procure the partnership Business Number (BN); the GST/HST account program identifier is 'RT'. Registration is mandatory when the LP carries on business in Canada and exceeds CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies; non-resident limited partners must obtain their own Canadian identification and may file treaty-based position Form NR3016. For BC PST, register separately with the BC Consumer Taxation Branch if taxable sales to BC consumers occur. Electronic BRO registration is immediate for most entities; RC1 processing takes about 20 business days.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — the BC limited partnership is fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act (ITA s. 96: each partner is taxed on allocable income/loss), but the partnership is a separate 'person' for GST/HST purposes (ETA s. 123). Non-resident limited partners are subject to Part I tax on Canadian-source business income and, absent a waiver, Part XIII withholding under ITA s. 194.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election exists in Canada. Elective and statutory mechanisms: GST/HST Quick Method election (ETA s. 197); annual-filing election (ETA s. 238); NR3016 treaty-benefit position for non-resident partners; Canada–US Income Tax Convention Article IV(7) fiscally-transparent entity treatment. OECD BEPS Action 2 hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4–18.6) apply non-electively to cross-border payments involving hybrid entities/instruments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in total worldwide taxable supplies for the small-supplier test; for non-resident suppliers of digital services, CAD 30,000 in specified digital supplies to Canadian consumers over the preceding four calendar quarters. BC PST: no turnover-based threshold; mandatory registration for any seller with taxable sales in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Digital web-novel subscription supplies made by the Canadian LP to non-resident recipients are zero-rated under Part V, Schedule VI, ETA (s. 7 for services to non-residents; s. 23 for intangible personal property supplied for exclusive use outside Canada). CRA may treat Apple IAP and Google Play as distribution-platform co-suppliers; supplies to Canadian consumers remain taxable at 5% GST, plus PST where applicable.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) — required only for physical media exports; not applicable to pure digital downloads.
  • Bill of Lading/airway bill — applicable only where tangible cargo crosses the border; substitute digital delivery and geolocation logs for subscription downloads.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/PSP settlement report evidencing foreign-currency receipt (e.g., Apple IAP or Google Play payout statements, SWIFT/ACH entries).
  • Supply contract stating chosen law, delivery mechanism, and non-resident status of the recipient.
  • IP address geolocation records and subscriber residency declarations demonstrating foreign use.

No real-time fiscal clearance, API-based posting, or digital stamping mandate exists in Canada. Electronic filing of GST/HST returns is mandatory where annual taxable revenues exceed CAD 1,000,000. GST/HST invoices must include prescribed content (supplier BN, date, description, tax rate/amount). The pan-Canadian Peppol e-invoicing framework remains voluntary.

CRA may reassess beyond the four-year limitation period in cases of misrepresentation or fraud, retrospectively reversing export zero-rating and assessing uncollected GST/HST, prescribed interest (T-bill plus 4%), and penalties: ETA s. 285 gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the amount and s. 280 daily interest. Wilful evasion attracts fines of 50%–200% of the taxes sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years under ETA s. 327; GAAR may apply under ITA s. 245.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada enforces no foreign exchange control regime under the Currency Act; the Canadian dollar is fully convertible for residents and non-residents on both current and capital account transactions. Profit remittances, dividends, partner distributions, royalty payments, and repayment of intercompany or shareholder loans are not subject to quantitative FX limitations. The only frictions are standard tax withholding on non-resident payments and FATF/FINTRAC reporting obligations for large/out-of-pattern transactions. For the BC Limited Partnership (CA_LP), capital injection and repatriation can freely occur through chartered banks, money service businesses, and authorized FX dealers, subject only to the bank's own KYC/AML onboarding and tax declaration requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender rules or mandatory conversion ratios exist in Canada. Export receipts and digital platform revenues (e.g., membership subscriptions from Apple IAP and Google Play Billing processed in foreign currencies) may be retained in US dollars, euros, or any convertible currency, or repatriated to CAD at the entity's discretion. These proceeds must be recorded for income tax, GST/HST, and Form T5013 partnership reporting purposes, but no counterparty remittance to the Bank of Canada is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank supervision and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering compliance

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore fintech and electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Revolut, Airwallex, or Currencycloud can be selectively used as supplementary disbursement rails for international author royalties and as virtual IBAN collection points for foreign app-store proceeds. However, they cannot replace a Canadian Schedule I chartered bank for CAD settlement, regulatory agility, or statutory escrow capacity. Any EMI operating in Canada or soliciting Canadian clients must be registered as a money service business (MSB) with FINTRAC; unregistered foreign EMIs have no direct access to Canadian clearing infrastructure (Lynx/ACSS). Legal constraints include lack of CDIC deposit insurance, restricted ability to hold actual CAD settlement balances, and a heightened risk of frozen funds if a compliant Canadian correspondent bank demands additional KYC evidence. Therefore the treasury blueprint should maintain the primary operating account at a Tier 1 Canadian bank and use contractual or agent-of-record EMI integrations only for foreign-currency segregation and low-value bulk payouts below the FINTRAC large-transaction threshold.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate (15% federal Part I rate under Income Tax Act (Canada), s. 123, plus 12% British Columbia provincial rate under the BC Income Tax Act; the combined rate applying where the corporate general partner’s share of British Columbia LP income exceeds the small business deduction threshold).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under ITA Part XIII on dividends or dividend-equivalent distributions paid to a non-resident of Canada. In the BC LP structure, because the LP is fiscally transparent, a distribution to a non-resident limited partner is first sourced as the partner’s allocable Part I Canadian business income; any portion characterized as Canadian-source dividend or interest flowing through the LP is subject to the 25% Part XIII gross rate unless the DTA reduces it.

T2 return: within 6 months after the corporation’s fiscal year-end. Balance-due date: 2 months after year-end generally; 3 months after year-end for a CCPC claiming the SBD. T5013 for the BC LP: where the fiscal period ends on December 31, due March 31 of the following calendar year; for fiscal periods ended after December 31, due by the last day of the fifth month after fiscal-period end.

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a fiscal period ending December 31, the T5013 is due March 31 of the immediately following calendar year. For fiscal periods ending after December 31, the return is due by the last day of the fifth month after the end of the fiscal period.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CA$1,000,000 aggregate annual value of non-arm’s-length cross-border transactions is the threshold above which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File/Master File equivalent) is effectively required and the s. 247(3) penalty regime applies; failure to maintain such documentation creates a rebuttable presumption that no reasonable effort was made to establish arm’s-length terms.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no codified Economic Substance Regulations comparable to offshore ESR regimes; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245) and the statutory definition of a partnership require that the BC LP be genuinely carried on as a separate partnership business. CRA will recharacterize a bare flow-through conduit as a taxable corporate enterprise where the LP lacks real Canadian premises, employees, decision-making, and commercial operations. The OECD GAAR/Principal Purpose Test under the MLI further bars treaty relief for structure lacking economic substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s. 239(1), evasion or wilful commission/omission is punishable by a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Under s. 163(2), a gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of the understated tax is assessable; interest compounds daily; BC provincial penalties mirror the federal statutory framework.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under ITA s. 227.1 for unpaid corporate taxes, Part XIII withholdings, and GST/HST. The CRA may issue a Director Liability Notice, register liens on personal assets, freeze bank accounts, and restrict international travel through the Global Tax Enforcement program; criminal conviction may impose personal fines and up to 5 years of imprisonment on the directing mind.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 247(3) penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the net understatement exceeds the greater of CA$5,000 or 10% of gross revenue; the mispriced payment is disallowed as a deduction and added to Canadian taxable income with full compound interest; double-tax exposure arises if the foreign recipient is denied corresponding relief.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorized the non-arm’s-length payments are jointly liable for the penalty once it is assessed against the corporate general partner under s. 227.1; civil recovery can extend to the directors’ personal holdings; a pattern of artificial pricing may constitute fraud-based tortious restitution claims by the CRA in Federal Court and can trigger director prohibition orders.
Shell Company / Sham Partnership Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245) to disregard the LP, reallocate the web fiction subscription revenue to the corporate general partner or the Canadian source, assess 27% combined corporate tax on the full profit, add a 50% gross-negligence penalty, and deny treaty benefits under the Principle Purpose Test of the MLI/Canada-US DTA.

Director personal liability: Under the BC Partnership Act, a limited partner who participates in control or management of the limited partnership loses limited liability and becomes jointly and severally liable for the LP’s debts, including tax liabilities; directors of a sham corporate general partner risk civil restitution, seizure of personal assets, travel bans via outstanding federal debt certificates, and personal prosecution for participating in an arrangement that falsely shields Canadian-source income.
Failure to Remit Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 227(8) penalty of 10% of the amount not remitted, plus the full 25% withholding amount, plus interest at prescribed rates compounded daily; criminal prosecution under s. 238(1) for wilful failure to deduct may add fines of up to 100% of the evaded amount and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are personally liable for the unremitted Part XIII withholdings and penalties under ITA s. 227.1; CRA has the power to issue a Director Liability Notice without judicial pre-approval in certain cases, to garnish directors’ salaries, to register a lien over personal residences, and to issue travel bans where the director leaves Canada with unpaid tax debts.
Sanctions / AML Payment-Rails Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and the Special Economic Measures Act: administrative monetary penalties up to CA$2 million per violation for failure to report or maintain records; obstruction or false reporting may lead to imprisonment up to 5 years; platform-side agreements with Apple and Google may be terminated, causing business interruption and secondary tax assessments for unreported revenue.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct, authorize, or acquiesce to transactions with sanctioned individuals face personal liability for civil penalties, disqualification from corporate directorship under Canadian corporate law, and criminal prosecution; travel to jurisdictions enforcing the sanctions regime may result in extradition proceedings; the corporate veil is pierced where the LP is found to be the alter ego of the director.
