Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - federal income tax and GST administration; official portals: Business Registration Online (BRO), CRA My Business Account, and GST/HST NETFILE. Provincial registration: BC Ministry of Finance, Provincial Sales Tax (PST) Administration via the Taxpayer Self-Service portal (BCeID), and the BC Corporate Registry for incorporation records.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Incorporate the ULC under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia) through the BC Corporate Registry, obtaining the certificate of incorporation and incorporation number; complete any extraprovincial registration if assets or premises are located in another province. Step 2 - Apply to the CRA for the nine-digit Business Number (BN) and the program accounts RC (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll), and RZ (import/export) by filing Form RC1 or online via Business Registration Online (BRO); the BN application must be made within 21 days of the ULC becoming liable to corporate income tax, and the RT account is mandatory immediately upon opening a retail grocery location in BC. Step 3 - Register as a PST vendor with the BC Ministry of Finance using a BCeID account through the Taxpayer Self-Service portal; a vendor with a place of business in BC and making taxable retail sales must hold a PST number before the first taxable sale. Step 4 - Complete ancillary registrations: municipal business licence for the grocery storefront, WorkSafeBC employer account, and the CBSA importer/exporter program account (attached to the RZ program account) for customs clearance of imported Asian condiments and vegetables. A non-resident-owned ULC is treated identically provided it maintains a registered and records office in BC; BC law does not require a Canadian resident director for a ULC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - For Canadian domestic income tax, the BC ULC is a resident corporation taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act on worldwide income at entity level, including BC provincial corporate income tax on the BC permanent establishment. However, the ULC is eligible for transparent treatment at the foreign shareholder level: a US shareholder may file Form 8832 to check the box and treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership, and the CRA administratively applies the fiscally transparent entity rule under Article IV(7) of the Canada-US Tax Convention to avoid double taxation. For GST and PST purposes, the ULC is a single juridical person and a mandatory registrant with no pass-through of indirect tax obligations.

Available election forms

US federal: IRS Form 8832 entity classification election, supported by the Canada-US Tax Convention Article IV(7) fiscally transparent entity allocation; CRA administrative guidance (Income Tax Technical News No. 22 and subsequent policy) permits a ULC to be treated as conduit-like for treaty purposes while remaining subject to Canadian Part I tax. Domestic indirect tax: election to register for GST/HST before exceeding the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold under ETA section 241(2.1); election under ETA section 221.1 for annual GST/HST reporting for large registrants; PST limited-vendor registration for out-of-province sellers attending trade shows or fairs in BC. Hybrid mismatch: proposed Income Tax Act sections 18.2 to 18.6 impose hybrid-mismatch adjustments where a US-disregarded ULC participates in hybrid transfers or hybrid entities; the ULC must self-disclose the election and maintain documentation proving dual-consistency of deduction and inclusion outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.05%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: C$30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (small-supplier threshold); once exceeded, registration is mandatory and the registrant must charge and remit 5% GST. PST: no revenue threshold; a supplier with a place of business in BC that sells taxable goods must register as a PST vendor before making sales, and a grocery retailer is therefore required to hold both GST and PST registrations concurrently.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: a supply by way of sale is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 1 of the Excise Tax Act where the ULC, as supplier, sends or causes the goods to be sent by common carrier, courier, or mail to a destination outside Canada. This covers both business-to-consumer export of imported Asian condiments and vegetables to US customers and business-to-business export to a US retail buyer. CRA practice requires the goods to be exported within a reasonable window, generally within 60 days of the supply, and the goods must not be diverted back into the Canadian domestic market. Services: procurement and sourcing services performed for a non-resident business qualify for zero-rating under Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 where the service is not performed in respect of Canadian real estate or tangible personal property situated in Canada and the non-resident is not a deemed-resident GST registrant. Domestic retail nuance: basic groceries, including vegetables, rice, soy sauce, fish sauce, and other staple condiments, are GST zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part III and generally exempt from BC PST; prepared meals, confectionery, snack foods, and carbonated beverages remain taxable at 5% GST plus 7% PST where applicable. Refund mechanics: a zero-rated exporter recovers embedded GST through the ordinary input tax credit system, generating a net refund when credits exceed tax collected on exempt or taxable supplies.

  • Customs export declaration lodged with the CBSA: Form B13A Export Declaration or approved electronic filing through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) with ACI eManifest data for the departing conveyance.
  • Commercial invoice in English with line-item detail, Incoterms, value in Canadian and foreign currency, and the zero-rating reason code (ETA reason code 25 - Export, with exemption code E where applicable).
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or courier waybill naming the foreign consignee and showing delivery to a destination outside Canada.
  • Proof of physical export and receipt by the foreign buyer: foreign customs entry, delivery confirmation, or signed proof of delivery evidencing departure from Canada within the 60-day export window.
  • Bank remittance records: Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign-currency conversion advice confirming settlement of the export sale in foreign currency.
  • Underlying import records for the Asian grocery goods: CBSA assessment, Form B3/BN-3, customs duty and GST paid at importation, to substantiate cost, origin, and supply chain integrity.
  • Export policy document and destination-mapping procedure retained in the ULC tax records for the statutory six-year retention period.

No mandatory e-invoicing, real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada or British Columbia. Invoices may be issued electronically (PDF, XML, or EDI) provided they contain the prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act and remain legible and accessible for the six-year retention period. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or through a third-party service provider) by virtually all registrants, with remittances made through the CRA electronic payment portal. PST returns are filed electronically through the BC Taxpayer Self-Service portal; no third-party invoice clearance or fiscal stamp is required.

The CRA conducts targeted export verification for grocery importers and will reassess any supply zero-rated without adequate export proof at 5% GST, plus interest at the prescribed rate, within the normal four-year reassessment window, extended to seven years where neglect, misrepresentation, or fraud is established under ETA section 298. A gross-negligence penalty under ETA section 285 applies to false statements or omissions, equal to the greater of C$250 and 25% of the tax understated or falsely claimed. Additional exposure includes failure-to-file and failure-to-remit penalties, and criminal prosecution for knowing evasion with fines up to C$100,000 and imprisonment for up to two years. The BC Ministry of Finance may assess unremitted PST at 7% with statutory interest and penalties on diverted goods. Clawback scenarios: goods zero-rated as exports that are returned to Canada or diverted to BC retail customers without a compliant customs trail are treated as never having been exported, triggering a GST assessment on the deemed domestic supply, a PST assessment on the BC retail value, and joint and several liability for any intermediary that facilitated the false export claim. The Voluntary Disclosures Program permits self-correction before the CRA initiates contact, with penalty relief generally available; however, large corporations face a reduced 50% penalty-relief benefit, and no relief is available where the disclosure is made after an audit demand or where the taxpayer knew that an evasion scheme was in progress. No statutory foreign-currency repatriation deadline exists under Canadian law; the 180-day benchmark is adopted as an internal compliance measure so that export receipts can be affirmatively matched to the B13A declarations, bank remittances, and the zero-rated GST lines in the ULC general ledger.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalised, freely floating Canadian dollar and has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations; there are no restrictions on current-account or capital-account conversion, no prior-approval regime, no surrender requirements, and no restrictions on foreign currency-denominated trade settlements or profit remittances. Practical remittance hurdles for the British Columbia CA_ULC are tax-driven rather than FX-driven: (i) non-resident dividend and interest remittances attract Part XIII Income Tax Act withholding at the statutory 25%, reduced by treaty (e.g., Canada-US Convention: 5% for a direct US corporate shareholder, 15% for portfolio dividends; interest 0%-15% as applicable); (ii) the payor must withhold, remit, and file NR4; (iii) payments to non-arm's-length foreign vendors may face CRA transfer-pricing and Part XIII re-characterisation review if not properly documented; (iv) a US parent utilising the ULC as a fiscally transparent entity must carefully apply the Canada-US Treaty anti-hybrid/ULC provisions to avoid unintended Part XIII gross-up and deemed-dividend results; and (v) financial institutions must report to FINTRAC any cross-border EFT of C$10,000 or more, which is a reporting obligation and not a convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement exists in Canada. Export receipts in foreign currency may be retained indefinitely in Canadian or offshore bank accounts, converted to CAD at any time, or used directly to settle foreign-currency import payables. There is no mandatory conversion ratio and no statutory repatriation timeline. Compliance obligations are limited to: (a) CBSA Form B13A export declaration for controlled or trackable exports; (b) FINTRAC reporting of cross-border EFTs of C$10,000 or more; (c) sanctions screening and export controls under the Special Economic Measures Act, United Nations Act, and Export and Import Permits Act; and (d) taxation of foreign-exchange gains/losses in ordinary business income, as required by CRA.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign-exchange control administration. The de facto FX control and monetary architecture is composed of the Department of Finance Canada (ministerial policy authority; Currency Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-52), the Bank of Canada (currency issuance, monetary policy, market intervention), OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions; prudential supervision of federally regulated banks), Payments Canada (national clearing and settlement), and FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada; cross-border EFT and suspicious transaction reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act).

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire-transfer protocol, purpose codes, and verification: (1) Classify the injection character before wiring: (a) equity subscription through issuance of common or preference shares from authorised capital; or (b) shareholder loan documented by written agreement and a CRA-prescribed or arm's-length interest rate. (2) Instruct the overseas shareholder's bank to send SWIFT MT103 with beneficiary being the ULC operating account; use ISO 20022 originator and purpose codes in field 70: 'EQUI' or 'INTC' for equity injection / shareholder group contribution, and 'LOAN' for shareholder funding. Canadian banks do not require a separately approved purpose code, but the receiving bank's screening system expects an explicit, business-consistent narrative such as 'CAPITAL SUBSCRIPTION - CA_ULC' or 'SHAREHOLDER LOAN - CA_ULC'. (3) Foreign exchange conversion is unrestricted; the receiving Canadian bank quotes a spot CAD rate for USD, CNY, HKD, or JPY proceeds. For injections above US$250,000, the bank will obtain a same-day or next-day forward quote to mitigate conversion risk. (4) Verification pack: board resolution authorising the injection; share certificate ledger or executed loan agreement; current BC corporate extract; and UBO/sanction screening clearance. The bank records FATCA/CRS attributes of the beneficial owners and files FINTRAC EFT reports where the amount is C$10,000 or more. (5) Tax verification: capital subscriptions are non-taxable and free of Part XIII withholding; interest on shareholder loans is subject to Part XIII unless treaty-reduced and verified by NR301/NR302 forms; dividend declarations must be withheld at treaty rate and remitted with NR4. (6) For conditional capital contributions, funds may be parked in a solicitor's trust escrow account until corporate conditions are satisfied, then released to the operating account under the same protocol.Clearing rails for trade settlements: (a) SWIFT MT103 direct wire is the standard rail for invoice values generally below US$250,000; the remittance narrative must tie to an invoiced purchase order (e.g., 'INV 2025-110 - RICE AND CONDIMENTS'); bank executes the CAD-to-foreign-currency conversion at spot or at the locked forward rate; beneficiary bank details include full SWIFT BIC and account. (b) For larger consolidated grocery-shipment contracts, the primary rail is a commercial documentary letter of credit issued by the Canadian bank via SWIFT MT700 and governed by UCP 600; the supplier's bank in Singapore, Hong Kong, or the respective Asian jurisdiction acts as advising or confirming bank; presentation documents typically include commercial invoice, full set clean on-board bill of lading, packing list, health and phytosanitary certificates (CFIA), certificate of origin, and independent inspection certificate (SGS/BV/Intertek) where required; settlement occurs at sight or under tenor drafts via SWIFT MT754/MT756 to the beneficiary bank. (c) In smaller niche markets, documentary collection via SWIFT MT400/MT410 (documents against payment) may be used but carries elevated counterparty risk and is not recommended for food-commodity imports without credit insurance. (d) Domestic rails within Canada: Payments Canada ACSS for cheques and EFT files; Lynx real-time gross settlement (RTGS) for C$ payments above C$100,000 to provincial distributors, cold-chain logistics providers, and customs brokers; Interac e-Transfer for small urgent disbursements; Interac Direct Payment and Visa/Mastercard acquiring rails for in-store and delivery-channel receipts. Tax Evasion

Shell Company

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for AML penalties if they knowingly fail to implement required compliance measures.
