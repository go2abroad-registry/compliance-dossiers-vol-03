E-government filing portal

Step 2 — Obtain the federal Business Number (BN) by filing CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or by using CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://broservices.ca. Step 3 — Activate program accounts on the same BN: GST/HST (RT) under the Excise Tax Act; Import/Export (RM) for CBSA; and Payroll (RP) if Canadian employees are engaged. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — Under subsection 248(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada), 'corporation' includes an incorporated company, and a BC ULC incorporated under section 51.1 of the BC Business Corporations Act is treated as a taxable Canadian corporation. The ULC is subject to federal Part I corporate income tax at the general non-CCPC rate of 15% (38% basic rate less 10% provincial abatement less 13% general rate reduction) plus BC provincial corporate income tax at 12%, producing a combined headline rate of approximately 27% on worldwide income; it is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation because the direct parent is non-resident, so the small business deduction is unavailable. The entity is separately liable for GST/HST (5% federal) and BC PST (7%), and does not qualify for the Tax_Exempt_Zone or Territorial_Scope classifications. Notwithstanding Canadian entity-level taxation, the ULC is Pass_Through_Eligible solely for United States federal income tax purposes: a single US member may elect to treat it as a disregarded entity or, with multiple members, as a partnership under IRC Section 7701 and Treasury Regulation Section 301.7701-3 (Form 8832); such US check-the-box election does not alter the CRA's classification of the ULC as a corporation.

Available election forms

(1) US check-the-box election: The non-resident US member may file IRS Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election) to treat the BC ULC as a disregarded entity (single-member) or partnership (multi-member) for US federal tax; the default classification of a single-member foreign eligible entity is disregarded and of a multi-member entity is partnership, creating a hybrid-entity structure. (2) Voluntary GST/HST registration election under subsection 240(1) of the Excise Tax Act: the ULC may elect to register even where taxable supplies are below the CAD 30,000 mandatory threshold. (3) Hybrid mismatch elective alignment under sections 18.6 and 18.7 of the Income Tax Act (OECD BEPS Action 2): the ULC may elect in its Canadian filing to align deduction or taxable-income outcomes where the same expenditure is deductible in Canada and exempt or excluded in the United States as a result of the check-the-box mismatch. (4) Non-resident investment elections under subsection 216(1) of the Income Tax Act are available for passive Canadian rental income only and are not applicable to an active craft beer wholesale and distribution business. (5) BC PST Certificate of Resale mechanism: BC purchasers who provide a completed Form FIN 491 (Certificate of Resale) cause the ULC's sale to be exempt from BC PST at the point of sale. (6) Federal-provincial provincial abatement election under subsection 124(1) of the Income Tax Act is automatic for provincial corporate allocation; no separate election is required.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — mandatory federal GST/HST registration threshold under subsection 240(1)(b) of the Excise Tax Act, computed as the aggregate value of taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies) made by the ULC in Canada in the current calendar quarter and the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Zero-rated export supplies are excluded from the threshold calculation, so a ULC exporting all of its output is still required to register before claiming input tax credits and zero-rating its exports. BC PST: no fixed registration threshold — registration is mandatory before the first PST-taxable sale, lease, or transfer in British Columbia. In addition, as a craft brewery wholesaler, the ULC must hold a BC Liquor Manufacturer's licence and the applicable Liquor and Cannabis Regulation Branch (LCRB) distribution authorization before selling or delivering kegs in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export treatment is available under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: (1) Goods for export, section 6 — the supply of personal property (craft beer kegs) is zero-rated where the ULC ships the goods to a non-resident recipient for export and the goods are exported as soon as reasonably practicable after the consideration becomes due (CRA administrative standard: generally within 60 days). The recipient must be a non-resident at the time the supply is made, and the goods must not be used or consumed in Canada before export; possession must transfer outside Canada by the ULC itself or at the recipient's risk. (2) Offshore services, section 7 — services supplied by the ULC to a non-resident who is not registered under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act and who does not use or benefit from the services in Canada are zero-rated, including distribution and marketing services performed for a US importer. (3) Services rendered to a non-resident principal, section 8 — commission or agency services arranged by the ULC for the sale of goods outside Canada are zero-rated. (4) Registration precondition: only a GST/HST registrant may apply zero-rating and claim input tax credits; the ULC must maintain an active GST/HST account even when making exclusively zero-rated export supplies. No CRA pre-approval or export certificate is required before zero-rating; eligibility is self-assessed and substantiated at audit. For BC PST purposes, delivery of goods outside British Columbia to a non-resident purchaser is exempt from PST and does not affect the federal zero-rating claim.

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, tax-authority pre-approval, or mandated continuous transaction control (CTC) e-invoicing regime exists in Canada; the CRA operates a self-assessment, post-audit verification model. Mandatory electronic filing and remittance requirements apply: GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or EDI/XML where annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 50,000, and any GST/HST remittance exceeding CAD 10,000 must be made electronically. CBSA requires electronic filing of the Canadian Export Declaration (B13A) through the Automated Export Declaration (AED) system and an approved service provider for shipments exceeding CAD 2,000. CRA GST/HST Memorandum 3.5 (Invoicing Requirements) prescribes mandatory invoice content: supplier legal name, GST/HST registration number, date of invoice, buyer name, description of goods, and consideration expressed separately; there is no API-stamping or official QR code requirement. Canada has commenced a voluntary consultation on a Stream 4 real-time e-invoicing framework and the Peppol-based public procurement invoicing standard, but adoption remains optional for private B2B transactions as of the current tax year. The BC Ministry of Finance does not impose any electronic fiscal-clearance or digital-stamping obligation on PST invoicing.

The CRA treats export zero-rating claims as a high-risk audit area because fabricated or circular export invoices are the dominant form of GST/HST fraud in Canada. Consequences for disallowed zero-rating include: (1) Reassessment — the CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period under section 298 of the Excise Tax Act; where the claim involves misrepresentation, carelessness, or neglect, the period extends to ten years; fraudulent claims are reassessable without limitation. (2) Tax clawback — full 5% GST is assessed on the supply together with compound daily interest at CRA prescribed rates; the BC Ministry of Finance may separately assess 7% PST, interest, and penalties if goods are found to have been delivered in British Columbia. (3) Stated penalties — subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act imposes a penalty equal to 50% of the tax that would be payable if the false or misleading statement had been accepted; subsection 285(1) imposes a gross-negligence penalty equal to 25% of the understated tax, minimum CAD 100, stackable on the tax and interest. (4) False-declaration penalties — section 281.1 of the Excise Tax Act authorizes penalties of up to CAD 5,000 per false declaration. (5) Criminal exposure — fraudulent export zero-rating may be prosecuted under the Excise Tax Act and section 380(1) of the Criminal Code (fraud exceeding CAD 5,000), with fines up to CAD 5,000,000 and imprisonment up to 14 years. Although Canada imposes no fixed statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, missing or inconclusive export documentation, including delayed or non-performing inward remittance evidence failing the 180-day evidentiary benchmark, will cause the CRA to recharacterize unpaid receivables as Canadian-source financing income, exposing the ULC to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax and Part I reassessment; the ULC must retain all export books and records for six years under subsection 230(1) of the Excise Tax Act, and directors are jointly and severally liable under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act for unremitted GST assessed on disallowed zero-rated exports.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has a fully free-floating currency and no exchange-control system under the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, or the Currency Exchange Act. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are freely convertible. Non-resident profit remittances, dividends, interest, and capital repatriations from a BC ULC are unrestricted at the FX level, provided tax withholding under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act is satisfied (typically 15% domestic rate, reduced under the Canada-US Tax Treaty) and transfer-pricing and thin-capitalization documentation is filed. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a deliverable G10 currency; there is no statutory conversion requirement, no repatriation license, and no minimum holding period for export proceeds. Practical hurdles arise only from banking counterparty due diligence and tax compliance, not from convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no timeline for repatriation of export receipts. A Canadian exporter, including a BC ULC engaged in cross-border distribution of craft beer, may retain foreign currency proceeds in resident or non-resident bank accounts and use them directly for foreign supplier payments. Exporters must nevertheless report goods exported under the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) customs procedures, provide a valid export declaration where applicable, and file statistical returns under the Statistics Act and the International Transactions Reporting System (ITRS) if aggregate transactions exceed the reporting threshold. No regulatory approval is required for holding USD/EUR settlement balances.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (monetary authority and Exchange Fund Account administrator); banking supervision is performed by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions Canada (OSFI); anti-money-laundering supervision is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no central exchange-control commission, and the Minister of Finance holds residual structural authority over foreign-exchange matters.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for settlement-only support; Low as sole treasury operator. A foreign fintech or electronic money institution (EMI) cannot obtain a Canadian chartered bank licence unless it submits to OSFI and CDIC supervision; a non-regulated EMI may operate only as a money services business (MSB) or payment service provider, requiring FINTRAC registration, a BC registration if operating locally, and adherence to the Retail Payment Activities Act supervision in Canada. Cross-border EMIs are viable for low-value operational disbursements, FX conversion, and supplier payments in limited amounts, but are not permitted to clear local Canadian direct debits through the Automated Clearing Settlement System, cannot open banker's acceptance facilities, and cannot provide regulated deposit insurance. For a BC ULC engaged in beverage distribution, the primary operational account must remain at a Schedule I or II Canadian bank to satisfy excise duty, GST, and trade counterparty expectations.
