Stage 03

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and CRA My Business Account. British Columbia provincial registrations are administered separately by the Ministry of Finance, including the Provincial Sales Tax (PST) Online Services portal. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers the CARM Client Portal and the importer/exporter program account for customs purposes.

E-government filing portal

A British Columbia limited partnership should first obtain or confirm its provincial registration and partnership identification documents. The partnership then applies to the CRA for a nine-digit Business Number (BN) through Business Registration Online, by telephone, or by submitting Form RC1 where applicable. The BN is used as the base identifier for CRA program accounts. A GST/HST account is opened where the partnership exceeds the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, voluntarily registers, or is otherwise required to register because of its taxable commercial activities. The GST/HST account uses the format BN plus RT0001. A payroll account uses RP0001 if employees are engaged. A corporation or partnership required to file information returns may require an information-return account. A non-resident entity may need to appoint a Canadian representative or provide additional identity, ownership, business-activity, and authorized-person documentation. If the entity imports or exports commercial goods, it must obtain an RM import-export account through the CBSA CARM Client Portal. A non-resident partnership carrying on business in Canada may also have Canadian income-tax filing, withholding, information-reporting, and treaty-compliance obligations even where the partnership itself is generally fiscally transparent.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for Canadian limited partnerships. A valid Canadian partnership is generally treated as fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes, with income and loss allocated to partners under the partnership agreement and applicable Canadian tax rules. The partnership may have a prescribed fiscal-period election or other partnership elections where statutory conditions are met. GST/HST registration is not an entity-classification election. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules under Part XVI.1 can deny deductions or impose income inclusions where cross-border hybrid arrangements produce deduction or inclusion mismatches; the partnership should document its classification in each relevant foreign jurisdiction. A foreign entity's foreign-law classification election does not automatically determine its Canadian classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, generally measured under the GST/HST small-supplier rules over the relevant preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter. Associated persons and specified supplies may affect the calculation. A non-resident or partnership may register voluntarily before reaching the threshold and may be required to register based on the nature and place of its commercial activities. British Columbia PST registration is separate and depends on carrying on business in British Columbia and making taxable sales, leases, or provision of taxable software or services; there is no universal PST threshold equivalent to the federal GST small-supplier threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be supplied at 0% GST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied and the supplier maintains evidence that the goods were exported or delivered for export under the applicable GST/HST rules. Services may be zero-rated only where a specific exported-service rule applies, typically requiring a non-resident recipient and satisfaction of conditions concerning the recipient's status, use, performance, or connection with Canada. Freight, logistics, warehousing, customs, parcel-consolidation, and linehaul charges must be analyzed separately; an export-related commercial purpose alone does not automatically make every service zero-rated. Services performed in Canada, supplied to a Canadian recipient, or relating to goods located in Canada may remain taxable. Zero-rated supplies may support recovery of eligible GST through input tax credits, provided the registrant is properly registered and maintains complete transactional evidence.

  • CRA-compliant tax invoice or electronic invoice identifying the supplier, recipient, consideration, GST rate, and transaction terms
  • CBSA export declaration submitted through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) where an export declaration is required, including the applicable export control or reporting reference
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing the goods, value, quantity, consignee, destination, and terms of sale
  • Air waybill, bill of lading, courier manifest, or other carrier transportation record
  • Proof of export or delivery outside Canada, such as carrier confirmation, customs exit evidence, delivery confirmation, or foreign import documentation where available
  • Customer purchase order, contract, service agreement, and evidence of the recipient's non-resident status where zero-rating depends on the recipient
  • Warehouse, consolidation, inventory, scan, and chain-of-custody records linking the exported parcels to the relevant invoice and customs entry
  • Proof of payment or commercial settlement records where relevant; an inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian statutory requirement for GST zero-rating
  • GST/HST return, input-tax-credit workpapers, and refund-support schedules reconciling zero-rated revenue, export documents, and claimed input tax credits

Canada has no general real-time GST clearance, mandatory nationwide e-invoicing, or digital tax-stamping requirement for ordinary business-to-business invoices. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices containing sufficient prescribed information to support tax collection, input tax credits, and audit verification, and must retain books and records generally for six years. CRA electronic filing requirements apply to specified returns and registrants, subject to statutory thresholds and exemptions. Electronic records must preserve authenticity, integrity, readability, and audit accessibility. Customs declarations are submitted electronically through CBSA systems, including CARM where applicable. British Columbia PST invoices and records must satisfy provincial PST documentation rules. Commercial invoices, transport records, customs declarations, proof of delivery, and customer-location evidence should be retained for parcel-consolidation and direct-injection transactions.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days. Zero-rating may be denied or reassessed where export, recipient-status, place-of-supply, or documentary conditions are not met. The CRA may assess uncollected GST, deny input tax credits or GST refunds, impose interest, and apply gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit or refund where the statutory standard is met. False statements, wilful evasion, obstruction, or fraudulent refund activity may result in additional civil penalties and criminal prosecution, including fines and imprisonment under the Excise Tax Act and related legislation. A registrant may be required to repay improperly claimed refunds and remit tax retrospectively for the applicable reassessment period. Customs non-compliance may trigger seizure, administrative monetary penalties, reassessment of duties and taxes, export-reporting penalties, and additional enforcement under the Customs Act and related CBSA programs.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. No prior government approval is required for foreign direct investment, equity contributions, trade settlements, distributions to non-resident partners, or repatriation of capital. Cross-border payments are subject only to standard financial-institution compliance and tax reporting, including withholding tax on certain payments to non-residents.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. There is no statutory surrender timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or required routing of export-related receipts. Export proceeds for warehouse, freight forwarding, parcel consolidation, and ancillary logistics services may be retained in any freely convertible currency and held in Canadian or offshore accounts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; prudential regulation by Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering supervision by Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada has no foreign-exchange control authority and no exchange-control legislation currently in force.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

High for payment settlement and low to medium for treasury concentration. Fintechs such as Airwallex and equivalent cross-border payment providers operate in Canada as Money Services Businesses registered with FINTRAC and as Retail Payment Service Providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act. They cannot create a Canadian clearing account or provide deposit insurance, but are effective for multi-currency collection, vendor payout, and direct-injection linehaul settlements.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the Limited Partnership entity level (fiscally transparent); allocated taxable income is taxed in the hands of partners at applicable rates, up to 27% combined federal (15%) and British Columbia (12%) corporate headline rate for a corporate partner.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII on dividends from a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident owner; treaty-reduced to 15% (portfolio) or 5% (direct 10%+ corporate shareholder) under Canada-US DTA; ordinary LP profit allocations are taxed as Canadian business income to the non-resident partner under Part I, not as dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 filing required when aggregate non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CA$1,000,000; country-by-country reporting required when multinational group consolidated revenue exceeds CA$750,000,000. Canada does not impose a statutory Local File/Master File threshold, but CRA expects contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation to obtain penalty protection.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations, but CRA applies GAAR, permanent establishment, substance-over-form, and anti-treaty-shopping principles. The BC LP must maintain a real office, employees, decision-making records, and Canadian books/records; otherwise CRA and the courts may disregard the partnership or deny treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion / false statements (ITA ss.163, 239)

Statutory sanction: Gross-negligence penalty of 50% of unreported tax; criminal evasion fine of 50%–200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to five years; CRA may reassess beyond the normal limitation period.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and general partners who directed or participated in the evasion are personally liable under directing-mind and joint-liability principles; limited partners can lose limited-liability protection under BC Partnership Act by taking part in management or control and may face full liability for the partnership's obligations.
Transfer pricing misstatement / unapproved profit shifting

Statutory sanction: Reallocation of income under ITA s.247(2); penalty under s.247(3) of 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment (minimum CA$5,000); interest on adjustments and penalties; possible CbCR filing penalties.

Director personal liability: The general partner is jointly liable for partnership tax debts to the extent of partnership assets; directors of a corporate general partner may be liable for unremitted withholding amounts under s.227.1 and for gross-negligence penalties if they knowingly approved false documentation.
Shell entity / sham / treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization and denial of treaty benefits; GAAR penalty under s.245.5 of 50% of tax avoided in respect of undisclosed avoidance transactions; possible deregistration of the BC LP and repayment of GST/BC PST credits.

Director personal liability: CRA may hold the general partner and its directors personally accountable for restitution if funds were distributed or commingled without bona fide commercial purpose; BC courts may pierce the LP form where it is used as a sham to defeat tax or creditors.
Part XIII withholding tax failure

Statutory sanction: CRA assesses the payer for the amount that ought to have been withheld under s.215(6), plus additional penalties and daily compound interest; s.227.1 director liability for unremitted amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors of the payor corporate general partner become jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding taxes, interest, and penalties unless they exercised the standard of care of a reasonably prudent person; asset and departure restrictions may be used in serious enforcement cases.
AML / banking compliance (PCMLTFA and Criminal Code)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CA$500,000 for a corporation and CA$250,000 for an individual per violation; money laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31 punishable by imprisonment up to 10 years; assets subject to forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized, directed, or acquiesced in AML failures can be named in FINTRAC notices, assessed administrative penalties, and prosecuted; individuals who commit or participate in money laundering face direct criminal liability, imprisonment, and civil confiscation.
