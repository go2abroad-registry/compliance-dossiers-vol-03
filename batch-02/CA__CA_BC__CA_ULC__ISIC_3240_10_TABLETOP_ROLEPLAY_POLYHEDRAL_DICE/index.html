Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) acting as the national tax authority; administered through the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account (MyBA) for GST/HST and payroll; provincial sales tax is administered separately by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance (Consumer Taxation Branch) through eTaxBC.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity or non-resident ULC managing a BC manufacturing and export business must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 or using the Business Registration Online service, then register separately for a GST/HST account under Division II of Part IX of the Excise Tax Act. Where the ULC is incorporated or extra-provincially registered in BC, it must also obtain a BC Corporate Registration number and, if making taxable sales of tangible personal property in BC, register for BC PST through eTaxBC. The BN becomes the TIN for federal tax purposes; the GST/HST account number and BC PST number are derived from that registration or separate provincial enrollment. Identification documents must include the certificate of incorporation/extra-provincial registration, directors' information, registered office address, and the ultimate beneficial owner profile for non-resident shareholders.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

For U.S. federal tax purposes, the BC ULC may file IRS Form 8832 to make a check-the-box election to be disregarded as an entity separate from its owner, aligning with the unlimited liability and hybrid entity attributes of the ULC. For Canadian income tax purposes, the CRA generally treats the ULC as a corporation unless a specific statutory election applies, such as the deemed remittance and acceleration elections under the Excise Tax Act or the section 216 and non-resident withholding elections under the Income Tax Act; hybrid mismatch rules under the BEPS Multilateral Instrument should be assessed for cross-border deductible payments between the ULC and its foreign parent.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 for GST/HST: a supplier becomes a mandatory registrant once worldwide taxable supplies (including exports but excluding zero-rated supplies and capital assets) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single quarter or over the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters. BC PST has no analogous small-supplier threshold for retailers making taxable sales in BC; PST collection registration is required from the first taxable sale, although certain incidental or occasional sales may be excluded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for GST/HST applies to supplies of tangible personal property made by a registrant that are exported by the supplier, or by the purchaser under a supplier-to-purchaser drop-shipment arrangement, where the property is delivered outside Canada as commercial goods or as a direct export in the ordinary course of business. The supplier must retain documentary evidence that the goods were exported in accordance with section 5 of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act; service supplies that are performed in Canada but delivered to non-residents may be zero-rated only if the recipient is a non-resident and the supply is an exported service within section 7 of Schedule VI. BC PST generally does not apply to goods sold for delivery outside Canada, but the seller must maintain export documentation to support that exemption. The tabletop dice must be physically exported or sent through a licensed customs broker with the purchaser taking delivery outside Canada; domestic warehousing followed by domestic delivery does not qualify.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency Form A1/CAERS or Export Declaration for shipments with value exceeding CAD 2,000 or controlled goods)
  • Commercial invoice clearly identifying the purchaser, description, HS classification (ISIC 3240 / HS 9504 or 9503 as appropriate), quantity, and sale value
  • Bill of lading or air waybill evidencing physical movement of the goods from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Cargo Control Document, customs delivery confirmation, or carrier manifest showing the goods left Canada through a designated port of exit
  • Proof of foreign customs entry or foreign port acknowledgment where available
  • Inward remittance certificate, SWIFT/MT103 confirmation, or bank payment record showing the export sale proceeds being received in Canada

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous digital stamping, or government pre-authorization of invoices. Invoices may be issued in paper or electronic form and must contain the prescribed GST/HST registration number, date, vendor and purchaser identification, description, consideration, and tax amount. CRA accepts electronic records, EDI, and API-based submission of GST/HST returns through GST/HST NETFILE; BC PST returns must be filed through eTaxBC. There is no API-based national e-invoice clearance portal, though supply chain partners trading in the EU may voluntarily use Peppol-compliant invoice exchange.

CRA and CBSA may jointly audit export zero-rating claims; the typical documentation-retention period is six years from the end of the reporting period. If a zero-rating claim is rejected, the supplier is liable for uncollected GST/HST at the full 5% federal rate, plus arrears interest and penalties. Knowingly making a false or misleading statement in a return or claiming fraudulent export zero-rating can trigger a penalty equal to 25% of the tax sought, additional penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act, and a fine and/or imprisonment for tax evasion under section 327 and the Criminal Code. Retrospective clawbacks are not time-limited where the CRA establishes misrepresentation, neglect, or willful default on the part of the registrant; otherwise reassessment is limited to four years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a freely floating exchange-rate regime with no exchange-control legislation and no prior-approval requirement for currency conversion or cross-border fund movements. Both current-account items (dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, trade payments) and capital-account items (equity injections, shareholder loans, repatriation of capital, liquidation proceeds) are fully convertible in CAD, USD, EUR, GBP, and any major convertible currency. Profit remittance from a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Corporation (BC ULC) to a non-resident shareholder is lawful and unrestricted, subject only to the standard Canadian tax architecture: a T2 corporation income tax return, withholding of Part XIII non-resident tax on dividends and certain payments (typically reduced under the relevant tax treaty), submission of Form T106 where transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000, and FINTRAC large-transaction reporting where a single receipt equals or exceeds CAD 10,000. Operational friction risk is low, but correspondent banks may append sanctions or FATF jurisdictional flags requiring additional provenance documentation for inbound wires from high-risk jurisdictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no obligation to repatriate export receipts in foreign currency. A BC ULC may retain USD, EUR, or GBP proceeds indefinitely in a foreign-currency bank account, and the decision to convert into CAD is purely commercial. In practice, the clearing bank will require a domestic or US correspondent account (e.g., RBC's New York branch or BMO Harris) for USD-denominated clearing, and for each receipt the importer or exporter must preserve the supporting commercial invoice, customs entry (if materially above the CAD 3,000 de minimis threshold for casual goods, or the applicable importer-of-record obligations of the counterparty), and the shipping evidence (bill of lading, airway bill, or courier manifest). Payment-matching under OSFI's Transaction Monitoring and Reporting expectations requires that the wire narrative include the relevant purpose code, invoice number, and F/X instruction; if the transaction is part of the FINTRAC-registered activity of the ULC's payment service providers (e.g., Stripe, PayPal), those providers also file their own reports. Large receipts of CAD 10,000 or more within 24 hours must be reported by the Canadian bank to FINTRAC as large cash or virtual-currency transactions, and the ULC must be able to produce the underlying crowdfunding or sales-contract evidence immediately upon demand.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting with the Department of Finance as the monetary and exchange-rate authority; prudential supervision of deposit-taking institutions is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and anti-money-laundering supervision is administered by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary liquidity rail, Low as a primary treasury solution. Canadian digital-first business accounts (Stripe Treasury, Shopify Balance, Desjardins/EQ Bank hybrid offerings) and regulated fintechs can receive CAD and hold settlement balances, but they are generally not licensed as banks under the Bank Act and do not carry CDIC deposit insurance. Foreign EMIs (UK FCA e-money institutions, EU MiFID-authorized PSPs, Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex) may lawfully provide payment services to a BC ULC, but they cannot open a Canadian deposit account, cannot provide a Canadian clearing code for CAD direct deposits, and will require the ULC to maintain a separate Schedule I bank account for tax-proof remittances, Part XIII withholding, and FINTRAC-facing activity. For crowdfunding settlement, the ULC should receive funds from Kickstarter/Stripe and PayPal directly into a Canadian bank account and thereafter sweep to the EMI only for cross-border payments where the EMI's foreign-exchange spread is demonstrably superior. Legal constraint: an offshore EMI is not a 'bank' for the purposes of the Criminal Code or the Bank Act, so holding material operating capital with an EMI triggers concentration-risk red flags in the ULC's AML/KYC profile and may complicate the Part XIII withholding trail.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol for equity and shareholder-loan funding into the BC ULC: (1) execute a board resolution approving the capital contribution and, where a shareholder loan is used, a written loan agreement with market-rate or prescribed-rate interest and term; (2) hold the funds in the ULC's capital escrow account or general operating account at a Schedule I bank (RBC/TD/BMO/CIBC) and designate the wire in SWIFT field 70 with purpose code 'CAPC' (Capital Contribution) or the ISO 20022 purpose code 'TOCA'/'INCA' for inward capital transfer; (3) use the remitter's bank account in the shareholder's jurisdiction with 'OUR' charges to guarantee clean proceeds; (4) instruct the Canadian bank to apply the Receipt of Proceeds verification: corporate search, board resolution, shareholder register update, and allonge to the share certificate or loan agreement. For a US-based shareholder injecting into a BC ULC, the ULC should provide the bank with its US 'check-the-box' classification evidence (Form 8832) only if relevant; the Canadian bank itself will record the funds as contributed surplus or notes-payable. The bank must verify no third-party beneficial interest obtains; where the shareholder is a non-resident corporation, the bank will additionally require a certified copy of the shareholder's corporate registry record and a list of its directors or controllers. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary liquidity rail, Low as a primary treasury solution. Canadian digital-first business accounts (Stripe Treasury, Shopify Balance, Desjardins/EQ Bank hybrid offerings) and regulated fintechs can receive CAD and hold settlement balances, but they are generally not licensed as banks under the Bank Act and do not carry CDIC deposit insurance. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined general Canadian federal and British Columbia corporate income tax rate of 27% (federal 15% + BC 12%). For a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), eligible active business income up to C$500,000 is taxed at an effective combined rate of 11% (federal 9% + BC 2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the federal Income Tax Act for dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to non-resident shareholders. Treaty relief may reduce the withholding rate: 15% for most non-resident beneficial owners, and 5% if the beneficial owner is a US or treaty-resident corporation that owns at least 10% of the voting shares of the ULC and satisfies treaty conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 (Canadian dollars) aggregate value of cross-border non-arm's length transactions with a related non-resident party in a single taxation year triggers mandatory transfer pricing contemporaneous documentation requirements (Local File / Master File) and the annual filing of Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents).

Economic substance scope: British Columbia does not apply offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as found in certain zero-tax jurisdictions. However, the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) applies the substantive doctrine under the Income Tax Act's general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) and a substance-over-form approach. The hybrid ULC structure must maintain real business activities, actual manufacturing operations, and a Canadian permanent establishment to benefit from the Canada-US treaty limitation on benefits (LOB) clause and to rebut any CRA claim that the ULC is a sham or an artificial shell.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal punishment under s.239 of the Income Tax Act includes a fine between 50% and 200% of the evaded tax, or imprisonment for up to five years, or both; civil gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax; daily compounding interest on unpaid tax from the date it should have been paid.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding and GST/HST under s.227.1 and s.323 of the Excise Tax Act; personal liability in civil fraud cases may pierce the corporate veil, exposing directors to personal judgment, travel bans, and imprisonment for willful participation in tax evasion.
Shell Company / Sham Transaction

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the GAAR penalty of 50% of the tax benefit and re-allocate income on a substance-over-form basis; the BC courts may disregard the ULC's separate legal existence as a sham and impose penalties under the BC Business Corporations Act; unscrupulous promoters may face fraud charges.

Director personal liability: Because a ULC imposes unlimited liability, the court may pierce the corporate veil where the entity is used for fraud; directors can be personally liable for corporate tax debts, civil restitution claims, and may be prohibited from holding directorships or leaving the jurisdiction.
AML / Terrorist Financing

Statutory sanction: Monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, up to C$250,000 for a criminal fine and administrative monetary penalties of C$100,000 for first-time non-compliance; Criminal Code offences may lead to asset seizure, forfeiture, and penalties up to C$1,000,000 or ten years in prison.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct or participate in money laundering are personally exposed to criminal prosecution, travel bans, property freezing, and imprisonment; civil liability may also result from breach of duty and knowing assistance in the unlawful scheme.
Failure to Document Transfer Pricing

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under s.247(4) is 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment, in addition to full reassessment and compound interest; GAAR may apply separately with a 50% penalty on the tax benefit; in egregious cases, criminal prosecution for making a false return may result in fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly approved false intercompany prices or failed to ensure the filing of accurate T106 returns may be assessed personal liability under tax fraud provisions; the unlimited nature of the ULC means directors can be personally accountable for the resulting corporate tax debts and civil restitution, and in extreme cases may face travel bans and imprisonment.
