Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); federal portal: Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MyBA). Provincial indirect tax authority: British Columbia Ministry of Finance – BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) registration via eTaxBC.

E-government filing portal

A BC unlimited liability corporation (ULC) is incorporated under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia) with the BC Corporate Registry. Upon incorporation, obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by completing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' via CRA's Business Registration Online or by telephone/fax. For a foreign-owned ULC without physical Canadian presence, apply through the non-resident streamlined registration using Form GST494, supplying the foreign certificate of incorporation, registered office address, director identity documents, and particulars of a Canadian agent for service of process. Once issued, the 9-digit BN program-account structure (e.g., RT0001 for GST/HST, RC0001 for payroll) constitutes the statutory tax identification number. A separate BC PST account is obtained through eTaxBC where taxable retail sales are made or taxable goods are consumed in British Columbia.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under Canadian domestic income tax law, a ULC is a separate taxable corporation; however, cross-border fiscal-transparency elections are available: (i) U.S. check-the-box election under Form 8832 to classify the ULC as an eligible entity taxable as a corporation, partnership, or disregarded entity; (ii) Canada–U.S. Tax Convention Article IV(6) election to treat the ULC as legally separate and resident in Canada or as fiscally transparent, and Article IV(7) mutual-agreement ULC residency election; (iii) Excise Tax Act s. 273 election to treat closely related corporations (including ULCs) as a single person for GST/HST purposes so inter-entity supplies are not subject to GST/HST; (iv) Income Tax Act branch-tax planning and s. 219(1.1) elections applicable to non-resident-owned corporations carrying on Canadian business; (v) provincial BC PST self-assessment elections for inter-company sales of taxable goods.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 – the GST/HST 'small supplier' threshold: mandatory registration is triggered once worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports) exceed CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or in the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Non-resident suppliers use Form GST494 and are measured on taxable supplies made in Canada; BC PST has no small-supplier exemption and registration is generally mandatory for every person selling or leasing taxable goods in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, a supply of tangible personal property that is exported from Canada, or in the case of VHP bulk raw cane sugar delivered to a carrier or consignee for shipment to a non-resident purchaser, is zero-rated at 0% GST/HST provided the supplier possesses contemporaneous documentary evidence that the goods were physically exported and were not diverted back into the Canadian market. International freight, shipping, and cargo-insurance services supplied in respect of the exported sugar are likewise zero-rated. The commercial and legal risk must pass to the buyer outside Canada, and the supplier must hold a complete export-evidence bundle at the time the return is filed and throughout the CRA reassessment period.

  • Customs Export Declaration
  • Bill of Lading
  • Inward Remittance Certificate
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Freight Forwarder's Cargo Receipt / Proof of Export Report

Canada does not impose a national real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance, or digital stamping mandate for GST/HST. Suppliers must issue invoices containing the prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act and CRA Information Circular IC 7000. Exports and imports are cleared electronically through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Single Window Initiative, the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED), and mandatory Advance Commercial Information e-manifests; no digital stamp is used on domestic invoices. Quebec has introduced mandatory B2B electronic invoice receipt for large enterprises, but this does not apply in British Columbia to the ULC wholesale sugar trading platform.

CRA may reassess beyond the four-year normal limitation period where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established. Retrospective clawbacks include reversed zero-rating, denied input tax credits, compounded daily arrears interest, and a gross-negligence penalty under s. 285 of the Excise Tax Act equal to 25% of the unreported net tax. For fraudulent evasion, s. 280 imposes monetary penalties up to double the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years. False statements in export declarations additionally attract CBSA penalties under the Customs Act, generally up to CAD 25,000 per false statement or 50% of the value of the goods. Export zero-rating claims lacking the mandatory customs documentation bundle are specifically targeted in CRA commodity-trading verification projects; confirmed failures trigger duty reassessments, refund cancellations, and treaty-related secondary adjustments.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Current account: full convertibility; no surrender, no approval, no repatriation limits; dividends, interest, and royalties may be remitted freely to non-residents subject to Part XIII Canadian withholding tax (default 25%; treaty-reduced under the Canada-US Income Tax Convention, e.g., arm's length interest exemption and 5% or 15% dividend rates for qualifying U.S. holders). Capital account: CAD is freely convertible; no exchange-control licence is required for a BC ULC to open, fund, or operate USD/CAD accounts; inbound foreign investment is screened only under the Investment Canada Act net benefit review at prevailing WTO enterprise-value thresholds; non-resident ownership of a BC ULC is expressly permitted.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None apply in Canada. There is no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. A Canadian-resident exporter may retain USD and other foreign currency balances freely onshore or offshore. Administrative duties: (i) file a CBSA export declaration through CERS if the physical goods are exported and the value exceeds CAD 2,000 or the item is permit-controlled; (ii) the financial institution automatically reports every international electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC; and (iii) for CRA income tax purposes, foreign-currency revenue must be translated into CAD at an acceptable Bank of Canada exchange rate (transaction-day or monthly average) in accordance with the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) / Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) / FINTRAC. Canada has no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration; monetary authority is the Bank of Canada, prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions is OSFI, AML/cross-border EFT reporting is FINTRAC, and export/import controls are administered by Global Affairs Canada with Canada Border Services Agency enforcement.

Feasibility Rating: Low for trade settlement; Medium for ancillary disbursements. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal-provincial general corporate income tax rate (15% federal Part I rate + 12% British Columbia provincial general rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act, applied to interest, dividend, royalty, and similar payments made by the ULC to a non-resident shareholder. Under the Canada-US Income Tax Convention, the rate is 5% on dividends paid to a US corporation that is a beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the ULC's voting shares; otherwise the treaty rate is 15%. The domestic 25% rate applies when no treaty benefit is established.

Schedule 1 / Schedule 5 – Tax Payable Computation of federal Part I tax at 15% and British Columbia provincial tax at 12%, before tax credits, including any applicable dividend refund, foreign tax credits, and national or provincial surtaxes. The ULC must file a full T2 in its own right; no consolidation with the non-resident US parent is permitted for Canadian tax purposes. Late filing penalty under ITA s.162(1): 5% of the balance owing plus 1% per full month late, up to 12 months. For repeat failures within three years, the penalty increases to 10% plus 2% per month late, up to 20 months. Arrears interest is compounded daily at the prescribed statutory interest rate, and late instalment penalties apply to corporations with insufficient quarterly tax instalments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian Form T106 reporting, a return must be filed when aggregate non-arm's length cross-border transactions exceed CAD 25,000. For Master File / Local File documentation, Canada does not prescribe a separate statutory monetary threshold; the CRA may require contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for any related-party cross-border transaction. Country-by-Country Report filing is mandatory when the ULC's ultimate parent consolidated group revenue equals or exceeds CAD 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies economic-substance principles through the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245), targeted anti-avoidance rules for dividend stripping and thin capitalization, and the limitation-of-benefits clause of the Canada-US tax treaty (Article XXIX-A). There is no separate Cayman-type Economic Substance Regulation, but a BC ULC must demonstrate real commercial activity, actual sugar trading contracts, shipping documentation, warehouse risk, and substantive treasury and management functions in BC to sustain treaty benefits and prevent GAAR reassessment. A ULC used purely as a sham conduit with no employees, no commodities risk, and no BC decision-making authority will be disregarded.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239(1) and Criminal Code s.380: imprisonment up to 5 years, fines of 50% to 200% of the total tax evaded, full reassessment of tax plus interest and civil gross-negligence penalties under ITA s.163(2).

Director personal liability: The corporate veil will be pierced where the ULC is a mere façade; directors who knowingly participate in fraud face personal civil restitution, arrest warrants, extradition, and travel bans through criminal proceedings. Directors may also be held personally liable for unremitted withholding taxes under ITA s.227.1.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess income, disregard the non-arm's length price, impose interest, and apply a transfer-pricing penalty under ITA s.247(3) equal to 10% of the understated tax payable after adjustment.

Director personal liability: Directors do not ordinarily incur statutory personal liability for transfer-pricing errors, but deliberate transfer-pricing fraud can trigger criminal tax evasion exposure and personal liability through participation in fraudulent financial arrangements.
Treaty Abuse and Shell Company / GAAR Conduct

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke GAAR (ITA s.245) to deny treaty benefits, recharacterize the payment as a dividend, impose the full 25% Part XIII withholding tax, apply arrears interest, and assess civil penalties under ITA s.163.2 for false statements and tax shelter misrepresentations.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize sham transactions may be treated as having participated in a fraudulent scheme; Canadian courts can disregard the separate legal personality of the ULC, impose personal restitution, issue travel restrictions, and pursue imprisonment in egregious cases.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act up to CAD 1 million for first violations and higher for repeated offences; criminal fines and imprisonment for laundering or knowingly dealing in property derived from crime; asset forfeiture orders may be made against the ULC.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally sanctioned for failing to implement adequate KYC/AML controls and may face criminal prosecution if they direct or authorize unlawful wire transfers; travel bans and asset-restraint orders can be imposed, and director liability for monetary penalties can reach personal estates.
