Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – federal GST/HST, income tax and payroll portal (My Business Account); British Columbia Ministry of Finance – Provincial Sales Tax (PST) and BC employer health tax (eTaxBC).

E-government filing portal

For a limited partnership, obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by registering through the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal or by filing Form RC1. Mandatory registration applies if the LP makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier (worldwide taxable revenues exceed CAD 30,000 in the current quarter or the four preceding calendar quarters). Use the same BN to open a GST/HST account (BN suffix RT) and, if applicable, a payroll account (RP). For BC PST, register with the BC Ministry of Finance through eTaxBC upon the first taxable sale in the province; there is no PST registration threshold. The LP is fiscally transparent for income tax and does not obtain a separate income tax TIN; it files an information return (Form T5013) and issues T5013 slips to each partner, including non-residents, who must obtain or maintain appropriate Canadian TINs where a Canadian filing obligation arises.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election. A limited partnership may elect under subsection 96(3) of the Income Tax Act to adopt a fiscal period, and under subsection 249.1(6) to change its fiscal period. For GST/HST, certain joint elections under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act may be available for closely related corporations and partnerships to simplify inter-party billing. Hybrid mismatch rules in sections 18.2, 18.3, 20.2(2) and 247 of the Income Tax Act may require additional documentation where cross-border payments involve fiscally transparent entities such as a Canadian LP with non-resident partners.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (small supplier threshold). PST: no threshold; registration required upon first taxable sale in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of patent prosecution and IP agency services to a non-resident of Canada is zero-rated for GST/HST under Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 of the Excise Tax Act where the recipient is not registered for GST/HST, the recipient is non-resident at the time of performance, and the service is not in respect of real property or tangible personal property located in Canada at any time during performance. The supplier must preserve contemporaneous documentation evidencing the non-resident status of the recipient and the export nature of the service.

  • Customs Export Declaration (where the service is provided together with goods; not applicable to pure services)
  • Bill of Lading (applicable only where physical goods are exported as part of the service deliverable)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank or transfer document proving receipt of foreign consideration)
  • Executed written service agreement identifying the non-resident client and the IP prosecution scope
  • Written declaration or certificate of non-resident status obtained before invoice issuance
  • Zero-rated invoice stating the supplier's GST/HST registration number, the nature of the supply and the export basis
  • Outbound payment confirmation (e.g., SWIFT, Wise payment receipt) showing funds remitted from a non-Canadian source

No federal or provincial mandate for real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance or digital stamping exists in Canada. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 10 million must file electronically through CRA My Business Account; others may file by paper or electronically. Records must be retained in a durable written or electronic format acceptable to CRA. BC PST registrants remit through eTaxBC and must issue invoices that separately state PST and GST/HST.

CRA may audit export zero-rating claims within the normal four-year reassessment period, extended to eight years or without time limit where misrepresentation or fraud is established. If documentation is inadequate, CRA may reassess the supply as taxable at 5% GST/HST, deny input tax credits, and assess interest. A gross negligence penalty under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act of 25% of the tax amount may apply; intentional evasion can lead to fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to eight years under section 327. Canada imposes no statutory foreign-currency repatriation deadline, but CRA may examine the timing of receipt as evidence of the supply's export character.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full and unrestricted convertibility on both the current account and the capital account for residents and non-residents. There are no foreign-exchange licences, quotas, approval gates, or repatriation limits applicable to CAD conversion, profit remittance, or capital withdrawal. A British Columbia limited partnership may freely hold foreign-currency accounts, convert CAD at market rates through any OSFI-regulated bank, and remit profits to non-resident limited partners. Remittance frictions are fiscal, not exchange-control: Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act applies to certain payments to non-residents (default 25%, reduced by treaty, e.g., 15% on interest and 10% on royalties under the Canada-United States Tax Convention; 10% on interest and royalties under the Canada-China DTA for PRC-resident limited partners), and a non-resident partner's share of the LP's Canadian-source business income may be assessed under Part I. Capital contributions into an LP are non-taxable equity events and attract no Canadian withholding. Non-resident ownership is screened under the Investment Canada Act: for a non-cultural business such as a patent prosecution agency, the WTO-investor direct-acquisition enterprise-value review threshold is approximately C$1.392 billion (2025), and a typical agency falls far below review; non-WTO and state-owned investor thresholds are generally C$5 million book value and should be monitored if a corporate limited partner is controlled by a foreign state. Sanctions screening under Canada's Special Economic Measures Act, the United Nations Act, and the Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act applies to all currency conversion and to payments routed to recognized patent offices in sanctioned jurisdictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None exist in Canada. There is no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or surrender obligation applicable to export proceeds or to receipts from exported services; patent prosecution and PCT agency services rendered to non-resident clients are exported services. The LP may retain proceeds in any currency, in any domestic or foreign account, without Bank of Canada or FINTRAC approval. The only applicable obligations are reporting-based: (i) every international electronic funds transfer of C$10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC by the Canadian financial institution within 10 days unless already reported as a large cash transaction; (ii) the LP must maintain identification records and transaction-purpose documentation for all wire transfers for at least five years under PCMLTFA record-keeping rules; and (iii) for GST/HST purposes, services supplied to non-resident clients for use wholly outside Canada are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule IX to the Excise Tax Act, so no GST/HST is charged and input tax credits on associated costs remain available.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting jointly with OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) for prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions, and FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) as the AML/ATF supervisor under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada maintains no central foreign-exchange control authority and no exchange-control statute; the Currency Act designates the Canadian dollar as the unit of account and the Bank of Canada operates an independent floating-exchange-rate policy, intervening only in extraordinary disorderly market conditions. Payments Canada owns and operates the Lynx high-value real-time gross settlement system and the ACSS retail clearing system, both overseen under the Bank of Canada's supervisory framework for systemically important payment systems.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Conditional / Low-Medium. Wise Business is the designated supplementary rail (per target payment rails) and is viable for low-value foreign-associate disbursements, translation fees, and small annuity-service payments, provided that no client trust funds are held at the EMI. Wise is not an OSFI-regulated deposit-taking institution, and Canadian Schedule I banks will not treat any foreign EMI as the primary settlement bank. CPATA by-law trust-account rules require all client funds advanced for official fees and associate disbursements to be held in a designated trust account at a Canadian financial institution; therefore the EMI cannot replace the primary Canadian bank. Foreign EMIs (Wise, Payoneer, Mercury, Airwallex) may be used for firm-own treasury flows only, must maintain an AML programme compliant with their home regulator (FCA, FinCEN, MAS, etc.), and must transmit complete FINTRAC-compatible originator and beneficiary information on every transfer. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Conditional / Low-Medium. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A British Columbia limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to Canadian corporate income tax. Taxable income is allocated to partners. For taxable Canadian corporations, the general combined federal and British Columbia rate is generally 27%: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia. The combined small-business rate is generally 11%: 9% federal plus 2% British Columbia, subject to eligibility and the applicable business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The general Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. Treaty rates commonly reduce this rate; for example, the Canada-United States treaty generally provides a 5% rate for qualifying corporate direct ownership and a 15% rate for other qualifying dividends, subject to treaty conditions. A limited partnership does not ordinarily pay dividends; distributions are analyzed as partnership distributions, allocations, interest, royalties, management fees, or other amounts according to their legal and tax character.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal transaction-value threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires arm's-length pricing and contemporaneous documentation for relevant non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. A T106 filing is commonly triggered where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions or property meet the CAD 1 million reporting threshold and other statutory conditions. Canadian local-file and master-file concepts are relevant for large multinational groups under OECD and Canadian reporting frameworks, while country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE ESR. Substance is nevertheless relevant under Canadian transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse rules, partnership anti-avoidance doctrines, sham analysis, and the requirement that expenses be incurred to earn income. The partnership should maintain Canadian operational substance proportionate to its activities, including qualified personnel, decision-making authority, premises or documented operating arrangements, books and records, client contracts, time records, and evidence that services are actually performed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, a conviction may involve a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded on summary conviction, or 100% to 200% on indictment, plus imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction or up to five years on indictment. Civil penalties, reassessment, interest, and denial of deductions may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and other participants may face personal criminal exposure where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. Ordinary partnership debts and income tax are not automatically imposed on limited partners personally, but a general partner is ordinarily exposed under partnership law. There is no automatic tax-evasion travel ban; court orders, bail conditions, passport restrictions, or immigration consequences may arise in particular proceedings.
Gross Negligence and Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: A gross-negligence penalty is generally 50% of the understated tax or the overclaimed credit or refund. Reassessment, interest, denied deductions, and additional withholding may apply.

Director personal liability: Personal liability generally requires personal participation, statutory director liability, or criminal conduct. Directors may be assessed for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST amounts notwithstanding the corporation's separate legal personality.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted withholding tax, arrears interest, late-remittance penalties, and information-return penalties. The statutory rate is generally 25% unless a valid treaty reduction applies.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions where the statutory director-liability conditions are met, subject to due-diligence defenses and limitation rules. Criminal liability may arise for knowing non-compliance.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust pricing under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, assess interest, and require extensive records. T106 penalties may apply independently.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for the corporate adjustment, but personal exposure may arise through participation in intentional tax evasion, obstruction, false records, or source-remittance failures.
Partnership Misclassification and Hidden General-Partner Exposure

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include loss of limited-liability protection under applicable partnership law, reassessment of partner allocations, civil claims, penalties for inaccurate filings, and tax treatment inconsistent with the intended structure.

Director personal liability: A limited partner may become personally exposed under provincial partnership law if it is treated as acting as a general partner or otherwise loses statutory protection. A general partner ordinarily bears unlimited liability for partnership obligations.
GST/HST Fraud and Improper Input Tax Credits

Statutory sanction: Reassessment, repayment of credits, interest, gross-negligence penalties, repeated-failure penalties, and prosecution may apply. False GST/HST statements can generate substantial civil and criminal consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST where statutory conditions are met. Individuals involved in deliberate fraud may face criminal prosecution and restitution orders.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Client-Money Misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, registration or access restrictions where applicable, forfeiture, and criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code. Criminal money-laundering offences can carry severe fines and imprisonment of up to ten years, or higher penalties for indictable proceeds-related offences depending on the charge.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, compliance officers, and employees may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, laundering, counselling, or obstruction. Seizure, forfeiture, restitution, and court-imposed release or passport conditions may occur; travel bans are not automatic.
Sanctions and Export-Control Breach

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include administrative penalties, criminal prosecution, asset freezes, prohibition orders, contract termination, and significant fines under Canadian sanctions and export-control legislation.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize or knowingly facilitate a breach may be prosecuted personally. Directors may face regulatory and criminal exposure where they ignored compliance controls or approved prohibited transactions.
Corporate Record and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Possible consequences include corporate-registry penalties, CRA records demands, reassessment based on available information, denial of deductions, administrative penalties, and prosecution for obstruction or false records.

Director personal liability: General partners and responsible officers may be personally liable for statutory offences and obstruction. A court may order production, preservation, restitution, or other compliance measures; imprisonment depends on the specific offence.
