Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

None. British Columbia law imposes no statutory minimum paid-up capital and no escrow account requirement for an unlimited liability company. The directors may issue shares for any agreed consideration, including cash, services or property; if cash is the stated consideration, it must be received by the Company before allotment, and full title to any property consideration must pass before issuance. The Notice of Articles must expressly state the unlimited liability of shareholders with respect to the Company's debts and obligations.

Reg. 296/97), including WHMIS compliance, forklift operator certification, fall protection, material handling procedures, and a hazardous materials register.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the BC Unlimited Liability Company under the Business Corporations Act (SBC 2002, c. 57) and obtain the BC incorporation number from the BC Corporate Registry. Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a nine-digit federal Business Number (BN) through Business Registration Online, paper Form RC1, or, for non-resident applicants, Form RC1A; the BN is the statutory tax identifier and carries program accounts for corporate income tax (RC), GST/HST (RT) and payroll (RP). Step 3: Register for GST/HST under Excise Tax Act s. 240(1) before making taxable supplies in Canada where worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters, or earlier on a voluntary basis to zero-rate exports and claim input tax credits. Step 4: Register for BC PST through eTaxBC under the Provincial Sales Tax Act once the ULC makes taxable retail sales of goods or taxable services in British Columbia. Step 5: Maintain the BC registered and records office, file the annual corporate registry report, and, if non-resident-controlled, appoint an attorney for service in British Columbia.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A BC ULC is a domestic corporation subject to entity-level Canadian income tax and Canadian law provides no general check-the-box election. Pass-through eligibility arises through the fiscally transparent entities provision in Article IV(7) (formerly Article IV(6)) of the Canada-U.S. Income Tax Convention, under which U.S. resident members may be taxed on their proportionate share of ULC income as though earned directly, typically supported by a U.S. Form 8832 check-the-box election (disregarded entity or partnership). Available domestic elections: (i) the GST/HST closely related corporation group election under Excise Tax Act s. 156 enabling one GST/HST registration and return for affiliated aircraft parts companies; (ii) allocation of the federal small business deduction among associated corporations under Income Tax Act s. 125; (iii) the joint venture election under Excise Tax Act s. 273 for a jointly operated aviation spares venture; and (iv) alignment of the ULC's financing and payment flows with Canadian hybrid mismatch rules (OECD BEPS Action 2) to prevent deduction/non-inclusion outcomes.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible current and capital account under a free-floating CAD regime. There are no restrictions on profit remittances, dividends, or capital repatriation for a BC ULC. The only material hurdles are tax compliance (Part XIII withholding or Part I tax as may apply) and FINTRAC cross-border wire/currency reporting thresholds (CAD 10,000 or equivalent). No central bank pre-approval or convertibility license is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender, retention timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. Export receipts from surplus commercial aircraft parts sales may be held in any permitted currency. Traders must only observe FINTRAC record-keeping and reporting obligations for currency or monetary instruments in excess of CAD 10,000, as well as usual customs declaration requirements for goods and payment processing.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange administration is effectively decentralized to the Minister of Finance, FINTRAC, and Global Affairs Canada, with no standalone exchange-control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Cross-border financial technology and electronic money institution (EMI) solutions have limited viability in Canada. A foreign EMI may not hold deposits or operate a Canadian dollar clearing account unless it is registered as a money services business (MSB) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail for lighter-value aviation parts trade, but High-risk due to enhanced EDD, liquidity concentration, and the inability to offer commercial escrow or documentary credit facilities. The primary treasury backbone should remain a Schedule I chartered bank account.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Root cause: Non-resident directors and beneficial owners with complex ULC pass-through structures

Defense evidence: Certified passport copies, two independent utility or financial proof-of-address documents, a corporate beneficial-ownership register extract, a BC-registered agent appointment letter, and a written organizational chart identifying all natural persons with economic interest.
Cross-border wire patterns to or from aviation parts suppliers in high-risk jurisdictions

Root cause: Cross-border wire patterns to or from aviation parts suppliers in high-risk jurisdictions

Defense evidence: Complete procurement trail including purchase orders, airworthiness certificates (FAA Form 8130-3 or TCCA equivalent), customs entry documents, supplier W-8/W-9 equivalents, inspection and quarantine reports, and final receipt certificates demonstrating genuine trade.
Unexpected commercial invoice values or second-hand surplus parts with low documentable value

Root cause: Unexpected commercial invoice values or second-hand surplus parts with low documentable value

Defense evidence: Independent ICAO/IASA-approved appraisals, engine or airframe serial-number traceability records, maintenance release documentation, historical serviceability history, and a consistent transfer-pricing memo prepared under OECD guidance.
Use of an Unlimited Liability Company perceived as a tax-transparent risk conduit

Root cause: Use of an Unlimited Liability Company perceived as a tax-transparent risk conduit

Defense evidence: A tax classification opinion confirming the ULC is treated as a corporation or partnership under the relevant treaty/allocation rules, CRA corporate filings for the prior calendar year, and FSMA-compliant director statements affirming no criminal or sanctioned party involvement.
High volumes of escrow account turnover and back-to-back trade settlement cycles

Root cause: High volumes of escrow account turnover and back-to-back trade settlement cycles

Defense evidence: Signed escrow agreements, source-of-wealth statements for all capital contributors, trade finance facilities documentation, authenticity reports of underlying contracts, and an internal audit trail matching each receipt to a precision-identified aircraft part.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder loans or equity contributions to the BC ULC should be routed by SWIFT MT103 with a valid UETR to a Schedule I chartered bank operational account. Transaction narratives should plainly state 'Direct investment – equity capital into BC ULC' or 'Intercompany debt funding for aviation spares distribution'. No prior Bank of Canada approval is required; however, if the capital injection originates from a non-resident controlling shareholder, it is reportable to Statistics Canada under the Canadian direct investment survey program. Verification and recording must include a corporate board resolution, a certified share/loan subscription agreement, banking source-of-funds declarations, and a FATCA/CRS self-certification for the foreign remitter.Outbound settlement for surplus commercial aircraft parts should use SWIFT MT103 with UETR for ordinary supplier payments, and Commercial Letters of Credit subject to UCP600 for high-value or program-critical transactions. CAD payables settle over the Canadian LVTS or the modern Real-Time Rail ecosystem; USD clears through the U.S. correspondent banking layer using the bank's USD concentration account. Payment milestones should be tied to receipt of inspection documentation, airworthiness certificates, and export/import customs clearance. Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraud

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 239 authorizes fines from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment; civil reassessment with daily interest and gross negligence penalty of 50%.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false returns can be jointly liable for civil restitution, face director disqualification, and in aggravated cases travel bans and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny DTA relief entirely, apply GAAR to recharacterize transactions, and impose penalties under s. 163(2) for misrepresentations amounting to 50% of the tax otherwise payable.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign T2 returns embodying artificial transactions face personal assessment for gross negligence, conspiracy, and monetary restitution; the corporate veil may be pierced for fraudulent purposes.
Transfer Pricing Deficiency

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust revenue under s. 247(2) and impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty where the net adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue; interest and restitution compound on the reassessment.

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not automatically liable for the corporate TP penalty, but they can be held personally liable for participation in negligent or reckless pricing misconduct and for resulting unlawful distributions or insolvent trading.
Withholding Tax / Part XIII Remittance

Statutory sanction: Part XIII failure subjects the payor to a tax gross-up under s. 215(2), immediate assessment against the payor, penalties up to 25% of the withheld tax, continuous arrears interest, and criminal prosecution where the omission is willful.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding taxes under s. 227.1; CRA may collect against personal assets, impose travel bans where tax is significant, and pursue personal bankruptcy in flagrant cases.
AML-Compliance at Payment Stage

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties for corporations can reach CAD 2,000,000 per violation; the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act permits asset freezing, seizure, and criminal money laundering charges.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly use unvetted cross-border payment channels can be charged with money laundering, face personal asset freezes, travel restrictions, and imprisonment for up to 10 years under criminal money laundering provisions.
