E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. A British Columbia limited partnership is fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act; the partnership itself is not a Part I taxpayer. Each partner is taxed on its allocated share of income, loss, and capital items. For GST/HST, the partnership is a deemed person and must separately register once it exceeds the small supplier threshold.

Available election forms

Canada does not have an IRS-style check-the-box election; classification follows the legal form of the entity. A BC LP cannot elect corporate treatment. Relevant elections and mechanisms include: partnership fiscal-period election under subsection 249.1(1); partnership allocation rules under section 96 as substantiated by the partnership agreement; non-resident partner income tax filing options under Part I; and elections for GST/HST that may apply to closely related corporations, but these are generally unavailable to limited partnerships. Hybrid mismatch rules under the OECD BEPS package are automatic and do not depend on a domestic entity-classification election.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Fully convertible. Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations and maintains no exchange controls on either current or capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely; inbound capital contributions, foreign direct investment, management fees, carried interest, and profit or capital repatriations for non-resident LPs are legally unrestricted, with no prior registration, approval, or licensing steps. The only frictions are tax and reporting in nature: (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) on certain distributions (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced, for example 15% or 5% under the Canada-US convention for eligible US limited partners); (ii) CRS/FATCA self-certification and Form T106 information returns for non-resident LP interests; (iii) FINTRAC large-value transaction reporting at the CAD 10,000 threshold, executed through the intermediary bank rather than by the client; and (iv) provincial securities registration consequences for the venture-capital GP under the Securities Act (RSBC 1996, c. 418) and National Instrument 31-103. None of these constitute currency-account convertibility hurdles.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender, repatriation-timeline, or mandatory conversion rules exist in Canada. Exporters, investment vehicles, and treasury entities are free to retain foreign-currency proceeds onshore or offshore, to invoice in any currency, and to convert at market rates without Bank of Canada approval. There is no statutory deadline, no prescribed conversion ratio, and no requirement to surrender export or investment receipts to a central pool. Banks apply only general FINTRAC duties, including Large Cash Transaction Reporting and Large Value Transaction Reporting at the CAD 10,000 threshold, filed by the reporting entity.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting with the Department of Finance Canada (exchange-rate policy), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI; prudential regulation of banks), FINTRAC (financial intelligence and AML/CFT oversight, including large-value transaction reporting), and Global Affairs Canada (sanctions administration under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act). Canada maintains no standalone foreign-exchange-control authority or exchange-licensing bureau; the Bank of Canada operates a free-floating national currency and does not issue FX permits, approval certificates, or conversion authorizations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium as an auxiliary rail; Low as a primary settlement account. Canada does not codify the EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence; non-bank payment service providers operate as Money Services Businesses under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17) and must transition to the Bank of Canada's Retail Payment Activities Act registration regime (registration from November 2024; enforcement from September 2025). Offshore digital EMIs (UK FCA-authorised or EEA-licensed) may lawfully supply USD/EUR virtual-IBAN, FX conversion, and cross-border disbursement rails auxiliary to a domestic tier-1 account, but: (i) they cannot create insured CAD deposits and are not eligible for Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) coverage; (ii) CAD movements through an offshore EMI remain electronic funds transfers subject to FINTRAC obligations at the Canadian receiving bank; (iii) tier-1 Canadian banks generally will not recognise offshore EMI balances as satisfying minimum-balance or capital-evidence covenants; and (iv) a BC venture-capital GP should therefore adopt a tier-1 Canadian bank account as the primary settlement node, using an RPAA-registered Canadian fintech or a FINTRAC-supervised PSP only for low-value, high-speed FX dispersals and small capital-call receipts.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (combined federal 15% + British Columbia provincial 12%) for corporations; note: the Limited Partnership is a pass-through entity and is not subject to corporate income tax at the entity level. Income is allocated to partners and taxed at their respective rates.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% for dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to non-residents. Treaty relief may reduce the rate to 5% if the beneficial owner holds at least 10% of the voting shares, or 15% in other cases (e.g., under the Canada-US Tax Treaty).

Additional penalties may apply for failure to file information returns (e.g., CAD 100 per partner per day, up to CAD 2,500). Willful failure may result in criminal prosecution.
Line 100 – Business Income Net income from business operations, including the partnership's allocated income if the corporation is a general partner. For a non-resident corporation acting as GP, report only Canadian-source business income; apply treaty provisions if applicable. Late filing penalty: 5% of tax payable plus 1% per month for up to 12 months (maximum 17%). Additional penalties for failure to file information schedules (e.g., T106 for non-arm's length transactions) up to CAD 2,500 per form.
Line 200 – Taxable Income Business income after deductions (e.g., capital cost allowance, losses carried forward). Non-resident corporations are subject to Part I tax on Canadian-source business income; may be subject to Part XIV tax on branch profits. Late filing penalty: 5% of tax payable plus 1% per month for up to 12 months (maximum 17%). Additional penalties for failure to file information schedules (e.g., T106 for non-arm's length transactions) up to CAD 2,500 per form.
Line 300 – Federal Tax Federal corporate tax calculated at 15% (or 9% for CCPC on first CAD 500,000). Non-resident corporations are not eligible for the small business deduction; federal rate is 15%. Late filing penalty: 5% of tax payable plus 1% per month for up to 12 months (maximum 17%). Additional penalties for failure to file information schedules (e.g., T106 for non-arm's length transactions) up to CAD 2,500 per form.
Line 400 – Provincial Tax British Columbia provincial tax at 12% (or 2% for CCPC on first CAD 500,000). Non-resident corporations with a permanent establishment in BC are subject to BC provincial tax; otherwise, no provincial tax. Late filing penalty: 5% of tax payable plus 1% per month for up to 12 months (maximum 17%). Additional penalties for failure to file information schedules (e.g., T106 for non-arm's length transactions) up to CAD 2,500 per form.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for intercompany services; CAD 5 million for intercompany goods. Additionally, a Master File is required if the multinational group's total revenue exceeds CAD 100 million, and a Local File is required if the Canadian entity's intercompany transactions exceed CAD 1 million (services) or CAD 5 million (goods).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the UAE, but the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles. A limited partnership must have genuine business purpose, real economic activity, and arm's length arrangements to avoid reassessment. The GP must demonstrate that it has the necessary personnel, assets, and functions to support its role.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties: 50% of the tax evaded (gross negligence penalty). Criminal prosecution: fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. The CRA may also levy interest on unpaid taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors of the GP may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes if they failed to withhold or remit amounts (e.g., payroll, GST/HST). For tax evasion, directors can be held personally liable for penalties and fines if they authorized or acquiesced to the conduct. Travel bans may be imposed for serious tax evasion, and imprisonment is possible.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the partnership and allocate income to the GP or partners based on substance. Penalties for false statements (50% of tax) and interest. Under GAAR, the CRA may deny tax benefits and impose penalties up to 25% of the tax benefit.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in the scheme. In extreme cases, criminal charges for tax evasion or fraud may lead to imprisonment. Directors may also face civil liability for breach of fiduciary duty.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) up to CAD 2 million for corporations and CAD 500,000 for individuals per violation. Criminal penalties for willful non-compliance: fines up to CAD 2 million and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be personally liable for AMPs if they were involved in the non-compliance. They may also face criminal charges if they knowingly facilitated money laundering. Travel bans and asset freezes may be imposed under the Criminal Code.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the transaction price, resulting in additional tax, interest, and penalties. Penalties for transfer pricing misstatements: 10% of the net adjustment (if the adjustment exceeds CAD 5 million or 10% of gross revenue).

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they signed false or misleading documents. In cases of gross negligence, they may face criminal charges for tax evasion.
