Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A foreign-owned ULC may elect under subsection 216.1(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada) to be treated as a corporation for all purposes of the Act, thereby preventing the application of domestic hybrid mismatch treatment that would otherwise cause the ULC to be disregarded as a separate taxpayer. Alternatively, in the absence of a timely s.216.1 election, the ULC may be treated as a flow-through entity for Canadian tax purposes to the extent of its foreign owners. Additional hybrid mismatch elections under Part XII.6 of the Income Tax Act may be relevant for cross-border arrangements involving the BC ULC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (worldwide annual taxable supplies from all taxable supplies; the 'small supplier' threshold triggering mandatory GST/HST registration for non-resident and resident entities alike)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property (including wooden puzzles) to non-resident customers are zero-rated for GST/HST under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (section 1, Part V). Services supplied to a non-resident may also be zero-rated where the services are performed for a recipient who is a non-resident and the supply is not subject to ordinary Canadian consumption rules. For zero-rating, the supplier must satisfy the two-part test: (i) the property is exported pursuant to a supply made to a non-resident; and (ii) adequate evidence of export is obtained (e.g., commercial invoice, bill of lading, customs declaration, or third-party transportation records). Online sales fulfilled from Canadian inventory to foreign buyers via platforms such as Amazon or Stripe must be supported by export documentation and treated as zero-rated, subject to CRA's administrative guidelines.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A/Canadian Export Declaration)
  • Bill of Lading
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or equivalent proof of foreign payment)

Canada does not currently mandate real-time electronic invoice clearance, pre-clearance API filing, or digital stamping for GST/HST. Invoices must include prescribed information (per section 169 of the Excise Tax Act) and electronic records must be retained in a readable and retrievable format for CRA audit. Voluntary e-invoicing via PEPPOL or similar networks is permitted, but no fiscal clearance is required.

CRA may audit export zero-rating claims and require production of all transport, customs, and payment documentation. Failure to substantiate exports results in reversal of the zero-rating, net tax reassessment, arrears interest, and penalties under sections 280 and 285 of the Excise Tax Act, including a penalty of 25% of the net tax amount for knowingly making false statements, plus gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated net tax. Fraudulent zero-rating schemes may expose directors and owners to additional civil liability and criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and Criminal Code.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full de jure and de facto convertibility for both capital and current account transactions. No pre-approval is required for direct foreign investment into a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Corporation (CA_ULC); a post-closing Investment Canada Act notification applies to WTO investors acquiring control of a Canadian business above the enterprise-value threshold (approximately CAD 5,000,000 for 2025), and the ULC is below that threshold in this atomic task. Profit remittance, dividend distribution, interest, royalty and management-fee payments to non-residents are unrestricted from an exchange-control perspective, but remain subject to Part XIII Income Tax Act non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%, potentially treaty-reduced) and, in the case of a ULC, to Canada's pass-through eligibility and the entity-classification uncertainties faced by unlimited liability corporations in cross-border tax treaty application.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no mandatory export-proceeds surrender, no statutory repatriation timeline, no conversion ratio, and no requirement to route export receipts through a domestic bank. A BC ULC manufacturing wooden 3D puzzles (ISIC 3240 / NAICS 339930) may freely invoice in CAD, USD or any other freely convertible currency, retain export proceeds in foreign-currency bank accounts, and use those proceeds directly for offshore supplier payments or physical USD clearing. The only binding obligations are FINTRAC record-keeping and reporting thresholds (including electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more), IFRS accounting recognition on the corporate balance sheet, and Canadian tax attribution in the hands of the shareholder for ULC pass-through purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada has operated a freely floating exchange-rate regime since 1951 and accepted IMF Article VIII obligations; there is no designated foreign-exchange control authority issuing convertibility licences or capital-transaction approvals. Prudential supervision of chartered banks is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act, while the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) supervises anti-money-laundering (AML) and terrorist-financing compliance under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), supervised by the Bank of Canada, now regulates retail payment service providers, complementing the Lynx/ACSS settlement architecture operated by Payments Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Viability rating: Medium-High for payment-orchestration and merchant treasury purposes only, not as the sole legal-entity banking relationship. A foreign or offshore electronic-money institution (EMI) is not a recognized deposit-taking institution under the Canadian Bank Act; it may not carry on the business of accepting deposits or describing itself as a bank in Canada. To serve a British Columbia ULC, an offshore EMI must either operate as a FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB) or, for retail payment services provided to Canadian residents, register with the Bank of Canada as a payment service provider under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Wise Canada, Stripe Treasury and similar partners may provide segregated e-money balances and merchant settlement, but final CAD liquidity and legal-entity clearing must terminate in a Canadian chartered bank account or a Payments Canada clearing participant. Digital-only onboarding is viable through major banks, but the ULC should maintain a physical-nexus correspondent relationship for high-value SWIFT flows.
