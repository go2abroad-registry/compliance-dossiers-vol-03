Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal income tax, GST/HST, and payroll administration via the My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-individuals/business-account.html); British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch — BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administration via the eTaxBC portal (https://etax.gov.bc.ca).

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated BC ULC obtains its federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) through the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) service; the BN activates the CRA program accounts (GST/HST, corporate income tax, import/export), and a GST/HST registration can be made simultaneously or subsequently on the same BN. A non-resident applicant may register using a foreign address but must disclose its non-resident status, describe its Canadian business activities, and provide a Canadian contact or agent for service of process where the CRA requires one. BC PST registration is a separate application to the BC Ministry of Finance (Consumer Taxation Branch) through eTaxBC or the equivalent paper registration form, yielding a distinct BC PST vendor number. For an exporter of zero-rated goods, voluntary GST registration is available and advisable in order to recover input tax credits on production inputs; registration becomes mandatory when worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports) exceed the $30,000 CAD small-supplier threshold.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a BC Unlimited Liability Corporation is a corporation under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia) and is therefore an entity-level taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act (Canada) in the ordinary domestic case; however, it is simultaneously eligible for fiscally transparent (pass-through) classification in cross-border structures because a US-resident owner may treat the ULC as a disregarded entity for US federal income tax purposes, and the Canada–US Tax Convention contains a dedicated Article IV(6) election that cures the resulting hybrid mismatch by deeming the ULC a resident corporation for treaty purposes. The domestic Canadian default remains entity-level taxation at federal and BC rates, while the pass-through eligibility persists for foreign-owner fact patterns until a binding treaty election or US entity classification election is made.

Available election forms

No domestic Canadian check-the-box election exists. The material mechanisms are: (i) the joint election under Article IV(6) of the Canada–US Tax Convention, effected by written notification to the CRA's International and Large Business Directorate and the equivalent US procedure, deeming the ULC a resident corporation that is not fiscally transparent for treaty purposes; (ii) the US check-the-box election on Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election) exercised by the US owner; (iii) the GST/HST voluntary registration election available to a small supplier whose taxable supplies are at or below $30,000 CAD, permitting input tax credit recovery; and (iv) any GST/HST election to register as an exporter-only registrant where all supplies are zero-rated. Because British Columbia is a non-participating province, no HST harmonization election applies and the provincial PST remains separate. Canada's anti-hybrid rules under the Income Tax Act apply to reverse-hybrid structures and structured arrangements, and the ULC treaty election serves as the principal statutory reconciliation of the Canada–US classification mismatch.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: $30,000 CAD in worldwide taxable supplies (zero-rated export supplies are excluded when computing the small-supplier threshold), measured over a single calendar quarter or cumulatively over the preceding twelve consecutive months; BC PST: registration is generally required of any vendor making taxable sales in BC, and a $10,000 CAD cumulative sales threshold applies to foreign and non-resident online sellers and marketplace facilitators — once exceeded in a twelve-month period, PST registration and collection obligations arise.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A BC ULC may zero-rate exported collectible trading cards under paragraph 1(a), Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when it makes a taxable supply of goods to a non-resident customer, possession passes to the non-resident or the non-resident's carrier, and the cards are exported from Canada 'as soon as is reasonable' after delivery (CRA audit practice generally accepts a 60-day export window; the statute prescribes no fixed period). The supply is eligible only where documentary evidence satisfies CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17-5, including the customs export declaration, transport document, and proof of the purchaser's non-resident status and receipt of the goods outside Canada. Where the ULC instead performs printing and securitization services for a foreign publisher, zero-rating may be available under paragraph 3(a) or section 23, Part V, Schedule VI, unless the service is performed in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of supply; in that case the goods-export zero-rating applies to the finished printed cards. Input tax credits attributable to zero-rated export supplies are refundable directly through the GST/HST return when the return shows a net refund position.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A filed through the Canada Border Services Agency's Canadian Export Reporting System — CERS, mandatory for export shipments valued at $2,000 CAD or more, showing commodity code, value, and exporter's Business Number)
  • Bill of Lading (carrier-issued ocean or air waybill, truck manifest, or comparable transport document evidencing physical removal of the trading cards from Canada to the non-resident's destination market)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank or foreign-exchange confirmation of payment received from the non-resident customer in convertible currency, matched against the commercial invoice, sales agreement, and the purchaser's foreign business registration evidence)

No real-time fiscal clearance, invoice approval, or digital stamping mandate exists in Canada. The CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns for registrants with annual taxable revenues above $1 million CAD and electronic T2 corporate income tax returns for all corporations in taxation years ending after December 31, 2023; e-invoicing through the voluntary Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Service (Peppol BIS format) is encouraged but not authoritative, and no prior approval by the tax authority is required for invoices. PST returns are filed through the eTaxBC portal, and all tax records, including export substantiation files, must be retained for six years.

The CRA, using CBSA export-data matching, audits zero-rating claims by testing the three-part documentary bundle and the purchaser's non-resident status; missing, late, or inconsistent documentation results in reassessment at the 5% GST rate with interest and denial of the corresponding input tax credits in the same audit cycle. Civil penalties include: (i) subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act — 25% of the tax sought to be evaded, minimum $250 CAD, for false statements made knowingly or with gross negligence; (ii) section 280 of the Excise Tax Act — $250 CAD or 50% of the falsely claimed refund or rebate, whichever is greater; and (iii) interest accruing on all clawed-back tax from the due date of the return. Criminal prosecution under section 286 of the Excise Tax Act can attract fines up to the greater of $10,000 CAD and twice the tax evaded, on conviction, with imprisonment up to five years (indictment) or two years (summary conviction). The normal four-year GST reassessment limitation period is suspended where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default is shown, and retrospective clawbacks are routinely asserted where repatriation evidence is absent within the 180-day benchmark or is contradicted by customs documentation.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating currency with full convertibility on both current-account and capital-account transactions. No central-bank approval, quantitative ceiling, or conversion license is required for: (i) paying foreign suppliers of holographic cold-foil, specialty inks, substrates, and printing equipment; (ii) remitting royalties or management fees; (iii) paying dividends to the BC ULC's non-resident shareholders; or (iv) repatriating liquidation proceeds. The only material hurdles are fiscal, regulatory-reporting, and trade-documentation in nature: a statutory 25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends (generally reduced to 15% for portfolio shareholders and 5% for U.S. corporate shareholders owning at least 10% of voting shares under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty); the obligation to report in-bound foreign direct investment to Statistics Canada under the Balance of Payments reporting program (Form 10 and the quarterly survey); the Investment Canada Act notification requirement triggered by a U.S. parent establishing or acquiring a Canadian business (a new start-up ULC engaged in trading-card printing below the WTO review threshold, approximately CAD 1.95 billion, requires only a post-closing notification, not review); and section 116 of the Income Tax Act, which applies to a non-resident's eventual disposition of shares constituting taxable Canadian property. For a ULC whose ultimate shareholders are U.S. residents, the unlimited-liability structure also invites enhanced due diligence by the Canadian clearing bank, but that is a bank credit/CDD matter, not an exchange-control impediment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law imposes no surrender requirement, no mandatory repatriation timeline, and no forced conversion ratio for export receipts. The BC ULC may retain 100% of its export proceeds in freely denominated foreign-currency accounts, hold USD working capital offshore, and net export receivables against import payables. The only compliance obligations are: (i) accounting and tax conversion of foreign-currency income into Canadian dollars under the Income Tax Act, including the possibility of a functional-currency reporting election under subsection 261(3) where the ULC's functional currency is the U.S. dollar; (ii) CBSA export documentation, namely a B13A export declaration for shipments valued above CAD 2,000 or goods on the Export Control List — collectible trading cards produced by security holographic cold-foil printing are generally uncontrolled goods and require no export permit or license; and (iii) FINTRAC cross-border currency reporting: any physical movement of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be declared, while electronic funds transfers above CAD 10,000 automatically generate bank-filed EFT reports.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central monetary authority operating within Canada's fully liberalized exchange-control framework. Exchange-control policy and capital-movement rules are formally vested in the Department of Finance Canada; prudential supervision of authorized banks and foreign-exchange dealers is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision, cross-border wire monitoring, and currency declarations are administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), with trade-related enforcement by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). The statutory anchors are the Currency Act, the Bank Act, the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and the repealed Foreign Exchange Control Act, which was never replaced by any new exchange-control regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM as an auxiliary payment sub-rail and LOW as the sole or primary treasury rail. Canada does not recognize the EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) license. Digital payment providers operate either as money-services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC or as retail payment service providers registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which is administered by the Bank of Canada. Platforms such as Wise, Airwallex, Mercury, or Relay Canadian entities can be accessed by a BC ULC and are cost-effective for low-value international vendor disbursements (USD/EUR/GBP) and for marketplace payouts, but they are not CDIC-insured banks and cannot lawfully serve as the keeper of the ULC's escrowed paid-up capital or as the principal settlement account. Accordingly, the ULC should maintain its statutory capital and primary operating liquidity at a Schedule I bank, with a Wise/Airwallex relationship layered on for payment sub-rails, provided each transfer is backed by an underlying commercial invoice and the RPAA safeguarding obligations are contractually passed through to the ULC.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM as an auxiliary payment sub-rail and LOW as the sole or primary treasury rail. Canada does not recognize the EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) license. Digital payment providers operate either as money-services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC or as retail payment service providers registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which is administered by the Bank of Canada. Platforms such as Wise, Airwallex, Mercury, or Relay Canadian entities can be accessed by a BC ULC and are cost-effective for low-value international vendor disbursements (USD/EUR/GBP) and for marketplace payouts, but they are not CDIC-insured banks and cannot lawfully serve as the keeper of the ULC's escrowed paid-up capital or as the principal settlement account. Accordingly, the ULC should maintain its statutory capital and primary operating liquidity at a Schedule I bank, with a Wise/Airwallex relationship layered on for payment sub-rails, provided each transfer is backed by an underlying commercial invoice and the RPAA safeguarding obligations are contractually passed through to the ULC.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.0% combined: 15% federal Part I general corporate income tax rate plus 12% British Columbia provincial general corporate tax rate, before allowable credits, deductions and loss utilization.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on all dividends (including deemed dividends) paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. The rate applies to gross dividends before treaty relief.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The Income Tax Act does not prescribe a stand-alone Canadian dollar threshold for preparation of Local or Master Files. Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed when the aggregate value of reportable controlled transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a tax year. Country-by-Country Reporting applies to MNE groups with consolidated annual revenue of CAD 750,000,000 or more. The CRA expects contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation whenever material controlled transactions occur; documentation should include a functional-economic analysis of the ULC's holographic cold-foil printing margins, royalty benchmarks for collectible trading-card IP, and evidence of pricing methodology.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not use a Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulation statute. Substance is nevertheless required through the common-law residence test of central management and control, the Income Tax Act's General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245), forced-reallocation provisions in s.247, and the permanent-establishment analysis embedded in tax treaties. A BC ULC that holds trading-card IP but has no employees, no active board decisions in BC, no Canadian bank accounts and no operational printing contracts is vulnerable to recharacterization as a conduit or shell, with denial of treaty benefits and retroactive Part XIII withholding.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Income Tax Evasion / Willful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act and section 380 of the Criminal Code, an offender is liable to imprisonment for up to 2 years and a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax evaded. Civilly, subsection 163(2) imposes a penalty of 50% of the understated federal tax plus interest. A criminal tax-evasion conviction also results in publication, disgorgement of proceeds, and ineligibility for future government licensing or contracts.

Director personal liability: A director who knowingly participates in, authorizes or turns a blind eye to false returns can be charged as a party to the offence under s.239(1.1). A court may impose a non-removal order or bail condition restricting international travel. For a BC ULC, shareholders have unlimited personal liability for corporate debts, and the CRA may collect assessed taxes and penalties directly from the personal assets of the shareholders where the company's separate identity is found to be a sham.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax Failures (Part XIII)

Statutory sanction: Under s.215(7) and s.227(9) of the Income Tax Act, the ULC is liable for the unremitted withholding tax, daily compound interest at the prescribed rate plus 4%, and a penalty of 10% of the unremitted amount. If the omission is grossly negligent, an additional 50% false-return penalty can be applied.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1(1) for unremitted Part XIII tax and interest. Liability follows the period during which the director acted; resignation does not extinguish liability for defaults occurring during tenure. For a ULC, the unlimited shareholder guarantee removes the corporate shield, allowing CRA to pursue shareholder personal bank accounts for unpaid withholding remittances.
Transfer Pricing / Mispricing Controlled Transactions

Statutory sanction: Section 247(3) permits the CRA to adjust taxable income to arm's length quantum and disallow tax benefits. Subsection 247(8) imposes a penalty equal to 10% of the gross transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the greater of CAD 100,000 or 10% of the aggregate reallocated amount. The penalty can be layered with gross-negligence penalties under subsection 163(2) if false materials are submitted.

Director personal liability: Directors who approved non-arm's length invoices despite red flags are exposed to personal criminal prosecution for participation in a false or fraudulent return. In ULC form, shareholders may be held personally responsible for transfer-pricing penalties and interest, and courts may require directors to testify in collection enforcement where the intercompany transaction was designed to defeat Canadian tax.
Shell Company / Substance Deficiency

Statutory sanction: The CRA may apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under s.245 to disregard the ULC's separate existence or recharacterize payments as agency or branch transactions; treaty relief is denied retroactively, adding 25% Part XIII withholding plus interest and penalties. The BC Registrar under the Business Corporations Act may strike the company from the register for abandonment, leading to forfeiture of assets and involuntary dissolution.

Director personal liability: Where the foreign parent is the true mind and management of the ULC, Canadian courts may treat the ULC as the agent of the foreign parent, making the parent and its shareholders liable for Canadian tax. For a BC ULC, shareholders must appreciate that unlimited liability makes it impossible to escape corporate tax debts through liquidation or bankruptcy; the CRA can obtain a judgment against the personal estates of directors and shareholders for the full tax arrears if corporate assets are insufficient.
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Reporting Deficiency

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, an administrative monetary penalty of up to CAD 1 million per contravention may apply, with higher penalties for continued non-compliance and up to 5 years imprisonment for criminal money laundering. Assets derived from undisclosed illegal-source cash may be forfeited.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers can be personally liable if they knowingly permit, direct or facilitate the failure to report suspicious transactions. A conviction for laundering proceeds can trigger criminal travel restrictions and a 10-year maximum sentence. Because a BC ULC has unlimited shareholder liability, forfeiture orders and CRA collection can beanchored directly against shareholder property where the entity is disregarded as a vehicle for the criminal activity.
