Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; online portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO). For British Columbia-specific indirect tax, the competent authority is the BC Ministry of Finance, BC Taxpayer Registration, administered separately from the federal GST/HST system.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign extra-provincial limited partnership that is not incorporated in Canada, the first step is to obtain a federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account (RP) via CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1. The foreign LP must provide partnership formation documents, non-resident partner details, authorized representative or agent for service in Canada, and Canadian business address if a permanent establishment exists. Registration for GST/HST is required where the LP makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier, or where it is a non-resident supplier of digital services to Canadian recipients under the place-of-supply rules; the general small-supplier threshold is CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in the current or immediately preceding four calendar quarters. If Paddle or Lemon Squeezy acts as merchant of record, the platform may be the registrant responsible for Canadian GST/HST, and the LP should confirm contractual responsibility before direct registration. For BC PST, a separate registration at BC Taxpayer Registration is required if the LP makes taxable sales or leases of taxable software or other PST-liable supplies in BC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box classification regime. A BC limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes: under Income Tax Act section 96, partnership income is computed at the partnership level but allocated to partners, and each partner is assessed on their allocable share. For foreign partners, Canadian tax exposure depends on whether the LP carries on business in Canada through a permanent establishment; the partnership is not an entity-level taxpayer. For U.S. federal tax purposes, a Canadian limited partnership may file IRS Form 8832 to elect entity classification if the Canadian entity is an eligible entity, or may be treated by default as a partnership if it has more than one member. Hybrid mismatch implications must be reviewed under Income Tax Act sections 18.4 and 18.5 and the OECD BEPS Action 2 framework where the Canadian LP is held through a U.S. partnership or corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 gross worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold). Non-resident suppliers of digital services to Canadian consumers should assess CRA place-of-supply rules; a non-resident making taxable supplies in Canada in the course of carrying on business in Canada must register regardless of the threshold if the supply is made in Canada, and merchant-of-record platforms may trigger a separate registration obligation under the Excise Tax Act.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under section 23 of the Excise Tax Act and Part V of Schedule VI, exported goods are generally zero-rated. Services supplied to a non-resident recipient may be zero-rated where the service is acquired for consumption or use exclusively outside Canada and is supplied to a non-resident; for lightly regulated cloud-based SaaS subscription services, a supply made over the internet to a foreign consumer is generally zero-rated when the ordinary residence of the recipient is outside Canada and the supply is not delivered to a Canadian address. The supplier must retain contemporaneous evidence of non-resident status, foreign delivery, and payment from abroad.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration (eManifest/B13-Customs Coding Form) — required for physical goods exports; not required for pure digital SaaS exports
  • Bill of Lading or airway waybill — physical goods only; for digital SaaS, equivalent evidence includes service delivery logs and IP/geo-location metadata
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign exchange/bank advice showing receipt of foreign-sourced consideration within the repatriation period — best-practice audit evidence
  • Subscription agreements and merchant-of-record platform reports from Paddle or Lemon Squeezy demonstrating the recipient's non-resident status and delivery of digital services outside Canada

Canada has no mandatory national real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping requirement for GST/HST invoices. CRA accepts electronic invoices provided they are accurate, complete, legible, and readily accessible. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or an approved certified software supplier when registration is active. BC does not currently impose a separate real-time electronic invoice clearance mandate for PST.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess uncollected GST/HST where the supplier cannot substantiate the non-resident status and foreign use of the service. Penalties include the false-statement penalty of 25% of any excess refund or rebate claimed, wilful evasion penalties of up to 50% of tax evaded plus interest, and potential director or partner liability. GAAR and general anti-avoidance reassessments may also apply where GST/HST registrants restructure digital sales to obtain a zero-rating benefit without genuine foreign supply.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There are no exchange-control laws, no restrictions on the repatriation of capital, and no approval mechanism comparable to the International Monetary Fund Article VIII exchange-restriction regime. Dividends, interest, royalties, and partnership distributions may be remitted to non-resident partners and shareholders without foreign-exchange approval, subject solely to income tax withholding pursuant to Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada), treaty reductions, and tax-compliance filings. For a British Columbia limited partnership, the conversion premise is that CAD-denominated liquidity may be converted to any major currency through authorised financial institutions at market rates without regulatory pre-clearance. The practical legal hurdles are documentary: banks require a valid partnership registration, beneficial ownership declarations, tax residency self-certification, and anti-money-laundering screening before permitting outward wires, but there is no sovereign exchange-ticket or surrender requirement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose any statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a central bank, to maintain export retention accounts, or to convert a mandatory proportion of export revenues into Canadian currency. A BC limited partnership may retain export proceeds in any eligible foreign-currency account, convert such proceeds at negotiated commercial rates, and deploy those funds for working capital, distributions to partners, or reinvestment. For non-resident partners, the only statutory constraints arise from tax law (e.g. withholding under Part XIII and section 212 of the Income Tax Act) and from bank-level documentation of source of funds, not from currency-exchange surrender rules. Trade settlement documentation for software subscriptions does not require a special export declaration as there is no physical movement of goods; the exporter must simply maintain accurate accounting records and issue GST/HST-compliant invoices where applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in conjunction with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada operates a liberalised foreign exchange regime; the Minister of Finance and the Bank of Canada jointly manage the exchange rate framework, but the Bank of Canada exercises no discretionary exchange-control licensing powers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) and pure digital non-bank payment providers are not recognised as 'banks' in Canada and may not accept deposits in the deeply regulated sense under the Bank Act (Canada). A non-resident EMI may lawfully assist a BC limited partnership in payment processing if the EMI is regulated in its home jurisdiction and if it does not create a 'deposit-taking' nexus triggering the Bank Act or so-called 'business of moneys lent' restrictions. The safer structure is to use a Canadian-registered MSB (money services business) that is registered with FINTRAC, or a card-payment aggregator (Paddle/Lemon Squeezy as merchant of record), rather than attempting to rely on an offshore EMI for primary treasury clearing. Legal constraints also include the Canadian prohibition on making unauthorised payment services available to Canadian clients without FINTRAC registration where the service constitutes an MSB activity, plus the provincial corporate registration threshold in British Columbia under the Partnership Act if the EMI were to take partnership-facing treasury operations. Viability tier is therefore 'Low' for full primary-banking replacement, but 'Medium' as a supplementary payout corridor for cross-border software subscription receipts.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity subscriptions and capital contributions to a BC limited partnership from non-resident partners are routed through the SWIFT MT103 message type for cross-border wire transfers. The wire must include the receiving bank’s institution number, branch/transit number, and the LP’s bank account number; the beneficiary line should read the exact legal name of the partnership as registered in British Columbia. For purpose codes, Canadian banks do not use the US Federal Reserve BOP code regime, but remittance information should state either ’Capital contribution to limited partnership’ or ’Trust/escrow deposit for partnership equity subscription’ in forwarding instructions. The bank will require proof of source of funds, typically in the form of a partner guarantee, signed capital subscription, funds-origin affidavit, and a notarised copy of the partnership agreement. Where the capital is raised through an intermediate Luxembourg/Hong-Kong entity, clear a mirror SWIFT record and show the ultimate partner on the SWIFT field 50K to avoid AML routing flags. On receipt, the LP’s bank balance will be held as CAD or, if open, as a foreign-currency account; if the capital is USD-denominated, the bank will quote a spot conversion to CAD or will keep the USD as a separate no-repatriation currency position depending on the account class. No Bank of Canada foreign-exchange approval is needed.For trade settlements and operating disbursements, the preferred clearing rails are (i) SWIFT MT103 for cross-border vendor payments and partner distributions, (ii) LVTS (Large Value Transfer System) or the real-time RTR (Real-Time Rail, where commercially available) for CAD-denominated payments to domestic vendors, and (iii) a Commercial Letter of Credit (typically documentary credit under UCP600) for any physical-equipment purchase or software-development outsourcing that requires payment against delivery of deliverables. Because the target business is a consumer micro-SaaS receiving payments through Paddle or Lemon Squeezy, the principal outbound movement is the monthly/quarterly settlement sweep from the merchant-of-record into the LP’s operating account, followed by tax payments to the CRA, subscriptions to cloud infrastructure, and distributions to partners. These recurring outflows should be executed using automated clearing house (ACH) in Canada for domestic gives, and SWIFT MT103 for cross-border. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low-to-Medium. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) and pure digital non-bank payment providers are not recognised as 'banks' in Canada and may not accept deposits in the deeply regulated sense under the Bank Act (Canada). A non-resident EMI may lawfully assist a BC limited partnership in payment processing if the EMI is regulated in its home jurisdiction and if it does not create a 'deposit-taking' nexus triggering the Bank Act or so-called 'business of moneys lent' restrictions. The safer structure is to use a Canadian-registered MSB (money services business) that is registered with FINTRAC, or a card-payment aggregator (Paddle/Lemon Squeezy as merchant of record), rather than attempting to rely on an offshore EMI for primary treasury clearing. Legal constraints also include the Canadian prohibition on making unauthorised payment services available to Canadian clients without FINTRAC registration where the service constitutes an MSB activity, plus the provincial corporate registration threshold in British Columbia under the Partnership Act if the EMI were to take partnership-facing treasury operations. Viability tier is therefore 'Low' for full primary-banking replacement, but 'Medium' as a supplementary payout corridor for cross-border software subscription receipts.
