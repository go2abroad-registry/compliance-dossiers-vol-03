Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is required under the Business Corporations Act; a BC Unlimited Liability Company (ULC) may issue shares without par value and no statutory escrow account or paid-up capital deposit is mandated for incorporation.

Funding & unlock path: No civil defense or fire safety certificate is required at incorporation. Upon securing a physical warehouse or office, obtain a fire smoke certificate and occupancy inspection under the BC Fire Code, procure structural load certification for racking systems, and complete a WorkSafeBC workplace clearance assessment before conducting packing or storage operations.

Paid-in capital rule: No minimum paid-up capital is required under the Business Corporations Act; a BC Unlimited Liability Company (ULC) may issue shares without par value and no statutory escrow account or paid-up capital deposit is mandated for incorporation.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal GST/HST registration and corporate income tax administration; online portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/gst-hst-accounts/gst-hst-account.html). British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST) is administered separately by the BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch, through the eTaxBC portal (https://etax.gov.bc.ca).

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned BC ULC must first incorporate under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act, then obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The CRA issues a GST/HST registration number (RT) upon application using Form RC1 (Request for Business Number Registration) or through My Business Account; registrants must supply the BN for all accounts, including corporate income tax and payroll. For BC PST, the entity must register as a vendor with the Consumer Taxation Branch via eTaxBC (Form FIN 418) before making taxable sales of tangible personal property in British Columbia. There is no minimum capital requirement, and non-resident ownership is permitted without special licensing; the ULC may be incorporated with non-resident directors, but the Income Tax Act imposes filing and information reporting obligations for non-resident-controlled corporations. GST/HST registration is mandatory when taxable worldwide supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in four consecutive calendar quarters; PST registration is generally mandatory before the first taxable sale in BC regardless of revenue.

However, the ULC may be treated as a pass-through or disregarded entity for U.S. federal tax purposes if a valid check-the-box election is made, creating hybrid implications subject to Canadian hybrid mismatch and foreign affiliate rules. Territorial scope: a resident Canadian corporation is subject to tax on worldwide income, while certain non-resident-owned corporations may be subject to special Part XIV rules. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (for Canadian income tax purposes, a BC ULC is a corporation resident in Canada and is taxed on its worldwide income; Canada does not recognize pass-through treatment for a domestic ULC. However, the ULC may be treated as a pass-through or disregarded entity for U.S. federal tax purposes if a valid check-the-box election is made, creating hybrid implications subject to Canadian hybrid mismatch and foreign affiliate rules. Territorial scope: a resident Canadian corporation is subject to tax on worldwide income, while certain non-resident-owned corporations may be subject to special Part XIV rules. There are no designated tax-exempt zones in British Columbia or Canadian federal law applicable to subscription-box merchandising).

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a domestic check-the-box classification system. Available statutory elections include: (i) fiscal-year-end election under section 249.1 of the Income Tax Act for a Canadian-controlled private corporation; (ii) section 216 election for a non-resident to file Canadian-source property income on a net basis; (iii) section 217 election for a non-resident to file Canadian employment income on a net basis; (iv) joint GST/HST election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act for qualifying closely-related registrants to treat inter-company supplies as made for nil consideration; (v) foreign entity classification elections under U.S. check-the-box (Form 8832) for a BC ULC, which may trigger hybrid instrument or hybrid entity rules under sections 18.6 and 20(12) of the Income Tax Act; and (vi) treaty-based relief elections under Article IV(6) of the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention when relevant.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (excluding exempt supplies and zero-rated exports) over one calendar quarter or four consecutive calendar quarters. BC PST: no general small-supplier threshold; a person making taxable sales in BC must register before the first sale unless a specific exemption applies. Importation of commercial goods into Canada also triggers GST at 5% under the Customs Act, with business importers generally able to claim an input tax credit.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for supplies of tangible personal property that are exported absolutely by the supplier to a non-resident. The subscription-box goods must be physically shipped to a destination outside Canada as soon as is reasonably possible after delivery to the carrier, must not be re-supplied into Canada before export, and must be supported by documentary evidence. Offshore services provided to a non-resident are generally zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI if they are not performed for a resident of Canada and are not otherwise consumed in Canada; however, logistics, payment-processing, and curation services physically performed in Canada for non-resident subscribers may be subject to GST unless they qualify for a specific exception. A subscription-box exporter should request buy-resell or direct-export purchase evidence to support zero-rating of goods, and should obtain an export declaration from CBSA where the shipment value or controlled goods rules require it.

  • Customs Export Declaration to CBSA (Form B13A filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System, CERS) if the export value exceeds CAD 2,000 or the goods are controlled, restricted, or require an export permit; for transshipment through the United States, a U.S. AES filing and in-bond transportation entry may also be required
  • Ocean, air, or land Bill of Lading or airway bill evidencing physical dispatch of the subscription box outside Canada
  • Commercial invoice describing the subscription-box contents, H.S. classification codes, quantity, country of origin, and export value
  • Proof-of-export supporting package, including carrier certificates of shipment, port authority bills, customs broker export summaries, courier tracking logs, and Canada Post international delivery receipts
  • Incoming remittance evidence from the non-resident subscriber or payment processor (e.g., Stripe Billing settlement report, Authorize.Net settlement, bank SWIFT/IBAN credit) demonstrating that export consideration was received in the registered foreign currency; this is not a statutory CRA requirement but is used by CRA auditors to substantiate the export-of-goods consideration

Canada and British Columbia do not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, pre-authorization of invoices, or digital stamping for subscription-box sales. There is no government-operated e-invoicing clearance platform. CRA requires registrants to issue legible invoices containing the vendor's BN or GST/HST number, transaction date, description, GST/HST amount charged, and applicable rate; electronic invoices are acceptable. For exports, a Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) filing or Form B13A may be required through CBSA for exports above CAD 2,000 or controlled goods, but this is not linked to invoice clearance; electronic records must be retained for six years.

CRA conducts targeted audits of export zero-rating claims for documentary-only exports and round-trip goods. If an exporter cannot substantiate physical export or if goods are diverted into the Canadian market after zero-rating, the supply is relinked to taxable, and the registrant becomes liable for uncollected GST/HST, statutory interest under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act, a civil penalty of not less than 10% and not more than 20% of the tax evaded under subsection 280(4), and third-party penalties in certain cases. Knowingly false invoices or repeated false export claims may lead to criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, with fines up to CAD 25,000 and imprisonment up to two years on summary conviction, or fines up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment up to five years on indictment; corporate directors and officers may be personally liable under subsection 323(1). There is no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline in Canadian tax law, but CRA uses a 180-day benchmark in audit practice to test whether export proceeds are truly repatriated; failure to repatriate within that period may cause the amount to be recharacterized as a loan, dividend, or services fee, leading to Part XIII withholding tax, Part XIII interest, transfer-pricing penalties under subsection 247(1), and even waiver of the zero-rating benefit where the export arrangement is considered artificial.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility applies to both current-account and capital-account transactions. There is no exchange-control licensing, no central-bank approval, and no advance filing requirement for equity injections, shareholder loans, subscription-box export proceeds, dividends, or royalty remittances by a British Columbia unlimited liability corporation. Non-resident ownership of a CA_ULC is permitted, and the ULC may hold, borrow, invoice, and remit in any convertible currency. Cross-border wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more trigger passive FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer reporting by the bank but create no administrative approval requirement. The only structural friction is fiscal, not regulatory: dividends or deemed dividends paid to a non-resident parent are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at the 25% statutory rate, reducible to 5% for a US corporate parent under the Canada-US Income Tax Convention, and cross-border payments to non-resident related parties must satisfy the transfer-pricing arm's-length standard of Income Tax Act s.247. Statistics Canada may require direct-investment statistical questionnaires when a non-resident holds at least 10% of the ULC's voting shares; this is statistical reporting, not a convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion regime exists in Canada. The statutory surrender timeline is not applicable, and the mandatory conversion ratio is 0%. A BC ULC may retain subscription-box export proceeds indefinitely in USD, EUR, or any other permitted currency without repatriation, conversion, or surrender deadlines. The bank will record the historical conversion rate for the ULC's books under International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) as adopted in Canada, and the ULC must translate foreign-currency balances and transactions using the Bank of Canada daily or annual exchange rates for GST/HST and income-tax filing purposes. Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) customs rules apply to imported merchandise, but no foreign-exchange condition is attached to export settlement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) acts as the monetary authority and as the supervisor of retail payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act; prudential regulation and supervision of federally chartered banks is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering and terrorist-financing surveillance is administered by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); economic and trade sanctions are administered by Global Affairs Canada and Public Safety Canada under the Special Economic Measures Act, the United Nations Act, and the Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority because the wartime Foreign Exchange Control Regulations were repealed in 1951; the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) merely defines monetary units and leaves all international payments unrestricted.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium as a complementary collection rail; Low as the sole treasury depository. Overseas digital EMIs (Wise, Revolut Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, and similar) are not Canadian banks; they may operate in British Columbia only as FINTRAC-registered money services businesses and, when performing payment functions, as retail payment service providers registered with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act. They cannot take deposits in the statutory sense, do not confer Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) coverage, and cannot issue Canadian bank guarantees or provide full trade-finance letter-of-credit confirmation services. For the CA_ULC subscription-box treasury, the recommended architecture is: Stripe Billing (operated by Stripe Technology Canada Inc., an FINTRAC-registered MSB with Canadian acquiring sponsorship) and Authorize.Net (a Visa-integrated gateway operating through a Canadian licensed acquirer) serve as the front-end recurring-charge rails; settlement funds flow by automated payout (typically daily or weekly) into the ULC's CAD/USD operational account at a Tier-1 Schedule I bank. The legacy HSBC Bank Canada network has been absorbed into RBC (transaction completed in Q1 2024), giving RBC account holders access to the former HSBC cross-border corridor for overseas supplier payments. Offshore EMI treasury concentration above CAD 150,000 should be rejected absent a parent-company undertaking or bank-issued cash balance guarantee, owing to the creditor-risk exposure inherent in the unlimited liability entity.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada – Federal: 28% basic rate (after provincial abatement of 10%) reduced by 13% general rate reduction to 15% for active business income of non-CCPCs; British Columbia: 12% general corporate rate, resulting in a combined effective rate of 27% for general active business income. For a CCPC, on the first C$500,000 of active business income, the federal rate is 9% and the BC small business rate is 2%, for a combined 11%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian non‑resident withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation (including a BC ULC) to a non‑resident shareholder.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Ontario/Canadian compliance thresholds: (i) T106 reporting is mandatory for any related‑party transaction with a non‑resident exceeding C$1 million in fair market value during the tax year (each transaction or group of transactions with the same non‑resident person); (ii) contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (commonly comprising Local File/Master File content) must be prepared and retained if the total amount of reportable related‑party transactions exceeds C$1 million and the taxpayer is in a significant position — CRA expects files when gross revenue exceeds C$50 million or assets exceed C$250 million; (iii) Country‑by‑Country Reporting (CbCR) is mandatory where the multinational group's consolidated annual revenue is at least C$1 billion.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime, but the General Anti‑Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under s.245 of the Income Tax Act and the judicial 'sham' doctrine serve as a strict economic substance police. A BC ULC with no real office, employees, board meetings, or business rationale in Canada, or one used predominantly to obtain treaty benefits, will be susceptible to GAAR reassessment, denial of treaty relief under the Principal Purpose Test (PPT), and CRA's anti‑avoidance characterisation. In addition, unlimited liability of shareholders under a ULC means that absence of economic substance can expose shareholders to the corporation's liabilities.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: On indictment: fine equal to 100% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. On summary conviction: fine of 50% to 100% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years. Civil penalties include 50% gross negligence penalty on understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who participate in wilful evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax, penalties, and interest under Income Tax Act s.227.1; in the case of a ULC, shareholders with unlimited liability may be directly liable for revenue liabilities. CRA can also issue third‑party penalties against directors and impose travel bans and asset freezes.
Shell Company and GAAR (Misuse of ULC Flows)

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under GAAR to disallow tax benefits, additional tax payable plus consequential interest, and penalties up to 50% of the tax benefit. Court may also deem the corporation a 'sham' for tax purposes, retroactively recharacterising transactions.

Director personal liability: Directors of a ULC are not normally liable for GAAR penalties, but because a ULC offers unlimited shareholder liability, shareholders may be pursued for the entire reassessed tax, interest, and penalties. Directors of a shell entity who approve fraudulent or sham transactions may be held personally responsible under directors' liability provisions for tax withholdings and may face criminal prosecution.
Transfer Pricing Penalties

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable income to the arm's‑length price, plus a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (before other reductions) where the net adjustment exceeds the greater of C$1 million or a set percentage of the taxpayer's gross revenue. Interest accrues on the reassessed amount.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign knowingly false transfer pricing documentation can be subject to civil penalties under s.163(3) (up to 50% of additional tax), and in aggravated circumstances with intent to deceive, criminal liability under s.239 (fine/imprisonment). The unlimited liability feature of the ULC can extend debts to shareholders if the corporation becomes insolvent due to a TP penalty.
AML and Anti‑Money Laundering Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) of up to C$1 million for a corporation and C$500,000 for individual directors on each violation; non‑registration penalties of up to C$250,000; in criminal cases, penalties include fines up to C$2 million and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly fail to implement AML procedures or who participate in laundering funds arising from the business are criminally liable and may be fined or imprisoned. Under the PCMLTFA, senior officers are presumed to have knowledge of the corporation's non‑compliance and may be personally penalised; for a ULC, the unlimited liability of shareholders may be invoked to cover AML administrative penalties.
