Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST Registration Online via Business Registration Online (BRO); portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services/business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must register for a Business Number (BN) with the CRA by completing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or through the Business Registration Online service. The BN incorporates the GST/HST account number (RT) once the taxpayer applies for it. Registration is mandatory if total taxable supplies in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 per calendar quarter or annually; voluntary registration is available if taxable supplies are being made in Canada. A BC ULC is treated as a corporation for Canadian income tax purposes and must obtain a separate corporate income tax account (RC) as well as GST/HST registration where applicable. Non-resident entities require a Canadian resident agent for service of process only where the ULC is extra-provincially registered; for federal GST/HST, CRA accepts the entity's foreign address and requires a Canadian contact where appropriate.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not offer Federal check-the-box classification. A ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation under the Income Tax Act and cannot elect to be fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax. For U.S. Federal tax purposes, a BC ULC may file Form 8832 to elect classification as a disregarded entity, but Canada continues to impose entity-level corporate tax and GST/HST obligations; no hybrid-mismatch election exists. GST/HST joint election under Section 156 may be available for closely related registrants to treat supplies as made for nominal consideration, but this does not alter CRA classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 per year in worldwide? Registration required once taxable worldwide supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, with the threshold assessed on supplies made in Canada by the foreign entity; for non-residents engaged in commercial activity in Canada, CRA applies a cross-border registration threshold based on Canadian-source gross revenue.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied by GST-registered persons are zero-rated when the supplier exports the goods directly from Canada or ships them to a destination outside Canada under Section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. A zero-rated export requires the supplier to retain sufficient documentary evidence that the goods were delivered to a non-resident and outside Canada. Offshore services and intangible supplies provided to non-residents may also be zero-rated under sections 7 and 21-24, Part V, Schedule VI, if the recipient is outside Canada and the supply does not relate to real estate or tangible personal property located in Canada. Dropshipping goods from Canadian distribution centers to foreign customers qualifies as zero-rated supplies if title and possession pass outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) or automated export reporting via CERS, where goods are exported from Canada with a commercial value exceeding CAD 2,000 or controlled goods
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill showing the destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and commercial invoice evidencing the foreign buyer and terms of sale
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign exchange/remittance record confirming payment from a non-resident party

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal electronic invoicing or digital stamping. Registered GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically via CRA's GST/HST NETFILE if net revenue exceeds CAD 1 million, or may file by paper otherwise. E-invoicing standards are not prescribed by CRA; any invoice must contain prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act. No API-based fiscal clearance or government e-invoice approval is currently required.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess uncollected GST/HST where a registrant fails to produce satisfactory proof of export. Misrepresentations or fraudulent claims attract penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act (not less than CAD 250 or 5% of the amount, increased to 10% for a repeat failure), gross negligence penalties up to 20% of the amount misreported, and 50% of the GST/HST evaded for third-party participation under section 239. Interest and retrospective clawbacks apply for a four-year statutory reassessment period, which is extended to three years only where the taxpayer carried on business and filed returns; fraud or wilful default opens an indefinite period. Taxpayers that knowingly obtain or use rebates or refunds to which they are not entitled face criminal sanctions and up to 200% of the refund.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The CAD is freely floating with zero exchange controls, zero approval requirements, and zero surrender obligations on any transaction type. Profit and capital remittance from a BC ULC are unrestricted by FX law, with the only residual frictions being tax-based: (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) at 25% on dividends, interest, royalties, and rents unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty (Canada-US treaty: 5% on dividends for a qualifying 10% corporate shareholder and 0% on arm's-length interest eligible for treaty benefits); (ii) ULC-specific deemed-dividend and paid-up-capital mechanics under ITA subsections 84(1), 84(2), and 84(3) that can re-characterize a return of capital as a Part XIII taxable dividend; and (iii) the absence of exchange-control approval does not relieve the ULC from reporting foreign-source revenue in CAD at Bank of Canada published exchange rates. The ULC can repatriate shareholder loans, pay intercompany royalties, and distribute dividends without Bank of Canada consent; only FINTRAC international EFT reporting thresholds (≥ CAD 10,000) apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender, forced-conversion timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio exists in Canada. Export receipts in USD, CNY, EUR, or other currencies may be held indefinitely onshore or offshore, in any freely convertible currency, without repatriation quotas. The only binding obligations are compliance-based: (i) FINTRAC reporting by financial intermediaries of international electronic funds transfers (inward and outward) of CAD 10,000 or more, with record-keeping obligations for FX transactions at the same threshold; (ii) CAD-equivalent tax reporting using the Bank of Canada daily published exchange rates or the annual average rate; (iii) OSFI collateral/margin and legal-entity-identifier obligations if hedging derivatives exceed cleared thresholds; and (iv) the ULC's own documented FX conversion policy to evidence arm's-length transfer pricing and to prevent constructive deemed-dividend or shareholder-benefit issues.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada, Ottawa) with prudential regulatory supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and financial-intelligence oversight by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada has no standalone foreign-exchange control authority; convertibility obligations are anchored in IMF Article VIII, the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements, and the free-floating CAD regime administered under the Currency Act and Bank of Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility as an ancillary collection/disbursement channel; Low-Medium as a primary treasury anchor. Foreign digital EMIs (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, PaySera) are not 'banks' under the Bank Act (Canada), are not CDIC-insured, lack direct Lynx/SWIFT membership, and are subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime administered by the Bank of Canada, effective September 2024, which requires PSP registration, risk-management, and safeguarding rules — materially increasing unlicensed offshore EMI exposure. For a Medium_Risk_Enhanced automated-dropshipping ULC, the optimal use is restricted to (i) Stripe and PayPal as merchant capture rails with mandatory daily settlement sweeps directly to the Tier-1 RBC/TD operating account; and (ii) small-value global supplier payouts where bank wire economics are prohibitive. All capital, tax, liquidity, and hedging flows must run exclusively through Tier-1 domestic banks to avoid PSP insolvency, reconciliation drift, and FINTRAC PSP registration liability.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident shareholder funding must arrive via SWIFT MT103 with a defined purpose tag. Recommended routing: USD from the shareholder's bank to RBC Bank (US), onward to RBC Vancouver (SWIFT ROYCCAT2) into the ULC USD foreign-currency sub-account, then converted to CAD under an executed FX forward/spot ticket (field 70 remittance tags: CAPT for capital contribution, EQUI for equity subscription, LOAN for shareholder loan, with a reference to the governing instrument). Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate annual value of cross-border non-arm's length transactions; at or above this level, the CRA will expect a contemporaneous Local File and availability of a Master File consistent with OECD transfer pricing guidelines. Below the threshold, a documented arm's-length analysis and basis-of-pricing records are still required for penalty protection.

Economic substance scope: Canada and British Columbia do not apply a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime; however, economic substance remains decisive under the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245), transfer pricing rules (s.247), permanent-establishment principles, and treaty limitation-of-benefits tests. An automated-dropshipping ULC should maintain substantive contracts, Canadian board decisions, and business functions; an empty shell distributing passive profits abroad will be challenged.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Mismatch and Missing Contemporaneous Documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize or redetermine prices under subsection 247(2) and impose a penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment under subsection 247(3) when reasonable efforts or contemporaneous documentation are absent; reassessment interest applies.

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not personally liable for transfer pricing adjustments unless they knowingly caused unremitted source deductions or GST/HST; where fraudulent invoices are issued, courts may pierce the corporate veil and hold directing minds jointly and severally liable.
Wilful Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act subsection 239(1), conviction may carry a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; civil gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) is 50% of understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners who direct or participate in evasion face personal criminal prosecution, travel bans, asset seizure, and extradition; the corporate veil does not shield intentional fraud.
Money Laundering Through Automated Payment Rails

Statutory sanction: Under the Criminal Code subsection 462.31, money laundering is indictable with imprisonment up to ten years; FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties for unregistered activity or failed reporting can reach C$500,000 per contravention, and bank accounts will be frozen or closed.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers who knowingly facilitate payment flows face criminal liability, disgorgement orders, and personal forfeiture; courts may lift the ULC veil to confiscate assets in which the promoter holds a beneficial interest.
Artificial Non-Residence, Shell Substance, and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The general anti-avoidance rule under section 245 denies the tax benefit and imposes interest; CRA may also reassess on a substance-over-form basis, deny treaty benefits under limitation-of-benefits clauses, and apply the 10% transfer pricing penalty where profits are improperly shifted.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false declarations may be liable for penalties and, in cases of fraudulent misrepresentation, personal restitution; travel bans and director disqualification can be imposed under BC corporate law and criminal tax proceedings.
