Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — My Business Account (canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); for British Columbia provincial sales tax, the BC Ministry of Finance Consumer Taxation Branch via eTaxBC (etaxbc.gov.bc.ca). BC provincial income tax is administered by CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident-owned British Columbia limited partnership must (1) register the LP with the BC Corporate Registry or with one corporate/registry agent, obtaining provincial registration; (2) apply to CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) using Form RC165B – Non-Resident Business Registration, or through CRA Business Registration Online, identifying the partnership legal name, fiscal period, and all partners; (3) open a separate GST/HST account (RT) once the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold is exceeded or intentionally registered; and (4) register for BC PST through eTaxBC when carrying on a PST-taxable business in BC. The CRA will issue the BN, and the BC Ministry of Finance will issue a separate PST number without a minimum capital requirement.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no direct check-the-box election. A BC limited partnership is automatically fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes under Income Tax Act section 96; the LP itself is not a taxpayer. Available elections and mechanisms include: (i) a partnership election under subsection 96(5.1) respecting fiscal periods; (ii) a non-resident section 216 election to file a discretionary Canadian income return on Canadian-source rental or remuneration income instead of Part XIII withholding; (iii) joint elections under subsection 97(2) for rollover of property into the partnership; and (iv) for U.S. federal purposes only, IRS Form 8832 may classify the Canadian LP as an eligible entity, but this does not alter Canadian CRA treatment. Hybrid mismatch limitations apply under the deductible-proper-allocation anti-hybrid rules in Income Tax Act subsection 18(4), and the Canada-BC inter-provincial corporate allocation is not applicable to a flow-through LP.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.05%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (including supplies made in Canada) in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters triggers mandatory GST/HST registration as of the supply that exceeds the threshold; BC PST has no economic threshold and registration is required before making any PST-taxable supply in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For tangible goods exported from Canada, a supply by way of sale is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part I of the Excise Tax Act if the supplier exports the goods as soon after the sale as is reasonable and retains prescribed export evidence. For services supplied to a non-resident, zero-rating is generally available under Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 if the service is not performed in connection with real property situated in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property located in Canada at the time of performance, and not supplied to an individual present in Canada. Affiliate media buying and lead-generation services supplied to a foreign advertiser may qualify as zero-rated offshore services where the service contract is with a non-resident, the recipient is not a GST/HST-registered Canadian business, the service is not consumed by a Canadian-based consumer, and the supplier maintains documentary proof that payment originates from outside Canada. Advertising directed at the Canadian market or benefiting Canadian persons would be taxable in Canada.

  • Commercial invoice (in English or French, with full details of goods/services)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill with clear export destination
  • Canadian customs export declaration (CBSA eExport/B13A) where controlled, regulated, or valid-export-reporting goods are concerned
  • Proof of destination customs entry / import declaration where available

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or pre-clearance of invoices. GST/HST invoices may be issued in paper or electronic form provided they contain the prescribed particulars. Certain registrants with annual taxable supplies over CAD 1 million must file GST/HST returns electronically using CRA-approved software / GST/HST NETFILE. BC PST returns are filed through eTaxBC. Records must be retained in a readable format and made available to CRA on demand; CRA supports eAudit and digital record extraction through its Standard Audit File.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated supply as taxable if the exporter fails to supply sufficient evidence of export, foreign service delivery, or non-resident recipient status. The normal GST/HST assessment period is four years from the filing deadline, but a misrepresentation or fraud leaves the timeframe unlimited and permits full clawback of claimed input tax credits or refunds. Civil penalties may include late-filing and remittance penalties, monthly interest on arrears, a 50% gross negligence penalty on misstated net tax under Excise Tax Act section 285.1, and prosecution for wilful GST evasion under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, which can result in substantial fines and imprisonment. The 180-day benchmark in this dossier is an administrative evidentiary rule of thumb for producing reconciled export/foreign-remittance documents; Canada does not impose a fixed statutory currency-repatriation deadline, but delayed repatriation may attract transfer-pricing adjustments on intercompany receivables.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current- and capital-account convertibility (IMF Article VIII status). The Canadian dollar floats freely; no federal or BC approvals are required for inbound capital contributions, LP distributions, profit remittances, or cross-border loan repayments. The sole practical hurdles are documentary: source-of-funds validation by the receiving bank, Part XIII withholding tax (25% base, treaty-reduced) registrations for non-resident limited partners, and FINTRAC reporting of transactions above CAD 10,000. BC imposes no separate repatriation permit or conversion-duty framework.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada has no statutory timeline for surrender and no mandatory conversion ratio for media-buying/affiliate export receipts. Foreign-currency proceeds may be retained in FX-denominated bank accounts indefinitely; only general bookkeeping, GST/HST attribution for BC-based supply of advertising digital services, and PCMLTFA record-retention rules (5 years) frame the operational requirement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign-exchange administration is functionally delegated to the market framework under the Currency Act (RSC 1985, c. C-52), with bank prudential oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High for Wise and High for Payoneer as collection/disbursement nodes, Low-Medium for Mercury. Legal constraints: offshore EMIs cannot operate as deposit-taking banks in Canada; any Canadian-facing conduit must be registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) under PCMLTFA or partner with a Schedule I/II bank for CAD clearing. Funds held at EMIs are not CDIC-insured and are not settled through Payments Canada LVTS/ACSS, making them complement (not substitute) for a bank treasury wrapper. HSBC Bank Canada has been consolidated into RBC (2024), so no separate HSBC EMI/banking tier is available.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A British Columbia limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to corporate income tax. Canadian-resident corporate partners ordinarily face a combined federal and British Columbia general corporate rate of 27% (15% federal plus 12% British Columbia). An eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate on qualifying active business income within the applicable federal and provincial business limits. Non-resident corporate partners are generally taxable on their Canadian-source income under applicable Canadian rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding generally applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident to a non-resident. A limited partnership ordinarily does not pay dividends; distributions are generally partnership distributions and must be analyzed as allocations, returns of capital, interest, royalties, management fees, capital gains or other amounts. Dividends paid by a Canadian corporate partner or subsidiary may qualify for treaty reduction, commonly 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership and treaty conditions and 15% in other treaty cases, including common Canada-United States treaty outcomes.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not prescribe a universal transaction-value threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires reasonable contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, and CRA may request it. Form T106 generally applies where the detailed aggregate reporting conditions are met, commonly including more than CAD 1,000,000 of reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions. A transfer-pricing penalty generally becomes relevant where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and CAD 5,000,000, unless the taxpayer made reasonable efforts and maintained adequate documentation. Canada does not have a separate mandatory Master File and Local File filing regime equivalent to all OECD jurisdictions, but OECD-style functional, comparability and financial support is prudent.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime applicable to all entities. Substance is relevant through transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty interpretation, beneficial-ownership principles, sham and agency analysis, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules and permanent-establishment concepts. The partnership should maintain genuine decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, commercially rational contracts, market-risk assumption, payment-control evidence, media-buying records, customer and platform records, bank-account control and accounting substance. A nominal Canadian partner or general partner with no meaningful functions may increase reassessment, treaty-benefit, agency, residency and characterization risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act offences may result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to five years, assessment of the underlying tax, interest and civil penalties. Gross-negligence penalties are generally 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: Partners, the general partner, officers and individuals who participated in, assented to or acquiesced in the offence may face personal criminal exposure. Directors of a corporate general partner may face derivative liability where statutory elements are proven. Civil assessments, restitution, asset seizure and prosecution may follow; a travel ban is not an automatic tax sanction but may arise through bail, court process or immigration consequences.
Unremitted Source Deductions and Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the full unwithheld amount, arrears interest and penalties. Failure-to-deduct penalties can generally be 10% of the required deduction and 20% for repeated or knowingly negligent failures, with additional failure-to-remit and information-return penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions under the director-liability provisions, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. The general partner may be directly liable under the partnership structure. Criminal prosecution is possible for wilful evasion.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny deductions, impute benefits, assess interest and impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory materiality and reasonable-efforts conditions are met. Repeated failures may increase audit scope and penalty exposure.

Director personal liability: Personal criminal liability generally requires participation in intentional misconduct or an offence; ordinary transfer-pricing reassessments ordinarily remain liabilities of the taxpayer and partnership or corporate partner. Directors may nevertheless face derivative liability for source deductions, GST/HST or criminal conduct.
GST/HST Fraud and Improper Input Tax Credits

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny credits, assess GST/HST, interest and civil penalties. False-statement or gross-negligence penalties can apply, and tax fraud may result in criminal fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST of a corporation under applicable director-liability provisions. Individuals involved in fraudulent invoicing or tax-credit schemes may face prosecution, restitution and asset forfeiture.
Payroll and Worker Misclassification

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess source deductions, CPP, EI, taxable benefits, interest and penalties. Payroll remittance failures may attract escalating penalties and criminal consequences for wilful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted employee source deductions, subject to due-diligence defenses. Responsible individuals may face criminal liability for deliberate evasion.
Shell Company, Sham Partnership or Beneficial-Ownership Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the general anti-avoidance rule, deny treaty benefits, recharacterize transactions, assess tax and penalties, and refer conduct for criminal investigation. Corporate records and beneficial-ownership failures may also trigger British Columbia corporate compliance consequences.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face civil restitution, criminal fines, imprisonment and asset forfeiture where fraud, laundering or tax evasion is proven. A travel restriction is fact-dependent and may be imposed by a court or arise from bail conditions; it is not an automatic corporate-tax penalty.
Anti-Money Laundering and Suspicious Payment Activity

Statutory sanction: Applicable reporting entities may face FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders and criminal prosecution under proceeds-of-crime and money-laundering legislation. A media-buying partnership is not automatically a money services business, but its status must be assessed if it performs regulated payment, remittance or virtual-currency functions.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers and individuals may face criminal imprisonment, fines, forfeiture and restitution. Banks and payment providers may freeze or close accounts and file suspicious transaction reports. Travel restrictions may occur in criminal proceedings but are not automatic.
Unreported Foreign Assets and Cross-Border Information Failures

Statutory sanction: Late, missing or false T106, T1134, T1135 and related filings may generate fixed daily penalties, extended reassessment periods and gross-negligence or criminal penalties. CRA may reassess income and deny unsupported foreign tax credits or deductions.

Director personal liability: Liability generally rests with the relevant taxpayer or partner, but individuals can face criminal exposure for knowing participation in false returns or concealment. Directors may be liable where corporate statutory obligations and the relevant director-liability provisions apply.
Prohibited Distributions and Insolvent Conduct

Statutory sanction: Distributions may be challenged, clawed back or recharacterized under insolvency, fraudulent-conveyance and partnership principles. The partnership and general partner may remain liable for unpaid tax, creditor claims and statutory remittances.

Director personal liability: Limited partners ordinarily have limited liability if they do not take part in control and comply with the partnership agreement and governing law. The general partner has primary management and liability exposure. Directors of a corporate general partner may face personal liability for statutory remittances and improper conduct; imprisonment and travel restrictions require separate criminal or court proceedings.
Improper Revenue Recognition and Affiliate Advertising Fraud

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include reassessment, denial of expenses, GST/HST adjustments, civil claims, contractual damages, consumer-protection enforcement and criminal fraud charges where intent and material deception are proven.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers and partners involved in deliberate fraud may face personal civil restitution, damages, fines, imprisonment and forfeiture. Ordinary accounting errors generally create entity-level tax and contractual exposure rather than automatic personal liability.
