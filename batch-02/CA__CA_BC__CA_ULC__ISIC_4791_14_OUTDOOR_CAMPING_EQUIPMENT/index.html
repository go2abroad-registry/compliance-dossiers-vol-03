Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity with Canadian taxable activities must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and register for a GST/HST account. For a BC Unlimited Liability Company (ULC), registration begins with CRA's online 'Registering a Business with the CRA' process via My Business Account, or by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts). Required supporting documentation generally includes: certified proof of incorporation or equivalent foreign registration, the company's registered office address in Canada, the name and contact details of all directors/resident agents, and the expected date of first taxable supply. The BN is issued in real time for online applications; a GST/HST program account suffix (RT) is added upon registration. The ULC must also register with the British Columbia Ministry of Finance for PST (Provincial Sales Tax) if its taxable sales to BC customers exceed CAD 10,000 per calendar year.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible (Note: For Canadian federal and BC income tax purposes, a BC ULC is a taxable corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada), subject to entity-level tax at the federal and provincial rates. However, the ULC structure is 'Pass_Through_Eligible' because it is eligible to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. federal tax purposes under Treasury Regulations §301.7701-3, while remaining a corporation for Canadian tax purposes. This hybrid classification may trigger hybrid mismatch rules and requires careful transfer pricing and withholding documentation.)

Available election forms

Canadian tax law does not provide a check-the-box election comparable to the U.S. rules. The ULC's pass-through eligibility arises from U.S. Treasury check-the-box elections (Form 8832), which must be filed with the IRS. For Canadian tax purposes, no statutory election is available to recharacterize the ULC as a partnership or sole proprietorship; the Income Tax Act (Canada) mandates corporation treatment. However, non-resident shareholders may invoke the application of a relevant tax treaty and file with CRA a notice under subsection 216(1) (election to file a net income return instead of Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on certain Canadian-source income) if applicable, and the ULC may elect under section 1106 of the BC Business Corporations Act to be an unlimited liability company for foreign classification purposes. Any hybrid mismatch election must comply with Part XI of the Income Tax Act (Canada) and OECD BEPS Action 2 recommendations.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in global taxable supplies for GST/HST registration (including e-commerce remote sales threshold); BC PST remote seller registration threshold is CAD 10,000 in taxable sales to BC customers per calendar year.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate exports of goods if the property is exported 'as soon as a reasonable time after the supply' and the supplier retains documentary evidence proving exportation (Excise Tax Act, section 22 and sched. VI, Part V). For an online retailer, zero-rating applies to goods sold to a non-resident buyer where the goods are shipped from Canada to a destination outside Canada by the supplier or the buyer's agent. Certain services supplied to non-residents are also zero-rated (sched. VI, Part V, section 7), including services directly in connection with tangible personal property sold to a non-resident for export, provided the services are performed in Canada and the property is exported. Supply of digital goods to non-residents may be zero-rated as made outside Canada if the usual place of consumption is outside Canada and the supplier makes an export record.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Customs Invoice / Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) equivalent; for shipments valued under CAD 2,000, a carrier-generated low-value report suffices)
  • Bill of Lading (or air waybill / Cargo Control Document evidencing international carriage)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or equivalent bank wire / payment service provider reconciliation showing receipt of foreign currency funds from the non-resident purchaser)
  • Commercial Invoice with purchase order evidence and agreed Incoterms
  • Proof of sale and payment record (Stripe/PayPal settlement reports, payment gateway transaction statements)
  • Carrier shipping logs and tracking history showing the goods left Canada
  • Packing list or detailed product description for matching to customs declaration

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API e-invoicing, or digital stamping regime in Canada. CRA requires filing of GST/HST returns electronically (NETFILE/GST/HST NETFILE) for registrants with a gross annual revenue over CAD 1.5 million; voluntary e-filing is otherwise permissible. Invoicing must contain standard GST/HST information (customer name, vendor name, registration number, tax charged, date, description), but there is no national pre-authorization or clearance mechanism. BC PST does not require any electronic fiscal clearance; however, audit-readiness requires supported transaction records in either paper or electronic format upon CRA or BC Ministry of Finance request.

CRA rigorously audits zero-rated export claims. If the required export documentation is not substantiated, the supply is deemed taxable in Canada and the registrant is liable for GST/HST not collected, plus interest and penalties under the Excise Tax Act. Reassessments may extend to eight years where gross negligence is alleged. Specific penalties include: (i) a late-filing penalty of up to 8% of the unreported tax; (ii) failure-to-collect penalty of up to 10% of the unremitted tax; (iii) gross negligence penalty of 25% of the understated tax (50% under certain ITA provisions); and (iv) fines or imprisonment for knowingly making false statements (Excise Tax Act, section 332; Income Tax Act, section 239). BC PST clawback risks similarly apply to exemption certificates for exports; PST may be reassessed on unauthorised exemptions with interest and penalties of up to 25% of the tax due. CRA and border agencies share export data through the eManifest and Royal Canadian Mounted Police trade audit units, elevating scrutiny for high-volume e-commerce exporters.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions; CAD is freely traded and the Canadian dollar is not subject to exchange control licensing. Profit remittances, dividends, shareholder loans, and capital repatriation from a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) are unrestricted; the only frictions are (i) subpart/Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada), (ii) solvency and authorization requirements under the BC Business Corporations Act, and (iii) FATCA/CRS and FINTRAC reporting for high-value transfers. No exchange-rate approval quota, currency spread mandate, or conversion license applies to an online retail exporter of outdoor camping equipment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender requirement exists in Canada. An exporter may hold USD, EUR, or other foreign currency receivables indefinitely in a domestic or foreign bank account; there is no mandatory conversion ratio or prescribed repatriation timeline. Regulatory thresholds are reporting-only: Canadian financial institutions and MSBs must file Electronic Funds Transfer Reports with FINTRAC for outgoing or incoming international EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more, and the business must maintain underlying trade documents (commercial invoices, bills of lading, customs export declarations) for verification in case of OSFI/FINTRAC audit.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central bank; Canada has not maintained exchange control since the repeal of the Exchange Control Act in 1951. The Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) oversee monetary and prudential policy; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers cross-border AML surveillance. Canada is an IMF Article VIII member, requiring no central-bank approval for current or capital transactions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-High, subject to Canadian licensing. Canada has no EU-style EMI passport; a 'fintech EMI' must be either (i) a federally-chartered bank under the Bank Act, (ii) an OSFI-regulated trust company, or (iii) a FINTRAC-registered Money Services Business (MSB), which requires a compliance programa CAD compliance program, a chief anti-money laundering officer, EFT reporting, and registration under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, and Currencycloud can partner with a Schedule I Canadian bank as clearing correspondents, but they cannot open or maintain settlement accounts in BC without a Canadian MSB or bank license. For Stage 4 treasury, using a Tier-1 bank as the anchor account with a FINTRAC-registered EMI as a secondary collection/payout rail is the viable structure; using an unregistered foreign EMI as the sole treasury rail is a legal blocker for BC ULC compliance.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound routing for a ULC: (1) the non-resident shareholder sends SWIFT MT103 from its home bank to the BC ULC's RBC/TD/BMO account; (2) field 50K must name the true ordering customer (the shareholder, not an intermediary); (3) field 59 must name the ULC with its Vancouver, BC address; (4) clearing is typically CAD-denominated with conversion executed at the receiving bank or a pre-agreed FX contract; (5) the payment reference line should state 'Shareholder capital contribution - Equity injection - no repayment obligation for [date] resolution'; (6) for shareholder loans use 'Loan under Subscription/Master Facility Agreement dated [date]'; (7) for amounts above CAD 10,000, retain the SWIFT gpi UETR, bank transaction reference, and confirm FINTRAC EFT threshold reporting; (8) no federal exchange-control permit is required, but the transaction must be recorded in the ULC's minute book and disclosed in the annual BC public accounts return if exceeding director-signing thresholds.Primary clearing rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 single-payment transfers for supplier payables and logistics settlements, executed via corporate internet banking with gpi tracking; (2) MT101 pre-authorisation for batch payroll or concentrated mass payouts; (3) commercial letters of credit (under UCP600, through RBC/BMO trade desks) for container-load imports from Asia where the supplier requires LC payment; (4) Payments Canada Lynx real-time high-value settlement for domestic CAD payouts; (5) card-network batching (Visa Direct/Mastercard MoneySend) for small refunds and B2C chargebacks; (6) stablecoin/blockchain rails are not permissible as primary settlement for the Canadian treasury because they lack Bank Act recognition and OSFI reconciliation.
