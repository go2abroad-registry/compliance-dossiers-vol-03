E-government filing portal

A BC unlimited liability corporation obtains a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN), which serves as the statutory TIN and as the base for program accounts, by: (1) completing incorporation or extra-provincial registration under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act; (2) filing a Business Registration Online application, Form RC1, or contacting the CRA Business Registration hotline; (3) activating the required CRA program accounts, including the corporate income tax account and GST/HST account where taxable Canadian supplies are made; and (4) registering for BC PST through BC eTaxBC before making taxable retail sales in British Columbia. Non-resident shareholders and foreign corporations may hold ownership of a BC ULC; a Canadian agent may assist with registration but is not statutorily required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box regime under the Income Tax Act. A BC ULC is a separate legal person and is taxed at the entity level for Canadian federal and provincial corporate income tax, and is a registrant legal person for GST/HST purposes. The unlimited liability feature does not create Canadian partnership, trust, or other flow-through status. A foreign tax election, such as the U.S. Form 8832 check-the-box election, may classify the ULC as disregarded or as a partnership for foreign tax purposes, but such election does not alter CRA classification. Under the Excise Tax Act, a person who is a small supplier may elect to become a GST/HST registrant before exceeding the CAD 30,000 threshold in order to claim input tax credits and zero-rate exports. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules apply by default and do not provide an elective exemption; they may disallow deductions or recharacterize payments involving hybrid entities or instruments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters unless the person voluntarily registered; PST: no broad equivalent of the GST small-supplier threshold, and registration is generally required before making taxable retail sales of goods or taxable services in British Columbia, with special considerations for out-of-province and non-resident sellers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate the supply of custom mechanical keyboard keycaps under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the tangible personal property is supplied to a non-resident purchaser and is physically exported from Canada as part of the supply. The registrant must maintain contemporaneous documentary evidence that the goods were delivered outside Canada, shipped by the vendor or its agent, or placed in the custody of a common carrier for export. Services performed on tangible personal property for a non-resident may also be zero-rated where the goods are exported. Zero-rating permits the exporter to claim all allowable input tax credits for GST/HST paid on inputs and to claim net tax refunds to the extent credits exceed output tax.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A) or equivalent electronic export declaration
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill clearly evidencing export from Canada to a foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / foreign-exchange record or bank statement showing payment received from the non-resident customer
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order identifying the non-resident buyer and Canadian seller
  • Packing list, cargo manifest, or carrier contract
  • Proof of customs clearance in the destination country or transit/re-export documentation, if applicable

Canada does not require real-time fiscal clearance, transaction-level e-invoicing validation, API-linked e-reporting, or digital stamping. The CRA accepts electronic invoices, books, and records; e-invoicing using standards such as Peppol BIS is voluntary. GST/HST returns are self-assessed and filed through CRA My Business Account or GST/HST NETFILE, while BC PST returns are filed through BC eTaxBC. Registrants must retain complete books, records, invoices, shipping documents, and export evidence for at least six years in a form accessible to the CRA.

CRA may disallow zero-rating and claw back input tax credits, net tax refunds, and earlier GST/HST recoveries if export documentation is incomplete, missing, or inconsistent. Reassessment may occur within four years from the original notice of assessment, or without time limit where a misrepresentation has been made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to culpable neglect. Penalties include statutory interest on arrears, failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties up to 25% of unpaid tax, and a false-statement penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated net tax. GST/HST collected or collectible is deemed held in trust for the Crown, and directors may be personally liable for uncollected, unreported, or unremitted GST/HST. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims may also lead to criminal prosecution and fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations; the Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible and fully deliverable for both current and capital account transactions. The Currency Act, Bank of Canada Act, and Income Tax Act impose no exchange controls, conversion permits, registration requirements, or repatriation approvals on foreign direct investment into a British Columbia ULC. Profit and dividend remittances to non-resident shareholders are legally unrestricted, subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%; reduced to 15% generally, or 5% for a US corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of voting shares, under the Canada-US Tax Treaty) and to the ULC's obligation to withhold on distributions. No central bank notification or 'purpose code' approval is required for inward equity injections or outward remittances; however, the remitting financial institution must satisfy FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting for CAD 10,000+ in a 24-hour period and applicable sanctions screening.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no surrender requirement, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio on export proceeds. A BC ULC exporting double-shot PBT keycaps may retain receivables in any currency in any institution, domestic or foreign, without Bank of Canada or FINTRAC consent. The sole statutory duty is reporting: physical cross-border currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and FINTRAC, and financial institutions must file Electronic Funds Transfer Reports for wire transfers of CAD 10,000+ per 24-hour period. Statistics Canada balance-of-payments reporting may also apply above thresholds; this is statistical, not a surrender or exchange-control requirement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - central bank and monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) - prudential bank supervision; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) - AML/CTF supervision and reporting; Department of Finance Canada - exchange-control policy lead (Canada maintains no active capital controls).

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (15% federal general rate + 12% British Columbia general corporate income tax rate, applied to a BC ULC as a taxable Canadian corporation for Canadian tax purposes)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by the BC ULC to non-resident shareholders. A ULC that is fiscally transparent in the United States but a corporation in Canada remains subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding on actual dividend distributions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the end of the corporation's fiscal year. For a December 31 year-end, the deadline is June 30 of the following year. The final balance of tax is due by the same six-month deadline, subject to instalment schedules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate annual cross-border non-arm's-length transaction value triggers mandatory contemporaneous Transfer Pricing Local File documentation and T106 filing; Master File and Country-by-Country Reporting are required for MNE groups meeting the applicable OECD threshold on consolidated revenue.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain an offshore Economic Substance Regulations regime, but substance requirements are enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under section 245 of the Income Tax Act, the beneficial-ownership doctrine, and treaty-shopping jurisprudence. A BC ULC operating without a substantive Canadian business, employees, premises, or independently contracted strategic functions will be denied treaty benefits and may be recharacterized as a sham or artificial conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239(1) imposes criminal fines of between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil penalties include reassessment, daily compounding interest, and a gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax under section 163(2).

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders of a BC ULC are jointly and severally liable for corporate debts by reason of the unlimited liability structure. Directors who knowingly participate in evasion face criminal fines, imprisonment, asset seizure, and travel bans in cross-border enforcement with Canada and the United States.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalization Violation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of taxable income, disallowance of interest deductions, and a transfer-pricing penalty under Income Tax Act section 247(3) equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where reasonable efforts were absent. Late-documentation penalties and compound interest may materially increase liability, and GAAR may be invoked for abusive hybrid or treaty arrangements.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted Part XIII withholding taxes under section 227.1. Under the BC ULC unlimited liability rule, shareholders and directors remain fully exposed to CRA collection, which may include travel restrictions, director disqualification, and enforcement against personal assets.
Shell Company / Sham / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR may recharacterize the structure and deny treaty benefits; false statements attract penalties under section 163(3), and gross negligence attracts a 50% civil penalty. A sham finding can also support criminal tax evasion charges under section 239(1).

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false declarations are personally exposed to criminal prosecution and civil restitution. A BC ULC's unlimited liability permits CRA and creditors to reach shareholders' and directors' personal estates, while border and travel enforcement measures may be triggered.
AML/ATF and Corporate Registry Non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the BC Business Corporations Act, failure to comply with transparency reporting can result in fines up to C$60,000 and imprisonment for up to two years. Federal charges under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may add fines of C$250,000 for individuals and C$500,000 for institutions.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for corporate registry and AML offences, and the ULC's unlimited liability exposes shareholders to monetary sanctions. Serious non-compliance may result in travel bans, asset freezes, director bans, and extradition risk under mutual legal assistance treaties.
