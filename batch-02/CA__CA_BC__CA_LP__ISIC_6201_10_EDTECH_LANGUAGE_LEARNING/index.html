Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital for a British Columbia limited partnership: the Partnership Act prescribes no minimum contribution and no escrow/deposit is required at Corporate Registry. The limited partnership agreement fixes each partner's capital contribution (cash, property, or services), and the contribution must be actually made for the limited-liability shield to attach to limited partners.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); official portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses.html); supplementary provincial authority for PST: BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch via eTaxBC (https://etax.gov.bc.ca/).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the limited partnership under the BC Partnership Act with the BC Registrar of Companies (BC Corporate Online) — file the Declaration of Limited Partnership listing general and limited partners, capital contributions, and the BC LP registration number. Step 2: Obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) for the partnership by filing Form RC1 or using Business Registration Online (via BCeID); the BN is the 9-digit statutory TIN. Step 3: Open CRA program accounts on the BN — GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), and import/export (RM) if physical goods are ever shipped; the partnership is the registrant even though income tax is borne by partners. Step 4: Register for BC PST with the Consumer Taxation Branch via eTaxBC (My PST Account) upon the first taxable sale in BC. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a BC Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96: income/loss is computed at partnership level and allocated to, and taxed in the hands of, the individual/corporate partners; the partnership itself files an information return (T5013) and is a separate 'person' for GST/HST and PST purposes.

Available election forms

No Canadian 'check-the-box' entity election exists; characterization follows statutory form. Available elections: (i) ITA s. 97(2) joint election by partners to transfer property into the LP on a tax-deferred rollover; (ii) ITA s. 96(3) election to treat a partner's share of identified partnership property as a separate and distinct property for disposition planning; (iii) GST/HST quick-method election (ETA s. 257) for registrants with annual revenues <= CAD 400,000; (iv) ETA s. 156 election for closely related corporations (group-level planning; not available to the LP itself); (v) for foreign partners, the LP remains flow-through for Canadian purposes while the partner's home jurisdiction may independently elect to treat the LP as opaque (e.g., US Form 8832 check-the-box); hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 12.8, 18.2, 18.3) apply automatically with no elective override.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is a freely convertible currency under a market-based floating exchange rate regime. Canada maintains no overarching foreign exchange control legislation on current or capital account transactions. Movement of inbound capital, dividend distributions, and profit repatriation are unrestricted, subject to standard corporate tax compliance and, for non-resident investors, Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory rate of 25% unless reduced under an applicable tax treaty. The Bank of Canada does not impose convertibility approvals, surrender requirements, or residual limitations on profit remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose statutory export proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion ratios. Under the Currency Act and official business practices, exporters are free to retain their export proceeds in Canadian dollars or foreign currency, settle through any authorized financial intermediary, and repatriate funds according to normal commercial terms without approval from the Bank of Canada or a foreign exchange administration authority.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, with foreign exchange administration coordinated through the Department of Finance Canada under the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, and the Financial Administration Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium - Offshore digital e-money institutions are not directly authorized to provide deposit-taking or payment settlement services to Canadian residents without complying with the Retail Payment Activities Act when capturing and clearing Canadian payment obligations, and with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act as a foreign money service business servicing a Canadian client base. In practice, a BC-limited partnership is best served by a Canadian Tier 1 bank account used for primary liquidity and settlement, while a regulated foreign EMI may operate legally only as a supplementary collection-settlement rail with FINTRAC registration and contractual allocation of liabilities. The onboarding viability of a full offshore EMI model is assessed as medium for a low-risk EdTech subscription business, but the absence of a physical Canadian nexus and a direct registered clearing relationship materially reduces flexibility.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal 38% gross, reduced to 15% net corporate rate (after provincial abatement); British Columbia provincial rate 12% for general corporations, resulting in a combined general corporate income tax rate of 27%. For Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) claiming the small business deduction, the combined federal-provincial rate on active business income up to the business limit is approximately 11% (federal 9% + BC 2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident partners (where the partnership is deemed to have paid a dividend on its own initiative, or in the case of a distribution by a Canadian corporation). For limited partnerships, distributions to partners are generally not dividends; however, for treaty purposes, income from a partnership interest is treated as business income, and Part XIII withholding tax may apply to the non-resident partner's share of partnership income if the partner is not subject to Part I tax. The applicable statutory rate is 25% for dividends and deemed dividends under subsection 212(2).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T5013 partnership information return is due on the later of March 31 following the calendar year of the partnership fiscal period-end, or the date that is five months after the partnership's fiscal period-end for partnerships with a non-calendar year-end. A nil return must be filed if the partnership has no income or activity. The T5013 return must be filed by the designated partner; the filing due date for the partnership itself is generally within five months after the fiscal year-end. However, each corporate partner's T2 corporate income tax return is due six months after the corporation's taxation year-end. Quarterly instalment payments apply to corporate partners and certain individuals.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Box 1 Partnership identification: legal name, business number, fiscal period end, and mailing address. Provide the partnership's Canadian business number and indicate the non-resident partner's non-resident ownership percentage and country of tax residence. For a non-resident partner, include the partner's social insurance number, business number, or account identifier in the T5013 supplementary slip. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No specific statutory monetary threshold in Canada that triggers a mandatory Local File or Master File. Under Section 247 of the Income Tax Act, transfer pricing documentation must be contemporaneous and prepared by the taxpayer's filing due date. The CRA may request that the taxpayer provide complete and accurate documentation within 30 days; failure to produce contemporaneous records may result in penalties equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (reduced to 5% for arm's-length transactions if the taxpayer has made reasonable efforts). However, the CRA publishes Administrative Guidance that documentation is required for all transactions with non-resident related parties, irrespective of amount. For the limited partnership, the documentation must cover the partnership-level cross-border subscriptions and intercompany licensing arrangements. The 'threshold' is effectively the existence of any cross-border non-arm's-length transaction. Small taxpayers (gross revenue less than $5 million) may use the minimal documentation exemption, but the partnership must still maintain sufficient records to establish arm's-length outcomes.

Economic substance scope: The Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) apply to British Columbia entities only insofar as BC has adopted the federal 'foreign affiliate dump' and 'anti-avoidance' rules. Generally, the ESR regime is a Caribbean/OECD initiative, not a Canadian federal law. For Canadian limited partnerships, the CRA applies the 'general anti-avoidance rule' (GAAR) in Section 245 of the Income Tax Act to deny tax benefits from artificial transactions. Additionally, the 'offshore investment fund rules' (FAPI) and the 'foreign investment entity rules' can recharacterize passive income. For a limited partnership with Canadian-source business income, the CRA will scrutinize whether the partnership is a 'conduit' for non-resident owners. The Canada Revenue Agency may apply the 'treaty shopping' doctrine and the 'beneficial ownership' test.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act, Section 238, 239)

Statutory sanction: On indictment, a fine up to 200% of the amount sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years. On summary conviction, a fine up to $10,000 and/or imprisonment up to two years. CRA may also impose a gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners of a limited partnership may be liable if they directed, authorized, assented to, or participated in the evasion. The CRA may assess directors' liability under Section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted withholding taxes, and the court may issue a travel ban under the Criminal Code (Section 462.34) and impose restitution orders. Imprisonment up to five years for conviction on indictment.
Failure to File / Late Filing (Income Tax Act, Section 162)

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalty for T5013: $25 per partner per day, up to $2,500. For a corporation partner, 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per month, up to 12 months. A person who fails to file a return as required and knows of the obligation may be liable for a penalty equal to 10% of the tax that should have been paid.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for the penalty, but if the partnership is a corporate general partner, directors may be liable under Section 227.1 if the corporation fails to withhold and remit Part XIII tax or employee source deductions. A failure to file may also trigger a 'director's liability' for unremitted amounts, and the CRA can obtain a court order for payment and seize assets.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance (Section 247)

Statutory sanction: A penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (if the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length price). The CRA may reassess and impose the penalty for each year, and the taxpayer may face interest on the reassessed amount. No automatic criminal sanction for the penalty itself; however, a deliberate overpricing scheme may be prosecuted as tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly signed or approved false transfer pricing documentation may be personally liable for the corporate penalty under Section 227.1 only if the corporation fails to remit the assessed amount. In criminal proceedings, a director may be convicted as a party to the offence, leading to fines and imprisonment up to five years.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Abuse (GAAR, Section 245; General Anti-Avoidance Rules)

Statutory sanction: GAAR denies the tax benefit, adjusts the taxpayer's taxable income, and may impose interest from the date of the original assessment. No specific 'economic substance' penalty in federal law; however, the CRA may impose a 25% penalty for misrepresentation attributable to negligence or wilful blindness (Section 163.2). In egregious cases, the arrangement may be considered a sham, leading to criminal penalties up to five years' imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners who were knowing participants in the sham arrangement may be jointly and severally liable for the reassessed tax. Directors may be held personally liable for the corporation's unremitted Part XIII withholding tax. Additionally, under the BC Business Corporations Act (which applies to a BC corporate general partner), directors face a statutory duty of care; a breach can lead to personal damages in civil actions and potential prohibition from acting as a director for up to 15 years.
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance (Part XIII, Section 215)

Statutory sanction: The non-resident beneficiary is liable for the withholding tax, but the partnership or the payer is also liable for the unremitted amount. The penalty is 10% of the amount not remitted if the failure was made knowingly or grossly negligent. Interest accrues on the unremitted amount. The CRA can assess a director of the general partner for the full amount under Section 227.1.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are personally liable for the unremitted withholding tax, including interest and penalties, if they failed to exercise due diligence to prevent the failure. The liability is absolute and no proof of intent is required. The CRA may issue a personal assessment and file a lien against the director's personal assets. Travel bans may be imposed under the Collection and Enforcement sections if the director leaves the jurisdiction with unpaid tax.
AML/ATF and Payment Aggregation (Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) up to CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 1,000,000 for corporations. Criminal penalties include imprisonment up to five years for knowingly laundering proceeds of crime. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) may suspend or revoke a license.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to implement a compliance program may be held liable as 'officers' of the reporting entity. They may be subject to fines up to CAD 1,000,000 and imprisonment up to five years. The corporate veil is pierced by the court if the partnership is used as an instrument for money laundering, and directors are jointly and severally liable for restitution.
FATCA / CRS Reporting (Tax Information Exchange)

Statutory sanction: FATCA penalties: up to CAD 2,500 for failure to provide information, and up to CAD 25,000 for deliberate failure. CRS penalties mirror FATCA. The CRA may additionally assess a 5% gross negligence penalty on the tax amount.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner can be personally assessed for these penalties if they knowingly participated in the failure. Under Canada's Income Tax Act, a director is jointly and severally liable for the corporation's failure to comply with international reporting requirements.
