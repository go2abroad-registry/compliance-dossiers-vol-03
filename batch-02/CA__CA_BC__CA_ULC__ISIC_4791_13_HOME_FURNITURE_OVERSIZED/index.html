Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility with no general foreign-exchange control statute. The Currency Act makes the Canadian dollar legal tender, but residents and non-residents may freely hold, use, and transfer foreign currency. Inbound equity injections, shareholder loans, and outbound profit remittances are not subject to Central Bank approval. Profit remittances from a BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax on dividends (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced under the Canada-United States Tax Convention and third-country treaties). The principal compliance hurdles are Canadian banking AML/KYC onboarding, FINTRAC large-transaction reporting (CAD 10,000 threshold), and documentary proof of source of funds and ultimate beneficial ownership. Capital distributions and returns of paid-up capital may be remitted without withholding if properly documented as return of capital rather than dividend; CRA forms NR4, NR301, and NR302 are required as applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation, or forced conversion requirement for export proceeds. There is no mandatory timeline for conversion into Canadian dollars and no prescribed conversion ratio. An exporting ULC may retain 100% of foreign-currency proceeds in domestic or offshore accounts. Canadian banks are, however, required to report any transaction involving CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC, and cross-border electronic funds transfers are subject to reportable-transaction monitoring. No Bank of Canada declaration or export-license is required for furniture e-commerce sales.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) exercises anti-money-laundering oversight; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) prudenitally regulates Canadian banks and clearing operations

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low-to-Medium supplemental viability. Offshore digital EMI products (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer, and similar specialized FX platforms) may be used for low-value disbursements and cross-border vendor payments but are not a substitute for a regulated Canadian bank account. The primary legal constraint is that an offshore EMI is not a member of Payments Canada and therefore cannot directly settle through Lynx or the Canadian Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS). In practice, an EMI must operate through a Canadian sponsor bank, creating settlement risk and an additional intermediary. EMI accounts are not CDIC-insured, and the ULC remains fully liable under its unlimited-liability structure for any outstanding obligations. The feasibility rating is 'Low' for capital escrow and treasury concentration and 'Medium' for small invoice disbursements, supplier trial runs, and sub-CAD 10,000 operational FX payments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity injections from non-resident shareholders must be routed by SWIFT MT103 single-customer credit transfer using the beneficiary bank's SWIFT/BIC, the ULC's Canadian transit number and account number, and a clear Field 70 remittance narrative stating 'Shareholder capital contribution to BC ULC' or 'Subordinated shareholder loan'. No Canadian exchange-control approval is required, but the receiving bank will require a source-of-funds declaration and may perform a FINTRAC large-transaction report if the amount equals or exceeds CAD 10,000. Documentation should include a shareholder resolution authorizing the contribution, a subscription agreement or loan agreement, and CRA-filed or notional paid-up-capital records. Inbound funds should first land in a dedicated Capital Escrow Account or segregated operational clearing account, never commingled with e-commerce payments from Stripe of Affirm, and the bank may require a quarantine period pending KYC verification.Outbound supplier and freight settlements must be executed through cleared corporate banking rails. Direct supplier payments should use SWIFT MT103 with full beneficiary details and HS-code / commercial-invoice references in Field 70. CAD-denominated settlement is cleared through Payments Canada's Lynx real-time gross settlement system in excess of the ACSS threshold, while USD-denominated balances may be cleared via U.S. Fedwire or Clearing House Interbank Payments System (CHIPS) through the Canadian bank's correspondent network. For higher-value or repeat furniture imports, a Commercial Letter of Credit issued by the Canadian bank pursuant to ICC UCP600 provides documentary risk mitigation; for oversized-freight logistics, standby letters of credit or bank-guarantee instruments may secure obligations to freight forwarders. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Full contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File and Master File) is administratively required when the aggregate fair market value of controlled transactions with non-residents exceeds C$1 million in a fiscal year. For multinational groups with consolidated turnover greater than C$10 million, CRA generally expects a Master File. Transactions below C$1 million may rely on simplified documentation (contemporaneous invoices, pricing policy, and a written arm's-length justification).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations; however, CRA invokes the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA) and substance-over-form doctrine to counter shell conduits. A BC ULC with no employees, no physical inventory storage in Canada, and no logistical decision-making is exposed to re-characterization and denial of treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under ITA s.239(1) carries a penalty of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment for up to 5 years. Civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163.2 is 50% of the understated tax (minimum C$100). Interest and CRA enforcement costs apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false T2 returns or cause withholding-tax evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax shortfall and penalties; they may be criminally prosecuted, banned from board service, and in serious cases issued travel injunctions.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR to disregard the ULC; reassess the parent as the true taxpayer; impose interest and gross-negligence penalties; and deny treaty withholding reductions. The BC Registry may strike off a non-active company for failing to file annual returns or provide its corporate dossier.

Director personal liability: Directors involved in orchestrating a sham may be personally assessed under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted Part XIII withholding tax if they controlled the entity and failed to act with reasonable care.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: CRA adjusts the price to an arm's-length amount under s.247(2); the net adjustment is subject to full tax plus interest. A separate s.247(3) penalty of 10% of the net adjustment applies if the adjustment exceeds either C$5 million or 10% of gross revenue and the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for transfer-pricing penalties, but if they approved contrived transactions with the intent to evade, they may be jointly and severally liable under the Tax Evasion rules and subject to oppression or fraud claims.
AML / Beneficial Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: BC Business Corporations Act: late annual-report fees and eventual dissolution after two years. Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties (up to C$1 million) for non-registration or reporting failures; obstruction or false reporting may lead to imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for late annual-report fees and penalty amounts imposed under BC law. For money-laundering activities directed or authorized by directors, personal criminal liability attaches under the PCMLTFA and Criminal Code, which may include imprisonment and order of restitution to the Crown.
