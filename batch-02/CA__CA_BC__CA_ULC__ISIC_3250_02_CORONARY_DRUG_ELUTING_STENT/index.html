E-government filing portal

BC PST registration requires completing BC Financial Institution Visa or the BC taxpayer application, obtaining a separate PST number from the Ministry of Finance. The BN is the statutory TIN for federal tax purposes; the provincial PST account supplements the BN for BC retail sales tax.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide check-the-box elections. A BC ULC is a Canadian resident corporation taxed at entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch rules (sections 18.4 and 18.5 of the Income Tax Act) apply to arrangements involving fiscally transparent ULCs used in cross-border structures; no separate elective classification mechanism is available for Canadian tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST registration threshold for non-resident vendors is CAD 30,000 of taxable worldwide revenue from Canadian supplies in any single calendar quarter or the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; PST registration is mandatory on the first taxable sale in BC with no small-supplier threshold, including remote sales of deemed taxable goods and services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated supplies are allowed for goods physically exported from Canada, typically delivered by the supplier or an exporter to a location outside Canada; qualifying services (including medical device contract manufacturing) may be zero-rated if performed on non-residents or exported and consumed entirely outside Canada. A supply of a commercial export is zero-rated when the exporter establishes that the goods were exported as soon as possible after the sale and satisfies all CRA documentation conditions.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13 or electronic export report)
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List evidencing shipment out of Canada
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill (Original or certified copy)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire confirmation showing payment from a non-resident foreign buyer
  • Proof of the exporter's possession and transfer of physical custody to a carrier for export

No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping is mandated in Canada. Suppliers may elect to issue conforming electronic invoices in standard XML or EDI compatible with CRA's multi-format requirements; CRA encourages electronic data exchange but does not operate a pre-clearance or real-time clearance system for GST/HST. PST invoices must separately show the PST and the seller's PST registration number.

CRA and BC Ministry of Finance audit zero-rated export claims with heightened review of medical-device exports. Failure to substantiate within the prescribed period results in reassessment of GST/PST, denial of refunds, and reversal to taxable supplies from the date of supply. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include 20% to 25% of net tax deficiencies for knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence, fines and imprisonment for wilful evasion (up to CAD 10,000 and/or two years imprisonment), and analogous penalties under BC's Provincial Sales Tax Act, including 100% refund clawbacks and director liability for unpaid PST.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada achieves full Article VIII IMF convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no exchange-control restrictions on conversion of CAD into foreign currency, on non-resident equity ownership, or on repatriation of capital, dividends, interest, royalties, or trade proceeds. Profit-remittance hurdles are tax-driven rather than FX-driven: dividends paid by the BC ULC to a non-resident shareholder attract Canadian Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (generally 25%, potentially reduced by treaty), and interest/royalty remittances may also face withholding unless treaty-relieved. All outbound remittances require no central-bank approval but must satisfy the bank's source-of-funds and OFAC/FINTRAC screening procedures.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender, no forced conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline on export proceeds. Exporters may freely hold USD, EUR, CNY or other foreign currencies in Canadian-domiciled foreign-currency accounts and convert at commercial rates. Documentary duties are limited to Canada Border Services Agency customs reporting and, for Class III medical devices, compliance with Health Canada establishment-licence requirements and export-permit screening by Global Affairs Canada. Banks nonetheless expect the ULC to provide underlying export documentation (commercial invoice, packing list, airway bill, certificate of origin) to support clearance of high-risk MedTech trade receipts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); banking supervision is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and AML/ATF enforcement by FINTRAC. Canada maintains a free-floating exchange-rate regime and does not operate a dedicated foreign-exchange control authority; the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium with material legal constraints. Offshore digital EMIs and payment-platform providers (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Currenxie) are not licensed as deposit-taking banks in Canada and cannot lawfully accept Canadian deposits or open a true CAD settlement account in the ULC's name. Such providers must either register as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC or operate through a Canadian Schedule I bank agency arrangement. Therefore, offshore EMIs are viable only as supplementary collection or small-ticket disbursement gateways, not as the primary treasury or liquidity rail. Core cross-border liquidity must remain with a Tier-1 Canadian bank to support documentary credits, correspondent clearing, and regulatory defence evidence.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

The statutory federal general corporate income tax rate is 15%. The statutory British Columbia general corporate income tax rate is 12%. The combined headline federal-plus-BC rate for a BC-resident active manufacturing corporation is therefore 27%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% per Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident recipient. Under the Canada-US tax treaty, the rate is reduced to 15% for general non-resident shareholders and to 5% where the beneficial owner is a US resident corporation that owns at least 10% of the voting shares of the BC ULC and satisfies the applicable limitation-on-benefits provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within six calendar months after the end of the taxation year. For a December 31 fiscal year-end, the deadline is June 30 of the next calendar year. The balance of tax due must be paid within two months after the fiscal year-end for the general corporate class; eligible CCPCs may enjoy a three-month balance-due deadline. Filing by an agent under a valid electronic authorization is required for any non-resident-representative return submission.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000. A BC-resident ULC engaged in reportable non-arm's-length transactions or a cross-border series of transactions with a total annual value above CAD 1,000,000 must prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation, including a Local File and, where applicable, a Master File, and file Form T106 Information Return. Country-by-Country reporting obligations apply at the consolidated group level if the group reports annual consolidated revenue equal to or greater than EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations filing, but the CRA and Canadian courts apply a robust economic-substance-inspired doctrine through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245): a transaction entered into primarily to obtain a tax benefit without substantial business purpose, economic reality, or changed legal involvement is subject to recharacterization. For a BC ULC, the unlimited-liability corporate form used as a pass-through vehicle must maintain real operational activity in British Columbia: staff, technical manufacturing assets, risk-bearing revenue, a registered office, board minutes, and substantive control over the stent-manufacturing process. OECD and MLI limitation-on-benefits rules further require a direct business connection and real economic activity before treaty withholding reductions are available to the ultimate non-resident owners.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion by false deductions and fabricated R&D claims

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion under ITA s. 239(1): a fine equal to 50% to 200% of the evaded federal tax plus BC provincial tax, full restitution with interest, seizure and forfeiture of the stent-manufacturing assets or bank proceeds, and imprisonment for up to five years. Civilly, CRA may re-assess at any time within four years, and indefinitely where misrepresentation is established.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, authorize, accept, or acquiesce in the filing of a false T2 return are jointly and severally liable for the unpaid tax, penalties, and interest under ITA s. 227.1 and comparable BC provincial legislation. Criminal conviction triggers travel bans, passport surrender, potential extradition under mutual legal assistance treaties, and imposed restitution. For a BC ULC, because shareholder liability is statutorily unlimited, shareholders also risk personal liability for debts arising from wrongful tax conduct when the corporate veil is pierced or upon winding up.
Failure to withhold Part XIII tax on cross-border profit repatriation

Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the full amount of unreported Part XIII withholding tax plus interest. ITA s. 227(8) imposes a 10% penalty on the unremitted amount; deliberate failure can lead to a penalty equal to 25% of the unpaid amount and criminal sanctions under s. 239. Further, an R&D tax credit or deduction previously granted may be forfeited where the transaction is belatedly recharacterized.

Director personal liability: Every director who was a director at the time the dividends, royalties, or interest became payable is personally liable for the unremitted Part XIII amounts, penalties, and interest unless the corporation can prove a transfer of funds before the payment. CRA routinely places freeze registrations on British Columbia real property, bank accounts, and export receivables of the ULC. Travel bans may be imposed as a condition of criminal bail, and a director may be subject to a departure prohibition order under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act if a tax liability exceeds the prescribed threshold.
Shell ULC intermediary / sham economic substance arrangement

Statutory sanction: Under the GAAR, CRA may disregard the ULC for federal tax purposes and reattribute the gross manufacturing profit to the true economic owner; the resulting adjustment may carry a 25% gross-negligence penalty under ITA s. 163(2) plus interest. Denial of treaty relief and recomputation of Part XIII withholding apply retroactively. The BC registrar may also enforce the unlimited liability obligation and order dissolution if the business is found to be a facade.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders are exposed to 'piercing the unlimited liability veil': every shareholder remains directly liable for the ULC's debts and unpaid taxes in proportion to the number of shares held. Directors who fail to maintain substantive activity or board oversight may face an offence of knowingly participating in the tax deception; the associated liability includes civil restitution, prohibition from serving as a director in British Columbia, travel restrictions during criminal investigation, and imprisonment for willful participation under the Income Tax Act.
AML non-compliance and sanctions evasion in export financing

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny all deductions attributable to the proceeds under the illegal-benefit doctrine; assets may be seized and forfeited under the Criminal Code and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. The BC ULC may be fined up to CAD 2,000,000 per occurrence for repeated prohibited activity, and responsible officers may be charged with money-laundering offences carrying imprisonment for up to ten years.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly receive, transfer, or launder the export funds are personally subject to criminal indictment, property forfeiture, international travel bans, and civil liability to the CRA for tax arrears. If the ULC is disqualified or dissolved, the unlimited liability of shareholders converts residual criminal fines into personal debt, and directors may be personally barred by the British Columbia Securities Commission or the Director under the Business Corporations Act from operating any corporation in the province.
