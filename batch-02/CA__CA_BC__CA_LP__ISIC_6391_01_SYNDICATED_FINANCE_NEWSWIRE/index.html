Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is required for a British Columbia limited partnership. Partner contributions are established in the partnership agreement and must be stated in the Statement of Registration. No statutory escrow account is required; however, subscription proceeds may be held in a lawyer's general trust account or a segregated bank account until deployed under the partnership agreement.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST and Income Tax Administration; British Columbia Ministry of Finance — Provincial Sales Tax (PST) Administration. Registration portals: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html) and BC eTax/Provincial Sales Tax Registration (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes/sales-taxes/pst).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a British Columbia limited partnership must first obtain a Canadian Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The BC limited partnership registers for the BN as a partnership and obtains its own GST/HST Program Account (RT) if taxable supplies in Canada exceed the CAD 30,000 registration threshold; otherwise registration is optional for recouping input tax credits. The partnership may also require a BC PST Registration for making taxable sales of goods or taxable services delivered into British Columbia. The BN registration can be completed online through CRA's Business Registration Online service, or by paper Form RC1. Foreign partners must provide their own non-resident tax identification numbers where applicable; the partnership itself is the registrant for GST/HST and PST, while income tax identification remains the responsibility of each partner.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box election. A British Columbia limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes unless it meets the definition of a 'Canadian partnership' or is treated differently under an applicable tax treaty. Certain elective mechanisms under the Income Tax Act are available for partnership distributions and retirements (e.g., ITA s. 97(2) and s. 98(2)), and non-resident partners can use the s. 216 election for passive or net rental income reporting. Hybrid mismatch rules contained in ITA ss. 18.2 to 18.6 may recharacterize payments made by or to the partnership where legally required; no autonomous domestic election can override these rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or accumulated over four consecutive calendar quarters from taxable supplies made in Canada. BC PST: no de minimis threshold; any vendor making taxable sales, leases, or taxable services into British Columbia is required to register, including non-resident vendors, unless an express exemption applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available for goods that are exported directly by the supplier or deemed exported in accordance with the Excise Tax Act (Schedule VI, Part V). For services, supplies made to a non-resident recipient are zero-rated where the service is not for consumption, use, or supply in Canada by a GST/HST registrant, and where the service is not connected with real estate or tangible personal property located in Canada. A financial news and syndication feed delivered via API to non-resident subscribers can qualify as a zero-rated intangible personal property or service if the commercial substance is the transmission of information to the non-resident and documentary evidence is maintained. The supplier must obtain and preserve proof of export or proof of non-resident status, ordinarily within the two-year time limit imposed by the CRA.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency B13A Export Declaration or authorized electronic equivalent)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank-issued proof of receipt
  • Commercial Invoice with GST/HST zero-rated indicator
  • Cargo Control Number or other carrier-issued export documentation
  • Non-resident status evidence (e.g., W8-style certification or equivalent)

Canada has no mandatory national real-time e-invoicing or government fiscal clearance regime. The CRA does not require digital stamping or API pre-clearance for invoices. Federal e-invoicing standards (PEPPOL BIS 3.0 and North American PEPPOL specification) are voluntary and used for B2B and business-to-government transactions through CRA-approved service providers. For GST/HST purposes, an invoice must contain prescribed information, including the vendor's BN, date of supply, total amount paid/payable, and applicable GST rate. British Columbia permits electronic invoicing; all records must be retained, and CRA/BC may require access to source data upon demand.

The CRA may disallow zero-rating claims upon audit if documentary requirements are not met, reassessing GST/HST at the full 5% rate plus applicable PST and interest. Failure to substantiate export eligibility permits the CRA to apply a four-year statutory reassessment period for ordinarily non-negligent claims and an unlimited period for misrepresentation, plus gross negligence penalties under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act equal to 25% of the tax understated. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims can trigger criminal prosecution under section 327.1, penalty assessments up to 200% of the tax involved, and director's liability under subsection 323(1). All supporting documentation must be retained for six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada applies full convertibility on both the current account and the capital account. There are no exchange-control permits, no prior approval for inbound or outbound remittances, and no surrender or repatriation nucleus in the Income Tax Act. For a BC Limited Partnership, the principal remittance hurdle is not FX convertibility but Canadian withholding and partnership attribution: distributions of profit to a non-resident limited partner are generally not subject to Part XIII withholding when the LP carries on a Canadian business and the partner reports a business income share; however, interest, royalties, or management fees paid by the LP to non-residents are statutorily subject to 25% Part XIII withholding, reduced under the applicable income tax treaty (e.g., Canada-US Art. XVIII). Additional hurdles: (i) payments for 'syndicated finance newswire' service subscriptions generally are current-account service royalties; if the payee is outside Canada and the payment is for the supply of intangibles (data licensing), the BC LP must apply GST/HST zero-rating for exported financial data, while cross-border intercompany license fees face transfer-pricing documentation under s.247. In practice, Canadian banks do not impose FX controls; the OSFI capital and liquidity rules govern the bank counterparty, not the LP.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no filing timeline for converting export receipts into CAD. A Canadian-domiciled LP may lawfully keep export revenue in foreign-currency accounts held through a Schedule I bank. The only binding administrative parameters are: (i) transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 (single or consecutive, within 24 hours) must be reported by the bank to FINTRAC as Large Cash Transaction Reports / Electronic Funds Transfer Reports; (ii) physical import/export of currency or bearer instruments exceeding CAD 10,000 must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) on Form BSF 149; (iii) all export/business records, including invoices and wire remittance narratives, must be retained for at least six years under the Income Tax Act and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration; exchange-rate policy is a free float governed by the Currency Act, and banking/AML conduct is supervised jointly by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM (ancillary) / LOW (primary). Offshore digital EMIs (Wise Platform, Payoneer, Mercury, banking-as-a-service issuers) are legally usable by a BC LP for outbound disbursement to non-resident data contributors, but they are not 'banks' under the Bank Act, hold no CDIC deposit insurance, and cannot receive 'qualifying' CAD settlement that preserves GST/HST input credits. Legal constraints: (i) if the EMI's settlement activity creates a Canadian 'money services business' nexus, it must register with FINTRAC; the LP must confirm the EMI is FINTRAC-registered or exempt; (ii) a Canadian LP holding substantial value on an EMI's pooled ledger creates a credit-risk exposure that must be disclosed to the LP's limited partners; (iii) Stripe, the specified payment rail, is permissible only for low-value API micro-payouts, not for capital injections or trade settlements above CAD 50,000.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate (15% federal + 12% BC provincial)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on distributions from a Canadian-resident partnership to a non-resident partner unless treaty-reduced; distributions in respect of a partnership interest are generally not dividends for Canadian tax purposes, but may be characterized as business income subject to Part I tax if the non-resident carries on business in Canada through the partnership; where the distribution is a deemed dividend from a corporate partner or upon redemption of a partnership interest, the statutory rate is 25% for non-treaty jurisdictions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

A Canadian-resident limited partnership must file the T5013 return within five months after the partnership's fiscal year-end; corporate partners must file T2 within six months after their taxation year-end, and the partnership must allocate income to partners by the filing deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under Canada's transfer pricing rules (Income Tax Act s. 247), a Canadian partnership or its corporate partners must prepare and keep contemporaneous documentation (Local File/Master File equivalent under the OECD aligned CBCR framework) if the aggregate value of cross-border transactions with non-resident non-arm's-length persons exceeds C$1,000,000 for financial or service transactions; CBCR notification and reporting thresholds apply to multinational groups with consolidated revenue equal to or greater than CAD 850 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: British Columbia does not impose a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the UAE, but the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s. 245 of the Income Tax Act can recharacterize an avoidance transaction; the partnership must maintain real economic substance, including substantive management, actual contracts, personnel, and business premises in Canada, particularly if treaty benefits are claimed. The CRA may challenge conduit arrangements or back-to-back licensing structures that lack commercial substance and apply GAAR penalties plus gross negligence penalties. For purposes of the OECD BEPS Action 5, the financial newswire business may be a mobile business activity; despite the absence of a statutory ESR regime, the Canada Revenue Agency can deny treaty benefits under the general anti-avoidance rule and the principal-purpose test if the partnership or its partners were established mainly to obtain Canadian treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under s. 239 of the Income Tax Act: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus up to five years' imprisonment; civil gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax; interest compounded daily; CRA may also levy a penalty of C$100 per T5013 slip up to C$2,500 for omissions.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted Part XIII withholding tax and GST/HST only if they knowingly participated in the failure; criminal tax evasion can lead to personal fines, seizure of personal assets, and imprisonment. A director who signed a false return may be personally liable for the penalty if they had knowledge or were willfully blind; civil restitution is available to the CRA via a director liability assessment.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization denies the tax benefit and imposes a 25% penalty on the amount of the benefit under s. 245(3); CRA may reassess up to 10 years for misrepresentation attributable to neglect or fraud; penalty 50% of tax avoided for gross negligence if the factual matrix is false; the partnership may lose its limited liability status if the limited partners participate in control of the business contrary to the BC Partnership Act.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable in tort for fraudulent misrepresentation or breach of fiduciary duty; a court may pierce the corporate veil of the general partner where the partnership is used as a façade to perpetrate a fraud; personal bank accounts are subject to garnishment or freezing orders; criminal charges of fraud under s. 380 of the Criminal Code apply, with maximum imprisonment of fourteen years, if fictitious transactions are used to deceive tax authorities.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 for non-compliance; failure to report a suspicious transaction can result in penalties of C$1,000–C$100,000 for an entity; knowing contravention or directing the commission of an AML offence carries criminal liability with imprisonment up to ten years and fines at the court's discretion.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable for corporate penalties if they consented to or acquiesced in the commission of an offence under the PCMLTFA; the Criminal Code (s. 462.31) allows for freezing of property, restraint orders, and personal fine or imprisonment; the BC Civil Forfeiture Office may commence civil proceedings to forfeit assets connected to unlawful activity, including personal assets of directors used to further the offence.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the transfer price to an arm's-length amount and impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty on the net adjustment (s. 247(3)(b)) if the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices; interest applies and the reassessment extends the normal reassessment period to seven years; the penalty is in addition to any Part I tax and Part XIII withholding tax on deemed appropriations.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner who knowingly participated in the transfer pricing adjustment are not automatically liable under s. 247, but they may be liable as secondary parties for breach of fiduciary duty or as promoters of a sham; if entries in the T5013/T2 are false, directors may face wilful evasion penalties and personal criminal liability under s. 239; the corporate veil may be pierced if the impugned transfer pricing structure has no economic substance and is a sham used to conceal actual profit attribution.
