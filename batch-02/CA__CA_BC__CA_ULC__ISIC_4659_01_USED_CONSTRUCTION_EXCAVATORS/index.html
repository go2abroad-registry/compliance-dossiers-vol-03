Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (domestic Canada): the BC ULC is a resident corporation (ITA s. 250(4) deeming rule) taxable under ITA Part I on worldwide income plus BC provincial corporate income tax, notwithstanding the metadata's Pass_Through_Eligible flag; that flag reflects only US federal check-the-box eligibility (a hybrid entity for US tax), not Canadian status. Territorial_Scope: the ULC's foreign-owner dividends attract Part XIII withholding (treaty-reduced to 5% or 15% under the Canada-US Convention); no Tax_Exempt_Zone applies.

Available election forms

(i) US IRS Form 8832 entity classification election — the BC ULC, being an eligible entity under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3 (not a per se corporation), may be classified as a partnership or disregarded entity for US federal income tax; (ii) ETA s. 273 election — closely related registrants may elect to treat specified supplies between them as made for nil consideration for GST/HST purposes; (iii) Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.4–18.7) apply to restrict deduction/income mismatches created by check-the-box hybrid structures; (iv) no Canadian election can convert the ULC from corporate to flow-through status for Canadian tax.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD $30,000 — the small-supplier ceiling measured on total worldwide taxable supplies of the person and associates in the four preceding calendar quarters (ETA s. 148(1)); once exceeded, the ULC must register for GST/HST. For non-resident vendors of goods situated in Canada, the same $30,000 ceiling applies, but goods physically in Canada at the time of sale are supplies made in Canada (ETA s. 142(1)) and are taxable unless zero-rated as exports. BC PST: no de minimis threshold — any vendor making taxable sales delivered in BC must hold a PST number; sales delivered outside BC are outside PST scope.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: used hydraulic excavators and rollers (ISIC 4659 / NAICS 423810 second-hand heavy machinery) are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when (a) the ULC is registered for GST/HST, (b) the supply is made in Canada, and (c) the goods are exported as part of the supply — sold to a non-resident buyer, delivered from a BC location to a foreign destination, and actually exported from Canada by the vendor, the buyer, or an agent. Substantiation requires contemporaneous proof of export (purchase and shipping documents, CERS export declaration, bill of lading, foreign customs entry, and payment evidence). Offshore services: services supplied to a non-resident who has no GST/HST registration and no Canadian establishment may be zero-rated under Parts IV/V of Schedule VI (e.g., machinery brokerage, inspection, or load-out services). Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible and is classified as floating. Canada adheres to the IMF Articles of Agreement (Article VIII) and the OECD Codes of Liberalisation, therefore current-account and capital-account transactions do not require central-bank licensing or prior exchange-control approval. Non-resident investors may inject equity, pay trade creditors, and repatriate profits (dividends, redemption proceeds, and loan interest) without currency-conversion restrictions, provided that the underlying transaction is commercially genuine, fully documented, and compliant with Part XIII of the Income Tax Act for certain cross-border payments. The Bank of Canada does not fix the CAD exchange rate, and banks are free to execute client FX transactions at commercially negotiated spot rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no surrender requirements or mandatory conversion ratios in Canada. A British Columbia unlimited liability corporation (BC ULC) is not required to convert foreign-currency export proceeds to CAD, is not required to repatriate export funds within a statutory time window, and may maintain foreign-currency accounts with Canadian deposit institutions or offshore banking affiliates. Export transactions must be correctly documented for Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) reporting through the CARM system (including an export declaration when above monetary thresholds), but such filings do not impose any retain-convert obligations on the exporter.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange policy is administered by the Department of Finance Canada; banking prudential oversight is held by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering and terrorist financing monitoring by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for routine cross-border payment flows and for provider accounts opened in Canada by a Canadian entity; legally, fintech EMIs operating in Canada must register as money service businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC unless the entity holds a federal bank license or is otherwise exempt as foreign-deposit-taking institutions. They cannot serve as a full substitute for a Canadian Schedule I bank because they are unable to issue conventional documentary letters of credit or provide deposit-taking under the Bank Act with Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) coverage. For used heavy-equipment trades with medium-risk/AML-enhancement classification, an MSB or offshore EMI may be used strictly as a pre-funded payment rail for invoices below the bank-appetite threshold, but the primary commercial clearing and trade-finance lane must remain a licensed Canadian bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For a BC ULC, share capital subscription or shareholder loans may be injected via SWIFT MT103; the bank instruction must include a remittance description clearly identifying 'share capital contribution', 'ultra vires funding' in the event of loan-level repayments, and reference the specific board resolution. The Canadian bank receiving the wire will process the incoming CAD or USD using the standard high-value clearing system (Lynx for CAD; CHIPS/Fedwire for USD via correspondent bank) and will verify the inflow against the corporate KYC. No exchange-control approval is required, but the remittance must be recorded in the ULC’s financial books and, for tax compliance, the inputs should be coded against CRA’s T2 Schedule 8 (Investment Tax Credit not relevant) and Part XIII withholding records for interest/dividends. For compliance with Canada’s Beneficial Ownership registry, the ultimate source-of-funds statement must be available on request. Bank purpose codes may be used (e.g., 'G' for general settlement) but a free-text competent remittance instruction should also be provided, discussing the underlying equity or debt instrument and clarifying that no goods/services are involved in the equity injection.Primary rails for outbound trade settlement are: (1) documentary letters of credit (MT700 and subsequent amendments), typically issued by a Canadian bank for the benefit of overseas equipment suppliers or for purchase from non-resident distributors; (2) SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfers for clean payments after receipt of shipping documents and satisfactory inspection; and (3), for very high-value single equipment purchases, participating bank syndication of a repayment facility can be used but is not a bank clearing rail. Due to the trade in used excavators, the following controls are assigned: a recipient bank must be an approved counterparty, the invoice must reference the machine’s unique serial/home identification number, and the SWIFT instruction must include the purpose code '923' (Goods – Commercial Transactions) or any other internal bank code chosen. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate: federal 15% + British Columbia provincial 12%. The BC ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and must not apply the Canadian-controlled private corporation small business rate if controlled by non-residents. For U.S. tax check-the-box purposes the ULC may be disregarded, but Canadian tax classification remains a corporation and full T2 taxation applies.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid or credited to non-residents, including deemed dividends arising on redemption, cancellation, or wind-up where the amount paid exceeds paid-up capital. The rate may be reduced by a tax treaty; the Canada-U.S. DTA generally provides a 15% rate and a 5% rate for a U.S. corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the voting shares.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000: mandatory contemporaneous Form T106 and Local File documentation are triggered when the aggregate annual value of reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceeds C$1,000,000. A Master File is expected in practice for multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of C$750,000,000 or more. Country-by-Country reporting applies at the C$750,000,000 group revenue threshold; failure to maintain contemporaneous records exposed to the full 10% s.247 transfer-pricing penalty.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a zero-tax Economic Substance Regulations regime in the Caribbean sense. However, the CRA applies the common-law substance-over-form doctrine, income tax sham doctrine, Part XVI anti-avoidance rules, and the general anti-avoidance rule under s.245 of the Income Tax Act. A BC ULC carrying on used construction machinery trading must have a substantive local presence: a functioning BC office, local bank accounts, Canadian employees or responsible directors, physical control of inventory, invoicing and contract execution, local risk management, and board decisions documented in Canada. Without those elements, CRA can attribute all purchasing and resale activity to the foreign parent's Canadian permanent establishment, deny treaty benefits, and reassess on a full arm's-length basis.
