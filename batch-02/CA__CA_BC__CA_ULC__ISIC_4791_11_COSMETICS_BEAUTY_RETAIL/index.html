Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal tax administration for GST/HST, income tax, and payroll; portal: www.canada.ca (My Business Account / Business Registration Online). British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST) is administered separately by the BC Ministry of Finance (Consumer Taxation Branch) via the eTaxBC portal.

E-government filing portal

Foreign-owned BC ULC procedure: (1) incorporate or continue the Unlimited Liability Company under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act via BC Corporate Registry (OneStop); (2) obtain the BC incorporation number; (3) register with the CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1 to obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN), activating GST/HST, payroll (RP), and customs import/export program accounts as needed; (4) a non-resident corporation without a Canadian presence may use its BC registered office address and must file schedule RC459 with director/officer particulars; (5) register for BC PST through eTaxBC before making taxable retail sales in British Columbia; (6) retain CRA-compliant books and records for six years. No minimum capital requirement applies to the ULC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — for Canadian Income Tax Act purposes, a BC ULC is a corporation resident in Canada, separately taxable on worldwide income, with no territorial scope election; Pass_Through_Eligible — under ITA subsection 216.1(1), a Canadian-resident ULC owned by qualifying non-resident (typically US) shareholders may elect partnership treatment for Canadian tax purposes, shifting tax liability to shareholders.

Available election forms

ITA s. 216.1(1) ULC-to-partnership election filed with the CRA by a Canadian-resident ULC whose shares are held by a non-resident corporation resident in a treaty country; US federal check-the-box election (Form 8832) may treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for US federal tax; Excise Tax Act s. 156 election for closely related corporations to zero-rate intercompany supplies; annual GST/HST reporting election for non-resident registrants.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide gross revenues from taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, triggering mandatory GST/HST registration; voluntary registration is available below this threshold. BC PST registration is required for vendors, including non-resident online sellers, making taxable retail sales in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V, zero-rated supplies include goods exported by the supplier and services supplied to a non-resident (other than services related to Canadian real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada) where the non-resident is not registered for GST/HST. An online cosmetics retailer may claim 0% GST on export sales when goods are shipped to an address outside Canada and documentary evidence proves export within a reasonable time.

  • Commercial invoice stating the goods were sold to and shipped to a foreign buyer
  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) or Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) declaration for goods valued over CAD 2,000 or for controlled/prohibited goods of any value
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill issued by the carrier evidencing movement of goods out of Canada
  • Postal or courier customs declaration (CN22/CP72) with tracking proof for low-value e-commerce shipments
  • Bank or payment service provider settlement records demonstrating receipt of foreign proceeds

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, government e-invoicing clearance, or digital stamping. The CRA accepts electronic invoices and records if legible and complete (CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17-2). Peppol-based Pan-Canadian e-invoicing (Bis 3.0) is voluntary; no API clearance with the tax authority or digital signature stamping is required. Shopify Payments/PayPal transaction records and CERS/EDIS export data must be retained for audit purposes.

The CRA may reassess GST/HST within four years, extended to seven years in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud (Excise Tax Act s. 298). False export zero-rating claims attract penalties under ETA s. 280 (false statements) and s. 285 (tax evasion: penalty of 25% of the tax sought to be evaded plus fines up to CAD 25,000 on summary conviction); income tax gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated tax apply under ITA s. 163.2. Retrospective clawback of zero-rating and input tax credits may result where export documentation is insufficient, and ULC directors may be personally liable for unremitted amounts under ETA s. 323.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no statutory exchange controls, no central bank approval for cross-border transfers, and no requirement to convert export receipts into CAD. Profit remittances from a British Columbia unlimited liability corporation to a foreign parent are lawful, but the practical compliance burden is tax-based: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (25% statutory rate on dividends, interest, royalties and management fees, subject to treaty reduction under the Canada-US treaty), transfer-pricing documentation, thin-capitalization rules, and CRS/FATCA reporting. Because a ULC has unlimited liability, any dissolution or capital reduction remittance must first satisfy potential creditor claims and follow the creditor-priority provisions of the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia).

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose statutory export-proceeds surrender timelines, mandatory conversion ratios, or designated-bank deposit requirements. The BC ULC may lawfully retain USD or other foreign-currency proceeds in onshore or offshore accounts; realized foreign exchange gains are taxable in Canada, and intercompany/operating documentation should distinguish retained earnings, equity capital, and trade payables.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Canada is the central monetary authority but not a foreign-exchange control regulator. Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange administration or exchange-control licensing authority; financial movement surveillance is carried out by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for ancillary collection and payout services; Low for primary treasury banking. Offshore digital EMI and PSP providers may offer multi-currency accounts, payment cards, and cross-border settlements, but they are not Canadian chartered banks and do not have principal access to Payments Canada clearing rails (Lynx, ACSS, and cheque imaging). Their accounts are not CDIC-insured, and trade finance instruments such as documentary letters of credit are generally not available from EMIs. Legally, an offshore EMI can act as an agent of the BC ULC, but all Canadian tax payments, payroll, GST/PST remittances, and high-value settlement must route through a Canadian financial institution that is a member of Payments Canada. Under the Retail Payment Activities Act framework, payment service providers operating in Canada may need to register with the Bank of Canada, so the ULC must verify the EMI's registration or reliance status.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity capital should be routed by SWIFT MT103 or ISO 20022 remittance from the non-resident shareholder's bank directly to the BC ULC's account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. Canada does not use IBAN; the beneficiary must be identified by institution number, transit/branch number, account number, and bank SWIFT/BIC code. Remittance advice should state 'Shareholder capital contribution' or 'Equity injection', reference the ULC incorporation number and authorizing board resolution, and never use ambiguous terms such as 'loan' or 'advance' unless a documented intercompany debt agreement exists. There is no official Canadian FX purpose code regime, but the bank's FINTRAC reporting obligation for international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more will be triggered. For large-value CAD clearing, use the bank's Lynx high-value wire capability for same-day final settlement; for standard cross-border wires, SWIFT GPI tracking is recommended. Verification materials include the bank's regulatory disclosure, the matching of remitter name to the known UBO file, and the ULC's updated KYC profile.Outbound trade settlements should use SWIFT MT103 for supplier invoices for cosmetics inventory and shipping costs, with CAD settled via Lynx and cross-border leg executed through the Canadian bank's correspondent network. For high-value stock purchases, irrevocable documentary letters of credit under UCP600 are appropriate, issued by the Canadian bank via SWIFT MT700/MT720, with document examination under eUCP or standard presentation and courier delivery. For small-ticket payments, CAD disbursements can use ACSS rails including EFT, wire, or payment APIs; card-not-present payments should be routed through the designated payment processor. USD supplier payments can be made either from a USD clearing account at a Canadian bank or through the bank's US correspondent using ABA routing for Fedwire or ACH; this is efficient for US-based beauty brand inventory and logistics vendors. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia provincial rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may obtain a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income up to the applicable provincial and federal business limits; a non-resident-controlled ULC generally does not qualify as a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident ULC to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty or other statutory relief. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for a qualifying corporate parent meeting ownership and treaty requirements or 15% for other qualifying corporate recipients, but the exact rate depends on the recipient's residence, ownership, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits rules, principal-purpose tests, and treaty provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying CCPC may receive a three-month balance-payment period if the applicable statutory conditions are satisfied. Monthly instalments are generally due by the last day of each month.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires reasonable contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory conditions. A country-by-country report and master-file requirements generally apply to a Canadian constituent entity of an MNE group meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold, with local-file obligations assessed under Canadian transfer-pricing rules and CRA documentation requirements.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone United Kingdom-style or United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance remains relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, the sham and series-of-transactions doctrines, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and judicial business-purpose analysis. A ULC should maintain Canadian commercial substance proportionate to its activities, including real decision-making, books and records, banking controls, inventory and fulfillment arrangements, employees or contracted operational capacity, customer and supplier contracts, and documented board oversight.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false corporate tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit for gross negligence, reassessment of tax, arrears interest, and denial of deductions. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years; fraud-related offences may carry more severe consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for statutory source deductions, GST/HST, and certain trust amounts where they failed to exercise due diligence. Individuals who participated in evasion may be prosecuted personally. Civil restitution, asset seizure, director-liability assessments, and travel restrictions may arise in enforcement proceedings; imprisonment is possible for criminal convictions.
Failure to remit payroll deductions and trust amounts

Statutory sanction: Remittance penalties, arrears interest, director-liability assessments, garnishment, liens, and prosecution may apply. Repeated or deliberate failures materially increase penalty exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses. Personal assets may be pursued after corporate assets are insufficient.
Improper treaty withholding and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the withholding shortfall, interest, failure-to-deduct penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and information-return penalties. Treaty benefits may be denied under anti-abuse provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure where they knowingly authorized improper withholding or failed to implement reasonable controls. Criminal liability may arise for deliberate tax fraud or falsification.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, impose a transfer-pricing adjustment penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, deny deductions, and assess interest. Corresponding adjustments are not automatic.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed for deliberate false reporting, fraud, or tax debts covered by statutory director-liability provisions. Routine transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Thin capitalization and excessive related-party financing

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions may be denied or recharacterized, withholding tax may apply, and transfer-pricing or anti-avoidance adjustments may be imposed with interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Personal liability generally requires an independent statutory basis or participation in wrongdoing. Directors remain exposed for unpaid source amounts and criminal conduct.
Shell company, sham management, and lack of economic substance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, recharacterize transactions, deny deductions, reassess tax, and impose interest and penalties. Corporate-law remedies may include oppression, fraudulent-conveyance, or breach-of-duty claims.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly implement sham arrangements may face civil damages, restitution, asset recovery, disqualification, and criminal prosecution. Imprisonment is possible where conduct constitutes fraud or tax evasion.
Unreported foreign property, affiliates, or cross-border accounts

Statutory sanction: Fixed and daily information-return penalties may apply, together with reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, interest, and potential prosecution for deliberate non-disclosure.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be personally liable for their own reporting failures. Directors may be liable for corporate failures involving false certifications, fraud, or statutory trust amounts.
GST/HST and British Columbia PST non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Tax assessments, late-filing and late-remittance penalties, arrears interest, audit adjustments, denial of input tax credits, collection enforcement, and possible prosecution may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for GST/HST trust amounts and other statutory remittances. Intentional collection and diversion of taxes can support criminal or restitution proceedings.
Customs, import valuation, and product-compliance misstatement

Statutory sanction: CBSA reassessments, duties, taxes, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, import restrictions, and prosecution may result. Health Canada product-compliance enforcement may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal prosecution where they authorized fraudulent customs declarations or unsafe-product conduct. Civil recovery and asset seizure may accompany criminal proceedings.
Anti-money-laundering and beneficial-ownership non-compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC compliance measures, administrative monetary penalties, transaction restrictions, seizure, and criminal money-laundering or proceeds-of-crime charges may apply. Criminal offences can carry substantial fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners may face personal prosecution, forfeiture, restitution, prohibition orders, travel restrictions, and imprisonment. Corporate limited liability does not protect participants in laundering or fraud.
Corporate-law misuse of an unlimited liability corporation

Statutory sanction: The ULC's shareholders may be directly liable for corporate obligations because shareholder liability is unlimited under the governing provincial corporate statute. Insolvency, fraudulent-preference, oppression, and breach-of-duty remedies may also apply.

Director personal liability: Unlike a standard limited-liability corporation, the ULC structure creates materially elevated shareholder exposure. Directors may face civil restitution, contribution claims, disqualification, asset recovery, and criminal sanctions for fraud or intentional misconduct.
Record-keeping and audit obstruction

Statutory sanction: Record-keeping penalties, adverse reassessments, denial of deductions or credits, contempt or obstruction proceedings, and criminal charges may apply.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers and directors may be personally prosecuted for obstruction, false statements, or destruction of evidence. Civil and criminal sanctions may include fines, restitution, asset seizure, and imprisonment.
