Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal GST/HST and income tax administration; portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO). British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST) is administered separately by the BC Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC / BC Online portal). Corporate and partnership registration: BC Corporate Online (OneStop BC) under the Partnership Act, RSBC 1996, c. 348.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the Limited Partnership under the BC Partnership Act via BC Corporate Online; obtain the BC LP registration number and general-partner particulars. Step 2: Apply to CRA for the 9-digit Business Number (BN) and program accounts using Form RC1 or Business Registration Online (BRO). A BC LP typically requires the RT program account (GST/HST), plus RC (income tax) and RP (payroll) accounts where applicable; the partnership is the registered person in its own name and must file T5013 Summary and T5013 information slips. Step 3: Non-resident partners must disclose their foreign TIN to the partnership; a non-resident partner may file the ITA s. 216.1 election to pay Part I tax on net Canadian-source partnership business income instead of Part XIII withholding on gross distributions. Step 4: Foreign corporate partners with no Canadian residence obtain a non-resident BN by submitting RC1, a certified certificate of existence, the partnership agreement, and Form RC24 (authorized representative) to the CRA International and Large Business Directorate; issuance typically takes 15–30 business days.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — a BC Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for federal and BC income tax under ITA s. 96: the partnership computes income or loss as if it were a separate person but pays no entity-level income tax; each partner (individual, corporation, or trust, resident or non-resident) is taxed on its allocated share for the partnership fiscal period. For GST/HST, the partnership is itself a 'person' under ETA s. 123(1) and may register in its own name; each general and limited partner is jointly and severally liable for the partnership's GST/HST debts (ETA s. 272.1). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a BC Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for federal and BC income tax under ITA s. 96: the partnership computes income or loss as if it were a separate person but pays no entity-level income tax; each partner (individual, corporation, or trust, resident or non-resident) is taxed on its allocated share for the partnership fiscal period. For GST/HST, the partnership is itself a 'person' under ETA s. 123(1) and may register in its own name; each general and limited partner is jointly and severally liable for the partnership's GST/HST debts (ETA s. 272.1). The MMORPG publishing scope (ISIC 5820 / NAICS 511210) is an ordinary digital software business with a Low_Risk_Standard AML tier and no statutory licence requirement. BC PST registration (7%) is required only for taxable sales of prewritten software consumed in BC; export-only digital supply is PST-exempt.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box entity classification election. Elections available to a BC LP include: (1) partnership fiscal-period election under ITA s. 96(3) to adopt a non-calendar year-end with partner consent; (2) ITA s. 216.1 election for non-resident partners to be taxed on net Canadian-source partnership business income; (3) GST/HST annual or quarterly filing elections and the 'quick method' election for small suppliers (ETA s. 301); (4) ETA s. 156 election for closely related registrants to treat intercompany supplies as made for no consideration, and registration under ETA s. 272 enabling the LP to file a single consolidated GST/HST return as a partnership; (5) hybrid-mismatch neutrality rules under ITA ss. 18.4(3)–(4), which apply automatically where a non-resident partner's home jurisdiction treats the Canadian LP as opaque (BEPS Action 2), with no blanket exemption election but possible CRA consequential adjustments; (6) late-filed elections may be accepted under ITA s. 220(2.1) fairness relief upon penalty.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 148: aggregate worldwide taxable supplies (goods, services, and intangible digital property, excluding financial supplies) over the current and previous three consecutive calendar quarters. A BC LP whose worldwide revenue exceeds CAD 30,000 must register for GST/HST and charge 5% GST on Canadian in-app purchases. Non-resident suppliers of digital services to Canadians are subject to the simplified foreign-supplier registration regime (effective 2021-07-01) once their worldwide digital-supply revenue exceeds CAD 30,000 in any 12-month period; the Canadian-resident LP uses the standard four-quarter test. Voluntary registration below the threshold is permitted but binds the LP to collect GST for 12 months and bars de-registration for 2 years.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A BC LP may apply the 0% GST export rate to: (i) supplies of intangible personal property (MMORPG software licences, virtual items, in-app purchases) made to non-resident persons for use wholly outside Canada under ETA Schedule VI, Part III, s. 10; and (ii) services supplied to non-residents under ETA s. 23(2) and Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7, provided the service is not performed in Canada or made for use in Canada. Revenue sourced through Apple/Google platforms is treated as supply to the non-resident end user; the LP must attribute each transaction to a foreign jurisdiction using IP geolocation, billing address, and app-store territory reports. Supplies to Canadian end users attract 5% GST and, where taxable, 7% PST on prewritten software. A zero-rated exporter files the GST/HST return (GST34) and may claim input tax credits (ITCs) on development, cloud-hosting, and marketing costs; refunds arise where ITCs exceed output GST. CRA may require security or withhold refunds where supporting export documentation is deficient.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A via CBSA/CERS) — required only for tangible media physically shipped from Canada (e.g., collector's editions); pure digital downloads are not customs-reportable
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill — required only for physical exports; for digital MMORPG delivery, retain Apple/Google payout reports, server delivery logs, and content-delivery-network logs as the shipping equivalent
  • Inward Remittance Certificate — Canada does not issue a statutory inward remittance certificate; the equivalent evidence is SWIFT MT103 advices, foreign-currency bank statements, and app-store payout reports demonstrating conversion into CAD
  • Apple App Store and Google Play payout statements (monthly financial reports) showing app identifier, territory-wise net revenue, store commissions, FX rates, and CAD settlement amounts
  • End-user licence agreement (EUA), MMORPG terms of service, and IP licensing agreements evidencing the grant of digital rights to non-resident users and the non-Canadian place of use
  • IP-geolocation logs, billing-address records, and storefront country-availability settings evidencing that each transaction's destination is outside Canada
  • T5013 Partnership Information Return, partner allocation schedules, and intercompany agreements (where a foreign distribution affiliate is used) supporting the zero-rated export sales ledger

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API-based stamping, or digital stamp regime exists in Canada or British Columbia. The CRA accepts electronic invoices in any machine-readable format (PDF, XML, JSON, EDI ANSI X12) provided the content requirements of ETA ss. 169–170 and the record-keeping rules of ETA s. 286 are met (vendor name, BN, invoice date, description, consideration, tax rate, and GST amount). The Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework (PEPPOL BIS 3.0) is voluntary; Quebec's phased mandatory B2B e-invoicing (announced for 2024–2027) does not apply to BC. For platform-mediated MMORPG sales, Apple App Store and Google Play payout reports function as the de facto commercial and tax invoice records; the LP should maintain consolidated e-invoices for business-to-business software licensing and digital ledgers for CRA inspection.

Canada imposes no statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline for export proceeds; the 180-day figure is a receivables best-practice benchmark aligned with CRA's ordinary reassessment cycle. Risk exposure: (1) Reassessment — CRA may extend the standard four-year reassessment window to ten years where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default (ETA s. 298), recovering uncollected 5% GST plus compounded daily prescribed arrears interest. (2) Civil penalties — failure to file or remit: ETA s. 280 (5% plus 1% per month up to 12 months); false statements or gross negligence: ETA s. 285 (25% of the misstated amount) and ITA s. 163.2 for income-tax misrepresentations; tax evasion: fines of 50%–250% of the tax sought to be evaded, up to CAD 100,000 on summary conviction or CAD 500,000 on indictment, with imprisonment up to 2 years (summary) or 5 years (indictable) (ETA ss. 327–330). (3) GAAR — artificial arrangements inflating export zero-rating are attackable under ETA s. 274, and ITA s. 247 transfer-pricing penalties apply where a foreign distribution affiliate is interposed. (4) Partnership exposure — ETA s. 272.1 renders each general and limited partner jointly and severally liable for the LP's GST/HST debts, materially eroding limited-liability protection in fraudulent zero-rating cases. (5) Mitigation — retain complete export substantiation for at least six years, obtain CRA advance rulings for novel platform structures, and file the GST/HST return with full digital-export evidence.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility as an IMF Article VIII member and does not impose exchange controls, profit-remittance licensing, investment screening by currency, or CAD conversion requirements on a British Columbia Limited Partnership. Non-resident limited partners may contribute capital in CAD, USD, EUR or any convertible currency and may receive distributions, capital withdrawals and liquidation proceeds without Bank of Canada or Department of Finance approval. The only practical hurdles are: (i) standard bank-level suitability and sanctions screening; (ii) possible Part XIII Income Tax Act withholding on passive-type remittances (interest, royalties, rents) at 25% or reduced treaty rates, while ordinary active-business allocations and return-of-capital payments to non-resident partners are not subject to withholding; (iii) non-resident partners remain taxable on Canadian-source active-business income and must file the required Canadian returns or claim treaty relief; and (iv) enhanced due-diligence documentation when the ownership chain is non-resident or complex.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian federal law imposes no statutory surrender timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation obligation for export proceeds or cross-border revenue. A BC Limited Partnership publishing hardcore MMORPG titles may retain all App Store and Google Play proceeds in USD or other foreign currency indefinitely, may convert at its commercial discretion, and may hold offshore settlement accounts. Only general record-keeping duties apply: FINTRAC large-transaction and suspicious-transaction reporting for deposits and transfers over CAD 10,000 (or equivalent), and Statistics Canada balance-of-payments surveys where the transaction qualifies. No penalty applies for non-conversion, and there is no statutory deadline for bringing export receipts into Canada.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2). Canada does not operate a dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration or exchange-control licensing authority; foreign-exchange restrictions were abolished, and the Minister of Finance holds only dormant emergency powers through the Exchange Fund Account (Currency, Exchange and Special Powers Act), which are not activated. The practical compliance authorities are FINTRAC (Anti-Money Laundering / Counter-Terrorist Financing), Global Affairs Canada (economic sanctions), the Canada Border Services Agency (cross-border currency reporting), and Statistics Canada (balance-of-payments data collection).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-High as a disbursement and multi-currency overlay; Low-Medium as the primary treasury or capital-concentration layer. Canada does not issue EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licences; non-bank cross-border payment providers operate as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Providers such as Wise, Payoneer, Stripe, Nuvei, dLocal and Airwallex can hold client funds in segregated trust accounts at regulated deposit-taking institutions and issue virtual multi-currency accounts, but they are not licensed deposit-takers, their balances are not CDIC-insured, and they cannot serve as the statutory bank account for capital-injection documentation, audit confirmation, or the LP's regulatory profile. Given the target payment rails (Apple_App_Store_Payout and Google_Play_Payout), the recommended architecture is: (i) a Schedule I bank account for all partner capital, operating cash and tax repository; (ii) a US-dollar clearing account (ABA and SWIFT) at the bank's US affiliate or a US partner bank to accept App Store and Google Play settlement; and (iii) a FINTRAC-registered MSB/EMI only for vendor disbursements in emerging-market currencies where the primary bank lacks a local clearing footprint.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Not applicable at the British Columbia limited partnership level because a Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent. Income is allocated to partners and taxed at partner level. Where income is earned by a taxable Canadian corporation, the combined general corporate rate is generally 27%: 15% federal and 12% British Columbia. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of 11% on eligible income within the applicable business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Canadian domestic Part XIII rules for dividends paid or credited to a non-resident. A treaty commonly reduces the rate to 15% for a portfolio dividend and 5% where the treaty ownership and corporate-shareholder conditions are satisfied. A limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend; its Canadian withholding result depends on the legal and tax character of the underlying amount, including business income, interest, royalties, rent, or dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 10,000,000 of gross revenue generally triggers the Canadian documentation exception threshold analysis for contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation, but documentation is required where the statutory conditions otherwise apply to non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Canada does not use a single transaction-value threshold that universally eliminates the arm's-length requirement. Form T106 reporting is generally required for reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, subject to prescribed exceptions.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Substance remains relevant through the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245, beneficial-ownership and treaty anti-abuse principles, sham and substance-over-form doctrines, specified anti-avoidance rules, foreign accrual property income rules, thin-capitalization rules, and principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions under applicable treaties. A Canadian LP conducting global MMORPG publishing should maintain real Canadian functions, personnel, decision-making, development or publishing activities, contractual authority, risk control, assets, records, and commercially supportable profit allocation. Apple and Google platform settlements must be reconciled to contractual revenue ownership and transfer-pricing positions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties can include 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, reassessment of tax, interest, and denial of deductions. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act, with more serious Criminal Code exposure in appropriate cases.

Director personal liability: General partners and responsible directors or officers may face personal liability for source deductions and other statutory remittances, civil recovery, prosecution, and imprisonment where personal participation or authorization is proven. Canadian law does not impose an automatic travel ban for tax debt; travel restrictions require a specific legal basis or court order.
Failure to withhold and remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted withholding, arrears interest, late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and costs of collection. False NR4 information or intentional non-remittance may support criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for prescribed source deductions and remittances under the Income Tax Act where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. General partners may also face direct exposure under partnership and tax law.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on relevant adjusted amounts where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, assess withholding, apply interest, and invoke the general anti-avoidance rule.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face civil recovery and, where records are knowingly false or conduct is wilful, criminal prosecution. Liability is not automatic solely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made.
Shell company, treaty shopping, or beneficial-ownership abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the 25% domestic withholding rate, reassess tax and interest, impose penalties, and apply the treaty's principal-purpose, limitation-on-benefits, or Canadian general anti-avoidance provisions.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly implement false residency or beneficial-ownership representations may face civil penalties, director or officer liability for statutory remittances, and criminal consequences for fraud or false statements. No automatic corporate veil piercing occurs merely because an entity has limited substance.
GST/HST and British Columbia PST non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Uncollected GST/HST or PST, interest, late-filing penalties, failure-to-register penalties, denial of input tax credits, audit assessments, and penalties for false returns may apply. Platform-specific marketplace rules must be tested against the actual supply chain.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for GST/HST amounts collected or required to be remitted where statutory due diligence is not established. General partners may face partnership-level and personal exposure under applicable legislation.
Payroll and worker misclassification

Statutory sanction: The entity may owe unremitted income-tax deductions, Canada Pension Plan contributions, Employment Insurance premiums, interest, penalties, reassessed benefits, and employment-law amounts. Deliberate false reporting may support criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for employee source deductions and certain payroll remittances. Officers may face prosecution for wilful non-compliance.
Anti-money-laundering, sanctions, and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include transaction blocking, asset restraint or forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties, FINTRAC reporting failures, prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and Criminal Code offences. Sanctions breaches may produce substantial fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and general partners may be prosecuted for knowing participation, counselling, or wilful blindness. Assets may be restrained or forfeited; travel restrictions are not automatic but may arise from bail conditions, warrants, or court orders.
Corporate-record, partnership, and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Penalties, reassessments, denial of limited-partner tax treatment, loss of statutory protections, corporate or partnership compliance orders, and potential offences under federal or British Columbia corporate legislation may apply.

Director personal liability: A limited partner may risk loss of limited-liability protection if it participates in control contrary to applicable partnership law. General partners have broader exposure for partnership obligations. Directors and officers may face statutory penalties and civil claims for inaccurate records.
