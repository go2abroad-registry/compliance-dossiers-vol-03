Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal administration of the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act (GST/HST); official portal: www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency. Provincial sales tax (PST) is separately administered by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance via the eTaxBC portal (www.sbr.gov.bc.ca). Business registration of the limited partnership is effected through BC Registries and Corporate Online (www.bcregistry.ca).

E-government filing portal

(1) First constitute the BC limited partnership under the Partnership Act, RSBC 1996, c. 348 by filing a Declaration of Limited Partnership via BC Corporate Online; the provincial registration number is issued. (2) The foreign general partner or designated partner then applies to the CRA for a federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) using Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number', either online through CRA 'Register my business' or by mail, selecting 'GST/HST' and 'Payroll/Income Tax' accounts as applicable. (3) A non-resident applicant may use the RC1 non-resident registration stream; CRA will assign the BN and a coincident GST/HST account number (e.g., BN + 'RT' program identifier). (4) If the LP makes taxable sales in British Columbia, a separate PST account number must be obtained from the BC Ministry of Finance through eTaxBC. (5) The LP must then file a T5013 Partnership Information Return (T5013 SUMMARY and T5013 slips) with the CRA; each partner must separately report their allocated share of income using their own BN, Social Insurance Number, or Canadian tax identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a BC limited partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent under section 96 of the Income Tax Act: the partnership itself is not a taxpayer and does not pay Part I tax; each partner, including non-resident limited partners, is subject to tax on their share of the LP's income. Non-resident partners are taxable only on income allocated from Canadian-source partnership activities, subject to applicable income tax treaty provisions. The LP files an information return only.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' election; partnership transparency is mandatory. Available elections include: (a) voluntary GST/HST registration under section 240 of the Excise Tax Act even when the CAD 30,000 threshold is not met; (b) the 'quick method' of accounting election for GST/HST; (c) a partnership taxation year election under subsection 249.1(4) of the Income Tax Act; and (d) designation of a 'designated member' as the partner responsible for the T5013 information return. With respect to hybrid mismatches, the Canadian hybrid mismatch arrangement rules (implemented pursuant to OECD BEPS Action 2) apply automatically without an elective carve-out; the LP must self-assess against deduction-without-inclusion outcomes involving its Canadian and foreign partners.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: mandatory registration where the LP's total taxable supplies, including those made outside Canada, exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters (Excise Tax Act s. 240); voluntary registration is available regardless of threshold. BC PST: registration becomes mandatory from the first day of the month following the month in which the LP's taxable sales of goods or services in British Columbia and eligible remote sales exceed CAD 10,000 in the preceding 12-month period; a small-seller exemption applies below this threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

0% GST applies to (i) goods exported from Canada in accordance with the export conditions of Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 of the Excise Tax Act, and (ii) services supplied to a non-resident under Schedule VI, Part V, section 9, provided the recipient is not a Canadian-resident GST registrant acquiring the supply for use in Canada and the supply is not associated with Canadian real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. Creator-management, talent-agency, and digital MCN services performed for non-resident TikTok creators qualify for zero-rating where the place-of-supply rules and the section 9 conditions are satisfied; intangible property rights supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated under section 10 where the rights are not used in Canada. The LP must substantiate the export nature of every supply even when payment is received through TikTok creator payouts, Wise B2B rails, or foreign-currency bank wire.

GST/HST returns are filed electronically via the CRA 'GST/HST NETFILE' (mandatory for registrants with annual revenue above CAD 1,000,000); BC PST returns are filed through the eTaxBC portal. Although the CRA accepts EDI and API-based bulk filing for high-volume registrants, no pre-clearance API or government-mandated XML invoicing schema applies to the export of services.

CRA export-validation audits require full transactional traceability from contract and service performance through invoicing, payment, and foreign-currency credit. The standard GST reassessment period is four years from the date of the return; it is extended to ten years where misrepresentation, wilful default, or fraudulent tax evasion is established (Excise Tax Act s. 298(4) and Income Tax Act s. 152(4)). Civil penalties include: Excise Tax Act s. 280 (tax evasion — 25% of the GST sought to be evaded plus compound daily interest under s. 280.1); Excise Tax Act s. 285.1 (false statements — CAD 500 plus 25% of the additional tax); Income Tax Act s. 163(2) (gross negligence — 50% of the understated or omitted tax); and Information Act Part I failure-to-file penalties. Criminal prosecution is available under Excise Tax Act s. 327 and Income Tax Act s. 239 for knowingly making false statements or evading tax. Erroneously claimed export zero-rating is clawed back by reassessing the full supply at 5% GST, reversing any claimed input tax credits or refunds, and applying interest and penalties retroactively from the original filing date.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a free-floating currency with full convertibility on both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange-control approvals for profit remittance, dividend distributions, or capital repatriation by a British Columbia limited partnership (BC LP). A BC LP with non-resident partners may freely retain foreign currency, use natural hedging, and repatriate funds; the only practical hurdles are Part XIII withholding tax considerations on certain payments to non-residents, GST/HST registration for taxable activity, and FINTRAC reporting for cross-border electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more. The LP's pass-through status triggers filing obligations for non-resident partners under Part I of the Income Tax Act, but this does not restrict currency convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose any statutory surrender timeline, conversion ratio, or directed disposition for export receipts or service payments. Exporting entities may hold USD, EUR, or any freely convertible currency in Canadian bank foreign-currency accounts without central bank approval. However, Canadian banks automatically report all international electronic funds transfers (ETF) of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); no dedicated foreign exchange administration exists. Indirect FX oversight is exercised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money laundering, and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for regulated bank prudential soundness.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High for low-value creator payouts; Low as the primary treasury and settlement rail. Wise, Airwallex, and Payoneer operate as FINTRAC-registered money services businesses (MSBs) in Canada and may legally provide CAD/USD cross-border disbursements supporting embedded FX. Legal constraints: (i) an offshore EMI without Canadian MSB registration cannot lawfully settle CAD payments to/from Canadian clients, thereby exposing the BC LP to unauthorized financial-services risk; (ii) funds held with an EMI are not bank deposits and are not covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation; (iii) the LP must aggregate and report ETF and large cash transactions to FINTRAC where thresholds are exceeded; (iv) Canadian chartered banks may reject activity routed from a non-bank EMI, so funds should be laundered through the LP's primary bank account on a periodic basis.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound partner capital contributions must be sent by SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for interbank) to the LP's designated beneficiary account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. Protocol: (i) use the LP's full legal name and BC registration number in beneficiary field; (ii) state in remittance information 'LP capital contribution — [partner name]'; (iii) transmit using bank identifier / transit code from TD or RBC; (iv) have the partner bank issue via an upstream correspondent with no impeding intermediate banks; (v) pre-notify the Canadian bank with a company resolution, capital call notice, and definitive source-of-funds evidence; (vi) classify the receipt for Canadian BoP statistics under 'Direct Investment — Equity Capital — Inbound'. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A British Columbia limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not pay income tax at the partnership level. Taxable income is allocated to partners. For a taxable Canadian corporation, the federal general corporate rate is 15% and the British Columbia general corporate rate is 12%, producing a combined general rate of 27%. The combined small-business rate is generally 11% on qualifying active business income within the applicable federal and provincial business limits.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. A limited partnership normally does not pay dividends; distributions generally represent partnership allocations or returns of capital. A corporate partner may separately distribute dividends, which can trigger Part XIII withholding. Treaty rates commonly reduce dividend withholding to 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% for other portfolio holdings, but the applicable treaty and limitation-on-benefits or beneficial-ownership conditions must be reviewed.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no universal CAD transaction threshold that eliminates the contemporaneous-documentation requirement under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. A T106 filing is generally required where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million in aggregate or another statutory condition applies. Country-by-country reporting and master-file obligations generally apply to constituent entities of multinational enterprise groups meeting the OECD consolidated-revenue threshold of EUR 750 million, subject to Canadian implementation rules. Local-file and master-file expectations should be assessed by reference to the taxpayer's transactions, group status, and CRA documentation requirements.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian authorities apply substance-over-form principles, the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, beneficial-ownership analysis, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, principal-purpose concepts where applicable through treaties, and the requirement that expenses be incurred to earn income. A Canadian LP should maintain commercially credible functions, personnel, decision-making, contractual authority, premises or operating capacity where appropriate, bank controls, creator-management records, and evidence supporting the allocation of platform revenue, commissions, royalties, and intercompany service charges. A conduit or nominal agency structure may be challenged notwithstanding formal legal documentation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, tax, interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act. Tax evasion may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years, subject to the applicable offence and court determination.

Director personal liability: General partners, directors, officers, and other individuals who participate in the conduct may face criminal prosecution, fines, restitution, and director or officer liability for certain source deductions and GST/HST amounts. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty; a court may impose release conditions, warrants, or other orders in appropriate criminal proceedings.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted amount, applicable penalties, and interest. Failure-to-deduct or failure-to-remit penalties can be significant and may be imposed separately from the underlying tax. NR4 reporting failures may generate additional information-return penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed under the Income Tax Act director-liability rules for unremitted source deductions where statutory conditions are met, subject to due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. General partners may also face liability under partnership law and the partnership agreement.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, impose a transfer-pricing adjustment penalty generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, and impose interest. Failure to maintain contemporaneous documentation may restrict penalty protection.

Director personal liability: Individuals are not automatically liable merely because a transfer-pricing reassessment occurs. Personal exposure may arise from knowing participation in false statements, fraud, obstruction, or source-deduction failures. A general partner remains exposed under the applicable partnership and civil-liability rules.
Shell company, nominee ownership, or sham agency

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, recharacterize income, assess tax and interest, impose civil penalties, and refer deliberate deception for criminal prosecution. Corporate registry, beneficial-ownership, and tax-information obligations may also be engaged.

Director personal liability: General partners and controlling individuals may face civil claims, penalties, criminal charges, asset recovery, and liability for dishonest or fraudulent conduct. Corporate separateness is not a defense to personal fraud or statutory director liability. Travel bans are not automatic and require a specific legal or judicial basis.
AML, proceeds of crime, and sanctions

Statutory sanction: Potential offences under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, Criminal Code, and sanctions legislation include administrative monetary penalties, account or asset restrictions, forfeiture, substantial fines, and imprisonment. FINTRAC reporting, client-identification, recordkeeping, and suspicious-transaction duties may apply depending on the business activities and registration status.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and responsible managers may face prosecution, personal fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and compliance orders for knowing participation or wilful blindness. Banking, payment-platform, and regulatory account closures may occur independently of tax proceedings.
GST/HST and indirect-tax non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of uncollected GST/HST, denial of input tax credits, interest, gross-negligence penalties, filing penalties, and potential prosecution for deliberate false reporting. Registration and place-of-supply analysis must be performed independently from income-tax residence analysis.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST under statutory director-liability provisions, subject to due-diligence and limitation rules. General partners may have direct exposure under the partnership structure and applicable provincial law.
Failure to preserve books and records

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny deductions, credits, treaty reductions, and loss allocations; impose records-related penalties; make indirect or net-worth assessments; and infer facts adverse to the taxpayer. Records generally must be retained for the statutory period, commonly at least six years from the end of the relevant year unless a longer period or ongoing objection or litigation applies.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face penalties or prosecution where record destruction or obstruction is deliberate. Directors and general partners may also face civil claims for breach of fiduciary, statutory, or partnership duties.
