Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the GST/HST and federal income tax programs, administered through the CRA 'My Business Account' portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); provincial indirect sales tax is administered separately by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch, through the eTaxBC online portal (https://etax.gov.bc.ca/).

E-government filing portal

For a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) owned by non-residents: (1) incorporate or continue the ULC under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act through BC Corporate Online and obtain the BC incorporation number; (2) apply to the CRA for a federal Business Number (BN) using Form RC1 or the Business Registration Online service, and activate the required CRA program accounts, including an income tax account, a GST/HST account (BN followed by 'RT'), a payroll source deductions account if employees are placed, and an import/export account where goods will cross the border; (3) register with the BC Ministry of Finance for a British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST) account through eTaxBC, because PST is a separate retail sales tax administered provincially; (4) register with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal for customs security and the export reporting program where the ULC will export goods; and (5) maintain the CRA BN and PST account details in all invoices, customs declarations, GST/HST returns, and provincial tax filings. Non-resident applicants must provide an agent for service in Canada if required by the CRA or the Registrar of Companies, and the CRA may request financial security or an undertaking from non-resident registrants seeking refund-based zero-rating.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box election equivalent to U.S. Treasury Regulations section 301.7701. A BC ULC is a legal corporation under the Business Corporations Act and is subject to entity-level Canadian income tax under the Income Tax Act; however, it is a hybrid entity for certain foreign tax systems. For U.S. federal tax purposes, a Canadian ULC is eligible to be treated as a corporation or, where permitted, as a disregarded entity or partnership for a U.S. owner by filing U.S. Form 8832, such that losses and tax attributes may flow through for U.S. purposes while the entity remains taxable in Canada. For Canadian GST/HST purposes, closely related corporations may jointly elect to have one member act as the electronic filing agent or to use group registration rules; registrants may also make certain elections respecting financial services and closely related supplies under the Excise Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act restrict deductions or inclusions where cross-border hybrid arrangements produce double non-taxation or deduction/non-inclusion mismatches. No territorial tax election is available; Canada generally taxes resident corporations on worldwide income and is not a tax-exempt zone for this entity.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 taxable supply threshold measured over four consecutive calendar quarters for GST/HST registration as a small supplier; however, a non-resident or manufacturer making export sales that apply zero-rating should voluntarily register to claim input tax credits. For BC PST, registration is required when the ULC makes taxable sales or leases of goods in British Columbia; there is no equivalent general CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for all PST vendors, and the ULC must register before collecting PST if goods are sold or delivered to BC customers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A manufacturer of electric cargo bicycles may zero-rate the GST/HST on the supply of tangible personal property exported from Canada where the supplier transfers ownership and possession of the goods to the purchaser and the goods are delivered outside Canada by the supplier or an agent, or shipped by common carrier, courier, mail, or international parcel service to a destination outside Canada, and adequate evidence of export is retained. The zero-rating is claimed under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act; the same supply is not subject to PST when the goods are exported outside British Columbia, provided the vendor keeps the prescribed evidence. The zero-rating does not depend on the separate ULC check-the-box classification but instead requires that the manufacturer prove the goods physically left Canada and that the sale was made to a person outside Canada or to a registered non-resident intermediary under the drop-shipment rules. Manufacturing inputs bear GST/HST and PST where applicable, but the ULC claims input tax credits to obtain a refund for federal GST embedded in inputs; exported electric cargo bikes generally qualify for zero-rating if the sale is not to a Canadian resident and the goods are not consumed before export.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A or the approved electronic equivalent filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System/CBSA Single Window/CARM), including HS classification applicable to electric cargo bicycles and the declared value
  • Commercial invoice with complete product description, unit prices, HS commodity code, Incoterms, seller and foreign buyer identification, and stated export destination
  • Bill of lading or airway bill (or comparable truck/rail manifest) evidencing movement from Canada to the foreign destination, together with packing list and weight manifest
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank remittance advice, such as SWIFT MT103/MT202 or foreign exchange deal ticket, confirming receipt of foreign currency proceeds from the non-resident buyer

Canada does not require real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or government digital stamping for standard GST/HST invoices. GST/HST registrants must issue tax invoices containing prescribed information set out in section 223 of the Excise Tax Act, including supplier name and BN, taxable supply description, consideration, GST payable or zero-rating status, and tax invoice date. Electronic invoices are permitted if presented in a readable and accessible format, and B2B electronic invoicing is increasingly implemented voluntarily using the Pan-American/Pan-Canadian Peppol interoperability framework, but no statutory e-invoice clearance API mandate currently applies at the federal level. Exports are reported digitally through CBSA Single Window/CARM and eManifest systems, and GST/HST returns must be filed electronically or by prescribed electronic means for registrants carrying on selected commercial activities; provincial PST has no separate invoice clearance system.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There is no general exchange-control law, no currency rationing, and no prior approval for inward equity subscriptions, dividends, interest, royalties, or loan repayments by non-resident shareholders, save that any remittance-out must satisfy Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, corporate-continuity documentation, and transfer-pricing substance rules under section 247. A BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (CA_ULC) is treated as eligible for corporate tax passthrough and may repatriate profits by dividend or shareholder distribution after CRA assessment. Cross-border payments are screened by regulated financial institutions for sanctions under the United Nations Act and the Special Economic Measures Act; for this ISIC 3091 e-cargo-bike OEM, no Canadian controlled-entity license issuing restriction applies because the activity is uncontaminated civilian manufacturing.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender or conversion requirement. There is no mandated timeline for bringing foreign currency into Canada and no mandatory conversion ratio to Canadian dollars. Export proceeds invoiced in USD, EUR, or CNY may be held offshore or in a domestic Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency account at a Schedule I bank. For Canadian corporate tax purposes, revenue is recognized at Bank of Canada annual/jurisdiction exchange rates, and any unrealized FX gains are booked for accounting subject to the substantive-entity and transfer-pricing documentation of CA_ULC. Export documentation, including Canada Customs Invoice and proof of export, must be retained for CRA audit and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) verification, but none of these steps create a forced surrender or conversion ratio.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), together with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as AML/ATF supervisor, and Global Affairs Canada for trade controls. Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange control bureau and does not maintain a two-tier currency market.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Use SWIFT MT103 or wire transfer (TT) from the non-resident shareholder/parent treasury to the CA_ULC Schedule I bank account in British Columbia. The sender must include the exact legal name of the ULC, the ULC's BC corporate registry number, and a clear remittance narrative: 'equity subscription – CA_ULC unlimited liability corporation – no personal relief'. Canada has no prescribed foreign-exchange purchase form; however, FINTRAC requires reporting of incoming international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more in a reporting period, and the receiving bank must capture the originator and beneficiary details. Under the Investment Canada Act, a new manufacturing business in BC by a non-Canadian investor is generally 'establishing a new Canadian business' and may require notification rather than net-benefit review; still file the standard business-activity notification within 30 days of establishment. The bank should verify the corporate resolution for the capital injection, confirm UBO declaration is current, and classify the transaction as a direct trade-inward treasury operation, not as a loan requiring form T106 on interest, and the ULC accountant should record the share subscription at fair value.For international supplier settlements, use SWIFT MT103 for direct payments under CAD/USD gross amounts up to transaction thresholds and Commercial Letters of Credit (UCP600) for material high-value purchases of battery cells, motors, IMUs, and frames where performance risk must be mitigated. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.00%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25.00% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on deemed dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. Because a BC ULC is classified as a Canadian corporation, it is responsible for withholding on any distribution to a non-resident shareholder, including a dividend that may be deemed under the tax rules when a ULC is wound up or when a non-resident shareholder transfers shares.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed on or before the day that is six (6) months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. For a fiscal year-end of December 31, the deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year. Balance due (final tax liability net of instalments) is due on or before the last day of the second (2nd) month following the fiscal year-end; for a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) with a qualifying small business deduction and business income, the balance-due day is the last day of the third (3rd) month following the fiscal year-end. A ULC is generally a public corporation unless it is a CCPC, so the balance-due day is generally the second month after year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For fiscal years beginning after January 1, 2023, the CRA's TP documentation threshold is based on the transaction size. A Local File is required where the total value of controlled transactions with a non-resident, per transaction category, exceeds CAD 1 million (for services) or CAD 5 million for tangible goods, intangible property, and intercompany financing. A Master File is required where the MNE group has consolidated revenue exceeding CAD 1 billion and has at least CAD 100 million in Canadian revenue. Specifically, the Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arms's-Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed by any Canadian taxpayer with controlled transactions exceeding an aggregate of CAD 1 million in a fiscal year. For a BC ULC manufacturing electric cargo e-bikes, all cross-border transactions with a non-resident shareholder (e.g., component purchases, sales of finished e-bikes to foreign distributors, licensing of battery-management software, and intercompany financing) above CAD 1 million per category require contemporaneous TP documentation (Local File) and a T106 filing. Total annual penalty for failure to file T106 or T1134 (foreign affiliates) is CAD 2,500 per month for each incomplete or missing filing, up to 24 months, maximum CAD 60,000; additional penalties of CAD 2,000 per infraction for false statements. A gross-negligence penalty under subsection 247(5) applies to TP adjustments where the taxpayer fails to use arm's-length transfer prices: 10% of the net adjustment (if assessed), plus 50% of the interest on the adjustment, unless the taxpayer made reasonable efforts and has contemporaneous documentation.

Economic substance scope: For a BC ULC, the Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as applied in offshore financial centres are not directly applicable. However, under Canadian domestic anti-avoidance rules, section 245 GAAR, subsection 247(2) TP rules, and the general substance-over-form doctrines apply. The ULC must have real substance: physical production facilities, employees, management decisions in BC, and documented operational activity. A ULC used solely for holding shares or IP and deferring tax may be attacked under GAAR or, if it is a resident of Canada under common law (central management and control), its worldwide income is subject to Canadian tax. Canada has no explicit Economic Substance Regulations for onshore companies; instead, CRA applies residency, PE allocation, and transfer pricing rules. For treaty-shopping structures, the DTA residence tie-breaker may negate treaty benefits if central management and control is outside Canada, but a ULC incorporated in BC remains a Canadian resident by incorporation. The ULC's unlimited liability feature is not a form of tax shelter; CRA may disregard the separate legal existence of a ULC if it is used abusively.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Wilful Evasion of Income Tax)

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act, every person who wilfully evades or attempts to evade tax is guilty of an offence and is liable to a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, or imprisonment of up to two years, or both. Under the CRA's criminal prosecutions, a fine of up to CAD 1,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years may apply for certain indictable offences (subsection 239(2)). Civil penalties include reassessment with gross-negligence penalties of 50% of unreported income under subsection 163(2).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in tax evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid taxes, source deductions, GST/HST, and Part XIII withholding under section 160 and subsection 227.1(1). Directors may be personally assessed for up to 100% of unremitted withheld amounts. For egregious cases, CRA may issue a section 160 transfer assessment against shareholders who receive assets from the ULC at below fair market value. Personal travel bans apply upon criminal conviction; imprisonment of up to two years may be imposed. Directors cannot indemnify themselves from section 227.1 liability through corporate indemnification.
Shell Company / Abusive Tax Avoidance (GAAR)

Statutory sanction: Under section 245 of the Income Tax Act, the CRA may disregard the avoidance transaction and reassess the tax consequences, resulting in additional tax plus arrears interest and penalties. A GAAR reassessment typically nullifies the deduction, credit, or treaty benefit. Penalties of up to 50% of the additional tax under subsection 163(2) may apply if the taxpayer knowingly makes false statements. The CRA may also apply transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the net adjustment plus 50% of the associated interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize sham transactions may be personally liable for gross negligence penalties, additional taxes, and interest under subsection 227.1(1) if the corporation is unable to pay. The privilege of limited liability does not apply to a ULC's shareholders (unlimited liability), and directors face common-law liability for fraudulent conveyance if assets are moved to defeat creditors. CRA can pursue foreign-based directors through treaty mutual assistance; travel bans and extradition may be possible in criminal cases.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering)

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), failing to report a large cash transaction or a terrorist financing suspicion carries a fine of up to CAD 500,000 for a first offence and up to CAD 1,000,000 for a subsequent offence on summary conviction; on indictment, fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and/or imprisonment up to 5 years. Non-registration as an MSB carries up to CAD 500,000 per violation.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to establish compliance procedures are subject to administrative monetary penalties (AMP) by FINTRAC up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation. In criminal cases, officers can be sentenced to up to five years imprisonment. BC's Business Corporations Act also imposes personal liability for dividends paid while insolvent and for fraudulent conveyances. A travel ban may be imposed as part of bail conditions; extradition is possible for international laundering offences.
Transfer Pricing (Non-Arm's-Length Misstatement)

Statutory sanction: Under section 247(5), the CRA may impose a penalty equal to 10% of the adjusted transfer-pricing net adjustment, reduced by the amount of the gross-negligence penalty, plus 50% of the interest associated with the adjustment. Unfiled T106 penalties: CAD 2,500 per month per form, maximum 24 months, up to CAD 60,000. For a larger MNE group, a missing Master File or Local File may attract a CAD 1,000 per day penalty (for each failure) after the first 90 days. CRA may also assess additional tax at the regular corporate rate on the reallocated income, plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly cause false TP documentation may be assessed for gross-negligence penalties and, under subsection 239(1), be subject to imprisonment for up to two years. The CRA often assesses directors personally for amounts the corporation failed to withhold or remit, but not for ordinary corporate tax unless they exercised direction with intent. Personal liability can extend to foreign directors if they have been involved in decision-making and the corporation is bankrupt or cannot be collected from.
Ultra Vires Acts and Unlawful Distributions under BC Law

Statutory sanction: Under the BC Business Corporations Act, a director who votes for or consents to an unlawful distribution is personally liable to restore the amount of the distribution or pay compensation, and may be liable to the corporation for damages. A shareholder of a ULC has unlimited liability for the debts of the corporation during the time they are a shareholder, so the shareholder may be sued directly by creditors for corporate debts upon winding up. The Act does not permit shareholders to contract out of liabilities to creditors.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unlawful distributions; a court may order restitution from directors and from recipients who knew the distribution was unlawful. Shareholders of a ULC have unlimited personal liability for the corporation's debts, liabilities, and obligations to the extent the corporation's assets are insufficient. This includes tort and environmental liabilities. There is no statutory cap on shareholder liability. In bankruptcy, the trustee may sue shareholders and directors for fraudulent preferences under the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act. Travel bans are not automatic, but personal bankruptcy consequences can severely restrict travel and business.
