Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal tax administration; official portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) and CRA My Business Account (MBC) for GST/HST, corporate income tax (T2), payroll, and import/export accounts. Provincial indirect tax authority for British Columbia: BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch (BC Taxpayer Portal) for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) registration, returns, and audit.

E-government filing portal

For a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Company (ULC): (1) incorporate under the BC Business Corporations Act, SBC 2002, c. 57, through the BC Corporate Online/OneStop BC Registry; (2) obtain the incorporation certificate and the BC incorporation number; (3) register with CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) to obtain the nine-digit federal Business Number (BN), establishing a corporate income tax (RC) account, a GST/HST (RT) account, a payroll (RP) account where employees exist, and an import/export (RD) account where required; (4) the ULC must file a T2 corporation income tax return annually and pay federal/provincial corporate income tax instalments as applicable; (5) register for the CRA GST/HST account using the RT program account — registration is mandatory if worldwide taxable supplies in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over any four consecutive calendar quarters, or the entity may voluntarily register to claim input tax credits; (6) register separately with the BC Ministry of Finance for provincial sales tax (PST) before making taxable sales in BC, using the BC Taxpayer Portal, and obtain a PST vendor number; (7) if the ULC is wholly foreign-owned, ensure compliance with the Investment Canada Act filing requirements and maintain a registered BC office, as this creates the PST nexus and CRA permanent-establishment nexus.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a general check-the-box election analogous to U.S. Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3. However, a foreign-controlled BC/AB ULC may achieve pass-through-equivalent tax outcomes through the following mechanisms: (i) where the ULC is fiscally transparent in the beneficial owner's jurisdiction, Canada may respect the transparent treatment for treaty and domestic purposes, subject to the CRA's administrative positions on unlimited liability corporations and to Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (Income Tax Act ss. 18.2–18.4); (ii) for U.S. tax purposes, a Canadian ULC may file an entity classification election (check-the-box) to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership, while remaining a Canadian corporation for Income Tax Act purposes — this creates a hybrid entity, and the CRA may apply the anti-hybrid rules to deny deductions for certain interest/dividend payments; (iii) non-resident owners may elect under Income Tax Act s. 216(1) (net-basis taxation of Canadian rental/business income) or s. 217 (non-resident employment income), as appropriate; (iv) under the Excise Tax Act (ETA), a small supplier may elect to register for GST/HST voluntarily to claim input tax credits, and closely related persons may make a joint election under ETA s. 179 to treat certain taxable supplies between them as made for nil consideration, subject to BC PST non-application; (v) the ULC may elect under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty to be treated as a resident of Canada for treaty purposes if it is subject to tax as a corporation, thereby limiting U.S. withholding on treaty-eligible income flows.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 of gross worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or in the last four consecutive calendar quarters — below this, the registrant is a 'small supplier' exempt from mandatory registration, although voluntary registration is available. BC PST: no general monetary de minimis exemption for a BC resident vendor; a person carrying on business in BC through a permanent establishment, place of business, employee, agent, or inventory located in BC must register and commence PST collection before making taxable sales. Remote sellers and marketplace facilitators selling into BC may be required to register for BC PST under the province's marketplace rules, with a CAD 10,000 gross-sales nexus trigger applicable to certain out-of-province non-resident digital or mail-order vendors.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (ETA), a supply of goods is zero-rated at 0% GST where the supplier exports the goods itself in the course of a commercial export operation, or delivers the goods (or causes them to be delivered) to an address outside Canada, and the export is substantiated by documentary evidence. For online cross-border retail of dietary supplements and nutraceuticals, zero-rating applies where the BC ULC consigns the goods to a non-resident customer, title and risk of loss pass outside Canada, shipment is made by common carrier, courier, or post to a foreign destination, and the goods are exported as soon as is commercially practicable. Services supplied to a non-resident may also be zero-rated where the services are supplied in conjunction with exported goods or are not for use substantially in Canada. Zero-rated export sales preserve the supplier's entitlement to full input tax credits for the 5% GST incurred on Canadian inputs. BC PST does not apply to goods exported from BC; an export exemption certificate must be retained to substantiate PST-exempt export sales. Domestic sales of nutraceuticals in Canada remain subject to 5% GST (and 7% BC PST where applicable); only the export supply qualifies for zero-rating.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration and Canadian Export Reporting System (CAERS) export report, or Canada Post/courier proof of export for low-value and postal shipments
  • Signed commercial invoice containing the ULC as seller, the non-resident customer, foreign destination address, HS/ISIC commodity description, quantity, value, and currency
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or courier waybill evidencing the movement of the goods from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent banking/payment-processor settlement report evidencing receipt of foreign-currency consideration (Canada does not require a statutory inward remittance certificate, but the CRA may require proof of payment in an audit)
  • Purchase order or export sales contract with the non-resident customer
  • BC PST Export Exemption Certificate or equivalent evidence supporting the PST exemption on exported goods
  • Exporter's internal logistics record identifying the mode of transport, departure date, port/office of exit, and the foreign destination

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) regime for GST/HST or BC PST. The CRA mandates electronic filing of GST/HST returns once a registrant's annual gross revenue exceeds the applicable threshold (currently CAD 1.5 million for standard registrants) and accepts any electronic invoice format meeting ETA books-and-records requirements. BC requires PST returns, remittances, and registration changes to be filed electronically through the BC Taxpayer Portal. Canada is participating in the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and the Peppol BIS 3.0 e-invoicing framework on a voluntary basis; there is no statutory government pre-approval of individual invoices. Exporters must nonetheless maintain a complete machine-readable audit trail (sales invoice, shipment manifest, export declaration, payment processor settlement data, and GL tax account reconciliation) accessible for CRA and BC Ministry of Finance audit requests.

The CRA audits zero-rated export claims using cross-agency trade data (CBSA export declarations, carrier manifests, courier records, payment processor settlements) and may reassess to deny zero-rating where the exporter cannot produce the mandatory supporting documentation. Denial exposes the exporter to GST at 5% plus interest, and the BC Ministry of Finance may separately assess PST if taxable goods were inappropriately exempted. Fraudulent or gross-negligent claims attract: (i) a false-statement penalty of CAD 100 plus 25% of the net tax sought to be evaded (ETA s. 280); (ii) a gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the understated tax (ETA s. 285); (iii) an extended normal reassessment period (no limitation period where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default is established, and an eight-year period for large corporations); and (iv) criminal prosecution under ETA s. 327(1), carrying a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment, or imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction, in addition to the clawback of any refunds, input tax credits, or rebates claimed. Shareholders of a BC ULC face unlimited corporate liability under corporate law, and directors may incur personal liability for failure to remit federal GST and BC PST under applicable statutory provisions. Note: Canadian law imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds; the 180-day figure is included as a prudent internal trade and audit-comparison benchmark aligning with maximum commercial credit terms and CRA's reasonableness review of export payment timing.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility under IMF Article VIII. The Canadian dollar floats freely; current-account transactions (trade invoices, royalties, dividends, interest) and capital-account transactions (equity injections, intercompany loans, share redemptions, portfolio flows) are unrestricted. A BC ULC may remit profits to non-resident shareholders without regulatory approval or FX license. The only remittance frictions are fiscal, not FX: CRA Part XIII non-resident withholding on distributions (25% statutory, treaty-reduced) and the alignment of the ULC's Canadian corporate tax posture with the US check-the-box pass-through election available to a ULC's US shareholders.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender rule. Canada imposes no mandatory conversion ratio, no repatriation deadline, and no exchange permit for export proceeds. The ULC may hold USD, EUR, or CNH sales proceeds offshore indefinitely. Information duties only: FINTRAC cross-border currency report (Form E677) if physical cash exceeding CAD 10,000 enters or leaves Canada; bank-filed FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reports for international wires of CAD 10,000 or more; and CRA T1134/T1135 informational returns for foreign bank balances or foreign affiliates above statutory thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada operates a decentralized FX and payments governance framework: the Bank of Canada holds monetary authority and administers retail-payment-service licensing under the Retail Payment Activities Act (2021); OSFI supervises prudential conduct of banks; FINTRAC enforces the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; the Minister of Finance retains residual authority under the Currency Act. There is no dedicated foreign-exchange control agency.
