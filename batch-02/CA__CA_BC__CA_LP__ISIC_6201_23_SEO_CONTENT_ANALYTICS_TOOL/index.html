Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online portals; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs and export reporting; British Columbia Ministry of Finance for provincial sales tax administration.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian limited partnership generally obtains a Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or the CRA non-resident registration process. The partnership must provide its legal name, formation jurisdiction, partnership agreement or certificate of registration, principal business address, responsible person, partners, fiscal period, business activity, and authorized representative information. The CRA assigns a nine-digit BN and, where applicable, program accounts such as RT for GST/HST, RC for corporate income tax, RP for payroll, and RM for import/export. A partnership that carries on business in Canada, makes taxable supplies in Canada, imports commercial goods, or conducts other taxable activities must assess registration obligations separately. A non-resident supplier may register for GST/HST through the ordinary regime or, for qualifying digital-economy supplies, the simplified GST/HST registration regime. GST/HST registration is generally required when taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, subject to special rules for non-resident digital suppliers and other prescribed activities. GST/HST registration applications may require a security deposit from certain non-residents. The partnership should separately register with the British Columbia Ministry of Finance for PST if it sells or leases taxable software or other taxable goods, or provides taxable services, in British Columbia. A Canadian BN or GST/HST account is not a VAT number because Canada does not operate a European-style VAT identification system.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes: the partnership generally calculates income, losses, and relevant tax attributes, and allocates them to partners under the partnership agreement and the Income Tax Act. The partnership generally files a T5013 partnership information return when statutory filing conditions are met, but it is not ordinarily subject to income tax at the partnership level. Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for a Canadian limited partnership. Classification depends on Canadian legal form, partnership law, the Income Tax Act, applicable treaties, and the facts of the arrangement. Partners remain responsible for their own Canadian filing, withholding, permanent-establishment, and treaty analyses. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules may deny deductions or impose other consequences for hybrid arrangements involving foreign jurisdictions; foreign entity classification elections do not automatically control Canadian treatment. A limited partnership may also be subject to entity-level obligations for specific taxes, source deductions, GST/HST, payroll, information reporting, and withholding regimes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies in the applicable calculation, in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters for a person that is not a small supplier. Certain non-resident digital-economy suppliers use a CAD 30,000 threshold under the simplified GST/HST regime, generally measured by qualifying supplies to specified Canadian consumers. BC PST: no single general GST-style threshold applies to every taxable software or taxable-supply business. Registration and collection obligations depend on the taxable product or service, the seller's business presence and solicitation activities, and specific small-seller or marketplace rules. Software supplied for use in British Columbia is commonly taxable, including electronically supplied software, subject to statutory exemptions and sourcing rules. The partnership must test GST/HST and BC PST independently.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST/HST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or shipment to a destination outside Canada and retention of evidence establishing export. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated only where the specific Excise Tax Act rules are met. A service is not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer is foreign or pays from a foreign bank account. Services performed in Canada, services in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada, personal services physically rendered in Canada, and certain advisory, marketing, data, or electronically supplied services may be subject to specific exclusions, use-and-enjoyment rules, or Canadian taxability. SaaS and keyword-analytics supplies require analysis of the contractual supply, customer status and address, place of use, user location, service performance, and whether the supply is a taxable digital service or an excluded service. Zero-rated supplies carry a 0% GST/HST rate but may generally support input tax credits under the ordinary GST/HST regime. BC PST does not generally apply to goods or software exported for use outside British Columbia, but the supplier must substantiate destination and non-use in British Columbia. Export treatment should be applied only after verifying the applicable statutory provision and retaining customer, contract, usage, delivery, and payment evidence.

  • CBSA export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where an export declaration is legally required
  • Commercial invoice identifying exporter, purchaser, description, quantity, value, currency, destination, and terms of sale
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or other carrier evidence for exported goods
  • Customs broker records, import entry, delivery confirmation, or destination-country customs evidence where available
  • Contract, purchase order, customer tax-residency evidence, and records showing that the recipient is non-resident where relevant to a service zero-rating provision
  • For SaaS and offshore services: customer location and billing records, contractual scope, IP or user-location evidence where relevant, service-delivery records, payment records, and evidence that the supply was not used or enjoyed in Canada where the statutory rule requires that analysis
  • Bank payment records or other commercial evidence linking consideration to the exported supply; an Inward Remittance Certificate is not a generally mandatory Canadian document and Canada does not impose a general requirement for foreign-export proceeds to be repatriated through a designated bank

Canada and British Columbia do not generally require real-time electronic invoice clearance, government-issued fiscal invoice numbers, or digital stamping for ordinary domestic GST/HST or BC PST invoices. Registered suppliers must issue compliant invoices or receipts showing required supplier information, applicable tax, consideration, invoice date, registration number where required, and sufficient detail for customer input-tax-credit support. GST/HST registrants generally file and remit through CRA systems; electronic filing may be mandatory for specified registrants and filing categories. BC PST returns and payments are administered through the provincial online system. Commercial export transactions may require electronic export reporting through the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), depending on the goods, destination, value, and applicable exemptions. Payment processors such as Stripe or Paddle do not replace the supplier's tax-registration, invoicing, collection, or record-retention obligations. The simplified GST/HST regime generally restricts input tax credits and requires separate compliance from the ordinary GST/HST regime.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange control regime requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within a prescribed number of days. Incorrectly applying zero-rating can result in assessment of uncollected GST/HST or BC PST, arrears interest, reassessment of input tax credits, denial or recovery of refunds, and liability for tax that should have been collected from customers. The CRA may impose penalties for false statements, omissions, failure to file, failure to remit, failure to register, or gross negligence, depending on the conduct and statutory provision. Deliberate evasion or fraud can lead to civil penalties, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under applicable tax legislation. A registrant must retain books and records, invoices, contracts, customs evidence, customer-location evidence, and payment records for the applicable statutory retention period, generally at least six years from the end of the relevant year unless a longer period or unresolved objection, appeal, or audit applies. Refunds based on unsupported export claims may be reassessed and recovered with interest. The partnership should maintain transaction-level evidence distinguishing exported goods, zero-rated services, taxable Canadian supplies, and BC PST-taxable software or services.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current- and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII and imposes no exchange controls, prior-authorization permits, or repatriation licensing. Profit remittances by a British Columbia limited partnership to non-resident limited partners are FX-unrestricted and may be executed without central bank filing. The only structural friction is taxation: Part XIII withholding of 15-25% (treaty-reduced) applies to distributions characterized as income, management fees, or deemed interest, whereas returns of capital are not subject to Part XIII. For CRA filing purposes, foreign-currency receipts and payments must be translated into Canadian dollars at the applicable Bank of Canada exchange rate, and Canadian-resident partners holding attributed foreign partnership property above CAD 100,000 must file Form T1135. No mandatory conversion into CAD arises at any point.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed repatriation deadline for export proceeds. USD/EUR SaaS subscription receipts settled through Stripe, Paddle, or direct corporate wire may be retained in foreign currency accounts indefinitely. Reporting duties: (i) GST/HST zero-rating applies to exported software and analytics services, with the foreign-currency value converted at the CRA annual average rate on the GST/HST return; (ii) the LP, if it elects a functional currency other than CAD under subsection 261(2) of the Income Tax Act, must compute taxable income consistently in that elected currency; (iii) any Canadian-resident limited partner whose attributed foreign income or foreign bank balances exceed the CAD 100,000 threshold must file Form T1135. The Bank of Canada does not maintain an export-proceeds settlement or foreign-exchange surrender window.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential regulation of Schedule I banks and FINTRAC for AML/CFT enforcement; the Department of Finance Canada retains statutory authority over exchange control policy.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH as a collection and settlement auxiliary, LOW as primary treasury. A BC-registered LP may lawfully maintain accounts with cross-border fintech EMIs (Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex, Paddle as merchant of record) provided the provider is registered as a Money Services Business with FINTRAC or operates through a FINTRAC-registered Canadian MSB partner. Wise Business is FINTRAC-registered; Payoneer operates via CFSI; Airwallex holds Canadian MSB registration. Legal constraints: (i) foreign EMIs are not CDIC-insured and cannot provide Canadian lending, overdraft facilities, or commercial reference letters; (ii) an EMI without Canadian MSB status creates enhanced-due-diligence friction when receiving inbound SWIFT transfers at a Schedule I bank; (iii) mass-payout rails from EMIs must always terminate in a named Schedule I bank account and funds should be swept weekly under a documented treasury policy to avoid concentration risk. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident capital contributions must be sent by SWIFT MT103 with a clean, sanctions-compliant narrative in the format: 'LP Capital Subscription / [Partner Legal Name] / Contribution per LP Agreement dated [date]'. Canada does not maintain a statutory purpose-code lexicon, so the narrative must be explicit yet free of ambiguous or high-risk keywords to avoid OFAC, UN Act, and US correspondent intermediary false positives. Supporting documents retained on the bank file: the capital call notice, the subscription schedule, and the LP Agreement Schedule A. Enhanced due diligence applies to individual transactions above CAD 100,000; any planned multi-tranche funding should be documented in a drawdown schedule to avoid structuring flags. Final funds may route to a segregated Capital Escrow/Subscription Trust account or directly to the operating account under board resolution. Settlement rails: (i) SWIFT MT103 direct beneficiary credit for cross-border vendor payments; (ii) Canada Lynx RTGS for domestic CAD disbursements above CAD 50,000; (iii) US ACH through the Schedule I bank's United States correspondent for USD-denominated operations; (iv) Stripe Treasury or Paddle Payouts configured to sweep settlement balances back to the LP's Schedule I treasury deposit account in the target currency. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-HIGH as a collection and settlement auxiliary, LOW as primary treasury. A BC-registered LP may lawfully maintain accounts with cross-border fintech EMIs (Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex, Paddle as merchant of record) provided the provider is registered as a Money Services Business with FINTRAC or operates through a FINTRAC-registered Canadian MSB partner. Wise Business is FINTRAC-registered; Payoneer operates via CFSI; Airwallex holds Canadian MSB registration. Legal constraints: (i) foreign EMIs are not CDIC-insured and cannot provide Canadian lending, overdraft facilities, or commercial reference letters; (ii) an EMI without Canadian MSB status creates enhanced-due-diligence friction when receiving inbound SWIFT transfers at a Schedule I bank; (iii) mass-payout rails from EMIs must always terminate in a named Schedule I bank account and funds should be swept weekly under a documented treasury policy to avoid concentration risk. Recommended topology: Stripe or Paddle as merchant of record for card acceptance; residual balances converted to CAD/USD and swept to RBC or TD on a 24-hour settlement cycle.

Turnover: No statutory audit is mandated for a British Columbia limited partnership solely because of its corporate form. Independent audit becomes compulsory only if (i) the partnership agreement or a unanimous partner resolution requires it, (ii) the LP is a 'reporting issuer' under the BC Securities Act, (iii) the LP holds a regulated financial license such as an investment dealer registration under IIROC, or (iv) a federal financial institution act applies.
