Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Revenu Québec (for Quebec-specific GST/QST and provincial income tax); federal portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html); provincial BC portal: BC Registries and Online Services (https://www.bcregistry.gov.bc.ca/)

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities establishing a British Columbia Limited Partnership must first register extra-provincially with BC Registries (if required) or file a declaration under the Partnership Act (RSBC 1996, c. 348). For federal GST/HST purposes, the non-resident partnership must apply for a Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account via CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), providing the partnership's governing documents, Canadian agent appointment (Form RC59 or equivalent), and proof of Canadian place of business if applicable. The BC LP itself is not a taxable entity for income tax but obtains its own BN for GST/HST and payroll where applicable. Non-resident partners should obtain individual or corporate Canadian tax identification numbers as required. Where the partnership carries on business in BC, the BC Corporate and Property Registry may require registration under the Partnership Act; after registration, CRA will issue a separate GST/HST account where the partnership's worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters. Registration credentials must be established through CRA's Represent a Client service or an authorized representative.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

The BC Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under section 96 of the Income Tax Act (Canada): each partner computes their share of income, losses, and tax credits as arising in the fiscal period, regardless of actual distribution. No check-the-box election analogous to the US exists; however, a limited partnership may elect under section 216 (non-resident rents and timber royalties), section 216.1 (non-resident maritime transportation), or use the s. 102(2) election for Canadian-controlled private corporation partnership income allocation among associated corporations. Hybrid mismatch rules in sections 12.6, 18.2, 18.3, 18.4, 20.2, and 95(2)(f), (g) and (j) of the Income Tax Act may deny deductions for payments characterized as interest or royalty where there is a deduction/no-inclusion mismatch, including for partnerships and hybrid entities, and require mandatory filing of Form RC4030 (reports on hybrid mismatch arrangements). Limited partners may also make a designation under subsection 96(8) to reattribute a share of partnership income to a limited partner for Canadian tax purposes if the partnership is not resident in Canada and the member is resident in Canada.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (worldwide taxable supplies of property and services in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, otherwise known as the small supplier threshold; provincial PST registration is required based on separate BC provincial criteria, generally immediately if you sell taxable goods or services in BC). A non-resident partnership that is not carrying on business in Canada but making supplies of intangible property in Canada may also be required to register for GST/HST under the 'supplies of intangibles in Canada' rules where the threshold is met; the CRA generally applies the same CAD 30,000 threshold for non-residents regarding supplies deemed made in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under section 23 of the Excise Tax Act, a supply of a service made by a Canadian resident registrant is zero-rated if the service is provided to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and is not resident in Canada, and the service is not performed while the non-resident is in Canada. For international student placement and immigration/visa advisory services supplied to non-resident individuals (foreign students or principal applicants outside Canada), the services qualify as 'exported services' zero-rated if the charge is made to the non-resident and the service is not in connection with real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada (except advisory services may be zero-rated if they fall within section 23 or 26). Immigration filing services do not automatically qualify for zero-rating where they relate to Canadian immigration applications and are performed for Canadian immigration purposes, but the CRA revenue ruling (GST/HST Memorandum 17.3) treats services provided to non-residents regarding Canadian immigration as zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident person and the service is not performed in Canada. Zero-rating is available only to GST/HST registrants and must be claimed at the time of invoicing; if the partnership charges foreign clients a payment via an escrow account or wire transfer, the supply is zero-rated provided the non-resident is not in Canada at the time of performance and the non-resident does not receive the benefit in Canada. Services to foreign students residing in Canada where the student is a non-resident but physically in Canada during the service period generally remain taxable at 5% GST. Offences: zero-rating requires a direct link between the consideration and the supply of 'prescribed services' as defined in SOR/91-33, and a supply of advisory services may be zero-rated if the service is not qualifying the supply under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI. Care must be taken for services related to Canadian permanent residence or study permits obtained for the benefit of the foreign national; the CRA will consider the place of performance of the service. In sum: 0% rates apply to services provided to non-residents who are outside Canada, not registered under GST/HST, and where the service is not performed in Canada.

Canada has no mandatory national real-time electronic invoice clearance platform; however, CRA requires electronic record keeping and a valid GST/HST registration number on invoices. For BC PST, invoices issued by a BC LP must show the PST registration number. The Excise Tax Act requires every registrant to issue a Simplified or Full GST/HST invoice for taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30 (simplified) or CAD 150 (full) containing prescribed particulars. The CRA promotes the Pan-Canadian e-Invoicing framework based on the Peppol standard, but participation remains voluntary. Quebec (not BC) mandates the Revenu Québec E-invoice regime for certain businesses. For BC PST, the BC Ministry of Finance administers the PST system; there is no digital stamping authority. If the LP exports services, it may need to issue invoices without GST/HST and support zero-rating with usual commercial documents (not subject to fiscal clearance).

If a GST/HST registrant claims zero-rating without proper substantiation, the CRA may reassess to charge 5% GST plus interest and penalties. Where a supply is later determined taxable because the non-resident was deemed resident in Canada or the service was performed in Canada, the supplier is liable for GST/HST remittance under section 296 of the Excise Tax Act, with penalties up to 20% under subsection 280(1) for failure to collect, plus daily late-filing penalties under subsection 280(1.1). Knowingly making false statements on a return or in export documentation carries criminal penalties under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act (including fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years). In addition, the CRA may waive input tax credits and impose a 25% gross negligence penalty under subsection 285(1) where the false statement was made knowingly or due to gross negligence. For funds repatriated more than 180 days after the date of export without reasonable explanation, the CRA may question whether the consideration was truly received from the non-resident, reassess the zero-rated supply for lack of proof of payment, and apply the normal assessment period extension of 4 years (i.e., reassessment beyond 4 years permitted in case of neglect or fraud). Provincial PST zero-rating does not apply; PST is imposed on the purchase price of taxable services and the BC Ministry of Finance may assess unremitted PST with a penalty of 50% of the tax for amounts not collected or evaded. The Export and Import Permits Act and the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act licensing violations may also apply where immigration services are conducted without proper RCIC (Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultant) licensing or where the partnership performs prohibited administrative work; the CRA will refer such matters to the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants (CICC) for disciplinary and penal enforcement.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account, and Canada observes IMF Article VIII obligations. There are no exchange-control approvals for inbound non-resident limited-partner capital contributions, outbound trade disbursements, or repatriation of partnership profits. Profit remittances by a British Columbia limited partnership to non-resident partners are subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, which the general partner must report and remit, with potential treaty-rate relief under an applicable tax treaty. There is no foreign-exchange conversion certificate or import/export licensing requirement for immigration or study-abroad service fees. The principal hurdles are tax compliance, FINTRAC international electronic-fund-transfer reporting, and financial-institution enhanced due diligence, not currency availability.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender rule and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. An exporter or service provider may invoice in CAD or foreign currency, retain proceeds in a foreign-currency account, and repatriate at market rates without Bank of Canada permission. For the subject study-abroad/immigration advisory business, export earnings in the form of client fees are freely retainable. All international electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more, whether inbound or outbound, trigger a FINTRAC report, and the banks will require a stated purpose of transfer.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money-laundering/countering-financing-of-terrorism administration by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign exchange administration; cross-border regulatory functions are distributed among these bodies and the Canada Border Services Agency for related compliance.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low for escrow or trust accounts; Medium for ancillary cross-border payment facilitation. Offshore digital EMI platforms are not banks under the Canadian Bank Act and may not accept deposits or hold client trust funds as a primary account. To provide payment services in Canada, an EMI must be registered as a money services business under FINTRAC and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act, including the maintenance of funds-safeguarding measures. The BC limited partnership should maintain its operational and escrow banking with a CDIC-insured Canadian bank, and may use EMI rails only for non-trust, low-value FX conversion or fee disbursement, provided the EMI entity is FINTRAC-registered and the underlying service is contractually documented.

There is no Canada-mandated statutory purpose code; the receiving bank will normally assign an internal balance-of-payments classification (e.g., inward direct investment or capital-account transfer) and may request completing an investor-funding statement. For funds exceeding C$10,000, FINTRAC international electronic-funds-transfer reporting is mandatory. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (combined federal 15% + British Columbia provincial 12% general corporate rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident person; where the partnership holds shares, dividends flow through to partners and are deemed paid to them).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 (annual aggregate value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions, excluding low-risk loans, above which contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is expected; country-by-country reporting threshold is C$1.16 billion for multinational groups).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); however, the Income Tax Act contains the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245) and broader anti-treaty-shopping rules (s. 94) that deny tax benefits where a transaction or entity lacks genuine economic substance. For immigration and visa programmes, IRCC and other federal authorities require the business to demonstrate real physical operations, active management, employment, and financial viability in Canada; a shell arrangement risks both tax reassessment and immigration inadmissibility.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, deliberate tax evasion is punishable by fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years (summary) or 5 years (indictable). The LP may also face civil penalties under s. 162 and s. 163.2, plus gross negligence penalties up to 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and the general partner may be jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 for unremitted withholdings, and if the LP is used as a sham, courts may pierce the veil, exposing individuals to civil restitution, travel bans, and criminal imprisonment.
Shell Company or Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, misrepresentation to immigration authorities carries a five-year inadmissibility ban and probable removal. Tax authorities may invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule to recharacterize the transaction, disallow losses, and assess substantial penalties. The British Columbia Registrar may strike the LP for filing false affidavits.

Director personal liability: Directors and immigration consultants may be investigated for counselling misrepresentation or fraud, resulting in professional licence revocation, criminal prosecution, travel bans, and imprisonment. Investors relying on such schemes may lose their immigration status and face removal.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA and FINTRAC administrative regime, non-compliance can trigger monetary penalties of up to C$500,000 for corporations and C$100,000 for individuals per violation. Criminal money-laundering offences carry imprisonment up to 10 years; professional citations before the CICC may result in revocation of immigration consultant licence.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may be held personally liable for failure to implement an effective AML compliance program. Criminal liability for laundering proceeds of crime can lead to extradition, travel bans, and extensive terms of imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s. 247, the CRA may reassess the taxpayer to the arm's length price and levy a specific penalty of 10% of the net upward adjustment where the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts to establish an arm's length price, plus gross negligence penalties. Interest is charged on all reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for the corporation's unremitted withholdings and source deductions under s. 227.1. Where transfer pricing adjustments are made with a related party that is not a Canadian controlled private corporation, the net upward adjustment may be treated as a dividend, leading to further director liability and personal exposure in cases of deliberate avoidance.
