Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada) for federal GST/HST, corporate income tax, and business number registration; BC Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC portal) for British Columbia PST, employer health tax, and other provincial tax accounts.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned British Columbia Unlimited Liability Company (ULC) must first obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or applying online through the CRA 'My Business Account' portal. The ULC must then open required program accounts: GST/HST account (RT), corporate income tax account (RC), payroll account (RP) if employees are in Canada, and import/export account (RM) if it will import or export commercial goods. Non-resident entities without a Canadian address may apply by mailed submission and must provide the foreign certificate of incorporation, proof of corporate existence, details of Canadian permanent establishment, and identification of all directors and signing officers. A separate British Columbia PST account is obtained through eTaxBC for any retail taxable sales in BC, with the ULC's BC business number linked to the CRA BN. All registrations must be completed before making taxable supplies in Canada or employment payroll remittances.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under Canadian domestic tax law, a BC ULC is an ordinary corporation taxable at the entity level; there is no Canadian federal or provincial 'check-the-box' election. However, the ULC may be eligible for foreign-entity classification elections, particularly a U.S. federal income tax Form 8832 election to be treated as a disregarded entity or a partnership, unless the entity is per se incorporated for U.S. tax purposes. In addition, hybrid mismatch rules in the Income Tax Act (sections 94.1 to 94.4, 18.4 and 18.6) may recharacterize payment, deduction, or exemption outcomes for cross-border treaty claims. A GST/HST voluntary registration election can also be made under section 240(2.1) of the Excise Tax Act to bring a non-resident into the GST/HST system where commercial activity or export zero-rating recovery is needed.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (the small supplier threshold for GST/HST registration); non-resident businesses carrying on a commercial activity in Canada may be required to register even below this threshold, and a non-resident seller may voluntarily register to claim input tax credits. BC PST registration applies to sellers making taxable sales in BC, subject to separate provincial rules for non-resident sellers and small-seller exemptions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods, including a newly built deep-sea ocean container vessel, may be zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier exports the goods as part of the supply or the goods are supplied to a non-resident who is not registered under the GST/HST and exports the goods from Canada without use or consumption in Canada. Certain services rendered to a non-resident, such as engineering, design, and consulting services that do not relate to Canadian real property or tangible personal property located in Canada at the time the service is performed, may also be zero-rated. The shipyard must hold contemporaneous documentary evidence demonstrating the physical export of the vessel and receipt of payment from the non-resident buyer in order to claim the 0% rate and to support any related input tax credit refund claim.

  • Canada Customs Commercial Export Declaration (CSD/CCED) or automated export reporting to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA)
  • Original or certified Bill of Lading (straight, order, or through bill) showing shipment to a foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or other internationally acceptable proof of payment from the non-resident buyer
  • Export contract, invoice, and Incoterms proof indicating transfer of ownership to the non-resident purchaser
  • Customs proof of export, vessel registration transfer, or delivery certificate evidencing the vessel left Canadian territorial waters
  • Cargo control documents or freight forwarder's certificate where third-party export movement is used

Canada does not currently mandate a real-time fiscal clearance, digital tax stamping, or government pre-clearance API for GST/HST invoices. CRA requires every GST/HST invoice to contain prescribed information: supplier name and GST/HST registration number, invoice date, description of the taxable supply, total amount and tax payable, and recipient name or business name for taxable supplies over CAD 150. Electronic invoicing via EDI, PDF, or other machine-readable formats is permitted. For British Columbia PST, the seller must collect and report PST on taxable retail sales, disclose PST separately where required, and submit returns through eTaxBC; no BC-specific digital stamping or real-time system exists.

The CRA routinely cross-checks GST/HST returns, domestic invoices, customs export declarations, and foreign exchange receipts to verify that zero-rating was properly claimed. If documentary evidence is incomplete or the vessel is not exported within the required period, the CRA will disallow the zero rate, reassess GST/HST at 5%, claw back any previously paid refunds and input tax credits, and charge statutory interest. False statements or fraudulent claims may attract a penalty under section 281.1 of the Excise Tax Act of not less than CAD 250 and not more than 25% of the tax sought to be evaded or the amount falsely claimed, plus additional penalties under section 280 for knowingly failing to collect or remit tax. Criminal tax evasion under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act is punishable with substantial fines and imprisonment. A BC ULC's unlimited shareholder liability may further expose its shareholders to unpaid GST, interest, penalties, and related BC tax liabilities.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible, free-floating currency regime under the Bank of Canada Act. Both current and capital account transactions are unrestricted, and there are no requirements for prior central bank approval on cross-border transfers, foreign-currency borrowing, or dividend remittances. Profit repatriation is subject only to Canadian tax laws, including withholding taxes on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders, which may be reduced under applicable tax treaties. No material legal hurdles exist for converting CAD to foreign currency or for retaining proceeds offshore; however, all transactions are subject to FINTRAC reporting standards and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose statutory export proceeds surrender requirements, mandatory conversion ratios, or forced repatriation timelines. Exporters may retain receipts in any currency and may choose the timing and method of conversion. Nevertheless, all receipts entering the Canadian banking system are subject to suspicious transaction reporting and any single wire or series of wires exceeding CAD 10,000 triggers FINTRAC reporting obligations (for MSBs and financial intermediaries). Businesses must also reflect export income in their Canadian tax filings and maintain underlying trade documentation for audit and AML verification purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with enforcement oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Rating: Medium. Offshore digital EMIs that do not hold a Canadian money services business (MSB) license cannot open local settlement accounts or provide direct clearing member services. Even when licensed, fintech EMIs generally facilitate remittances and small-to-mid value FX, but are less adapted for high-value maritime escrow facilities, commercial letters of credit, and shipbuilding installment structures. For this Class 3011 ocean container vessel shipyard, a chartered bank account remains mandatory; a fintech EMI may serve as a supplementary payment channel only if fully registered with FINTRAC and compliant with PCMLTFA record-keeping and reporting obligations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity capital from non-resident investors must be routed through a Canadian schedule I chartered bank using standard SWIFT MT103. The instruction should reference field 50K with the true ordering shareholder name, field 56A/57A for intermediary and beneficiary banks, and field 70 for remittance information, with a clear purpose code such as 'EQUITY CAPITAL INJECTION' or 'SHAREHOLDER LOAN FOR SHIPYARD OPERATIONS'; no formal purpose-code registry is mandated by OSFI, but banks will require the underlying corporate resolution and source-of-funds evidence. All funds exceeding CAD 10,000 are automatically reportable to FINTRAC. For escrow-based capital support, the injection may be parked in a dedicated interest-bearing capital escrow account held with the same bank, with release conditions tied to drawdown requests matched against supplier invoices or construction milestones.Outbound settlements for raw materials, ship equipment, and subcontractor services should ideally traverse SWIFT MT100 series (MT101/MT103) directly from the operational account, or, for larger stages subject to contractual guarantees, by back-to-back or commercial letters of credit via the same Canadian bank. The transaction must adopt Incoterms 2020, the full legal contract, and the relevant ISBP745/UCP600 checking rules. For maritime installments, the bank will require presentation of documents commonly consistent with LC conditions, including signed packing lists, bills of lading, certificates of quality/quantity, and pre-shipment inspection certificates. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.0% combined Canadian federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate as of 2026 (15% federal + 12% BC), applicable to active Canadian-source business income of a non-CCPC or a corporation exceeding the CCPC small-business deduction threshold.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner, unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty. For a BC ULC paying dividends to a foreign parent, the statutory rate applies at source; treaty reductions are not automatic and require documented treaty eligibility.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than 6 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end; e.g., a December 31 year-end is due June 30 of the following year. Instalments are due on the last day of each month for specified large corporations; smaller corporations may remit quarterly if annual federal and provincial tax payable is above CAD 3,000.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Filing and payment deadlines mirror the federal T2 deadline; provincial balance-due is due the last day of the taxation year for instalment-based remittances, with the remaining balance due 2 months after fiscal year-end for CCPCs and 3 months after year-end for non-CCPCs absent special exemption? In BC, the balance due for corporations other than CCPCs is 3 months after year-end; CRA administers payment timing uniformly with federal instalment rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No single absolute dollar threshold is set by CRA that automatically triggers a complete Local File or Master File; however, for purposes of contemporaneous documentation and penalty defence, taxpayers are administratively expected to maintain transactional documentation where individual cross-border non-arm's-length transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000, and a Country-by-Country Report is mandatory for a Canadian-origin group with consolidated revenue of CAD 750,000,000 or more. The BC ULC should also prepare a Local File and Master File when its aggregate controlled transactions with related parties exceed CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not implement the OECD ESR regime in a direct statutory form, but CRA, under the Income Tax Act GAAR (s.245) and case-law substance doctrines, may re-characterize transactions that are pure tax-title or artificial. A BC ULC, despite being an unlimited liability company, cannot function as a mere paper conduit: it must have a real managerial office, active shipyard or effective shipbuilding subcontract operations, employees or engaged manufacturing capacity, and a substantial physical presence in British Columbia. CRA may examine as a sham any arrangement where the ULC uses the treaty dividend rate while the true beneficial control rests in a foreign parent with no operational substance in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – False or Incomplete Returns

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.239(1): fine equal to 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for up to 5 years; under s.163(2): gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax, with interest compounding daily from the filing due date. CRA may also issue a formal demand and compel production of all shipyard cost records, bank statements, and shareholder communications.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in the misreporting are jointly and severally liable for the penalty under shareholder liability provisions if they direct the sham subcontractor scheme. Directors may face personal tax assessments, criminal prosecution with travel restrictions pending trial, a court order prohibiting departure from Canada under s.226, and civil restitution for induced breach of the corporation's duty. If the ULC is insolvent, CRA may attach personal guarantee payments from directors who signed the T2 knowingly false.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Base Erosion

Statutory sanction: CRA transfer-pricing adjustment to the arm's-length price, plus application of optional interest on the additional tax payable. A specific transfer-pricing penalty may apply under s.247(3) equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the misstatement is grossly negligent or a document failure occurs; in repeated cases, penalties escalate. Associated governments may assess cross-border adjustments under the mutual agreement procedure.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the transfer-pricing documentation knowing it is false risk personal penalties for aiding and abetting under s.239; CRA can invoke the 'knowing participation' doctrine to pierce the responsibility shield. Directors of the ULC may be personally assessed for unremitted Canadian withholding where the offshore related buyer is not a true arm's-length party, and are personally liable to return distributions made while the corporation was in a non-deductible debt-servicing context.
Shell Conduit and Economic-Substance Abuse (ULC as Treaty-Only Vehicle)

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment collapsing the ULC's separate tax personality, denial of treaty dividend relief, full recovery of Part XIII tax (25%) on distributed dividends, retroactive interest from each payment, and penalties for misrepresentation where the NR301 declaration omits substance facts. Ontario and other treaty-shopping precedents confirm that the ULC is ignored as a conduit when it performs no substantive profit-generating activities; the same doctrine applies to a BC ULC.

Director personal liability: In a ULC, shareholders and directors are uniquely exposed because of the unlimited liability clause: a creditor, including CRA, can directly enforce a tax debt against ULC shareholders for the corporation's obligations. Directors who knowingly allow the ULC to be used as a treaty shopper are personally liable for the foreign tax adjustment, can be prohibited from leaving Canada via court order, and, upon criminal treaty-abuse prosecution, may receive prison sentences and an order to pay restitution. The BC Registrar of Companies may also impose an administrative dissolution where the ULC fails to file an annual report or has no registered office activity.
Unremitted Withholding Obligations – PAYE and Part XIII

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.227(8) and s.227(9): an initial failure-to-remit penalty of 10% of the amounts not remitted and 20% for repeated failure, plus compound daily interest on the unremitted principal; the CRA may demand reassessment under s.164(4.1). Criminal convictions follow under s.227(13) for intentional non-remittance; a summary conviction fine is discretionary but can reach 200% of the deficiency.

Director personal liability: Directors are deemed trustees of withheld amount under s.227.1 and are jointly and severally liable for the full unremitted source deductions and Part XIII amounts, irrespective of whether the corporation fails; a director has limited due-diligence defence and must demonstrate they exercised reasonable care and diligence. CRA commonly obtains garnishment against director bank accounts, registration of personal liens, and travel prohibition through the issuance of a demand under s.226 where the director attempts to leave Canada with assets.
