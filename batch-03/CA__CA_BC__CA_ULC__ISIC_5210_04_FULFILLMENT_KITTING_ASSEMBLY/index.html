Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. For Canadian domestic tax purposes, a BC ULC is a taxable Canadian corporation and a resident corporation under the Income Tax Act; it files a Part I corporate return and pays tax on worldwide income at entity level. The 'unlimited liability' feature of the ULC does not confer Canadian pass-through treatment. The metadata label 'Pass_Through_Eligible' is relevant only to the US owner's eligibility to make an IRS check-the-box election under IRC 301.7701-3; the CRA does not respect that election for Canadian tax classification and continues to impose entity-level taxation on the ULC.

Available election forms

(a) No Canadian check-the-box election exists; Canadian anti-hybrid rules (implementing OECD BEPS Action 2) may disallow deductions where a US check-the-box election on the ULC creates a deduction/no-inclusion or double-deduction outcome. (b) GST/HST s. 273 election for closely related registrants: an election may deem supplies between closely related Canadian-registered corporations to be for nil consideration. (c) GST/HST drop-shipment election under ss. 178.8/179 and Form GST352: permits a BC fulfilment/3PL registrant to elect with a non-resident principal so that goods physically situated in Canada and shipped to a third party are not inadvertently subject to GST at a second level. (d) BC PST: no election to treat services as goods, but a PST registration allows wholesale purchases of packaging and kitting materials on an exempt basis where the buyer holds a valid BC PST number and the goods are not consumed in BC. (e) Income Tax Act ss. 216/217 elections are available only for specified non-resident rental/revenue or employment income streams and are not relevant to the trading, kitting, and assembly activities of a resident ULC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.05%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. A person carrying on business in Canada who makes more than CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in the immediately preceding four calendar quarters or in the current quarter is required to be registered for GST/HST. If the BC ULC exceeds this threshold, registration is mandatory and GST at 5% applies to taxable kitting/assembly services performed in BC. BC PST registration has no analogous minimum turnover threshold; the ULC must register for PST with the BC Ministry of Finance if it makes retail sales of taxable goods or designated services in BC, and must separately account for PST at 7% where applicable.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act where the goods are exported from Canada by the supplier or on behalf of the supplier and the supplier retains prescribed evidence of export, normally including proof that the goods were removed from Canada within 60 days of the supply and that no use or consumption of the goods occurred in Canada before export. A supply of a service to a non-resident person may be zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 only if the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada and not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed. Kitting and assembly services physically performed on goods located in a BC warehouse are services performed in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada and are therefore generally taxable at 5% GST rather than zero-rated. To preserve export zero-rating for the goods movement, the foreign seller (not the BC ULC) should retain title to the goods, contract directly with the overseas buyer, and engage the BC ULC as a warehousing/kitting agent; the export of the kitted goods may then be zero-rated by the owner of the goods, supported by the drop-shipment election in Form GST352 where applicable. Canada does not condition export zero-rating on foreign-exchange repatriation, but the CRA will expect documentary evidence that the transaction was genuinely an export to a non-resident person.

  • Canada Customs Coding Form B13A (Export Declaration) or an equivalent transmission through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) where the export value exceeds CAD 2,000
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the kitted SKUs, quantities, unit values, and HS/SS (Schedule of Numerical) codes
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill showing the Canadian port of exit, foreign destination, and non-resident consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or an equivalent payment-rail settlement record (ACH_Direct/Airwallex receipt showing funds from the non-resident buyer) evidencing the transaction value
  • Carrier or freight-forwarder certificate of export confirming the goods left Canada within the prescribed period
  • CRA-prescribed proof-of-export records under Excise Tax Act s. 3, including customs stamps, border service release documentation, or US/foreign customs import confirmation where applicable
  • Supply contracts and purchase orders showing that ownership passed to a non-resident and that the export was not a round-trip or temporary export

Canada does not operate a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government-authorised API invoice validation regime at the federal or BC provincial level. GST/HST registrants are required to file returns electronically through CRA GST/HST NETFILE or 'My Business Account', and to retain invoices, receipts, and tax records in a readable English or French electronic format for CRA audit. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Initiative (PCEI) uses the PEPPOL interoperability framework on a voluntary basis; CRA does not require pre-clearance of invoices. The BC PST system requires quarterly or annual self-assessed returns filed via eTaxBC; invoice data APIs may be used internally for reconciliation with payment rails such as ACH_Direct or Airwallex but are not submitted to a government clearance gateway.

CRA applies heightened audit scrutiny to export zero-rating claims, particularly in fulfilment, 3PL, and kitting environments where the physical service is performed in Canada. A registrant claiming 0% GST must substantiate the export within the normal four-year reassessment window; where misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud is established, the CRA may reassess retroactively for up to ten years and claw back the zero-rated treatment. Civil penalties under ETA s. 285 apply to false statements or gross negligence: the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax sought to be evaded, with 50% for repeated false statements, plus arrears interest from the date the tax was otherwise due. Wilful evasion may be prosecuted under ETA s. 327, exposing the ULC and its officers/directors to fines up to 200% of the amount evaded and terms of imprisonment; CRA may also apply the director liability provisions of ETA s. 323. BC PST audits may similarly assess unpaid PST at 7%, interest, and penalties under the BC Consumer Taxation Act; the unlimited personal liability of ULC shareholders does not shield them from statutory penalties for fraudulent PST or GST schemes.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates under a fully floating exchange-rate regime with unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility. There are no exchange-control visas, licensing requirements, or advance approvals for inward foreign direct investment into a British Columbia unlimited liability corporation (ULC). Dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, and intercompany loan repayments may be freely repatriated to non-resident shareholders. The only material hurdle is withholding tax: a 25% Part XIII gross withholding tax generally applies to dividends and certain passive payments remitted to non-residents, with treaty rates often reduced (for example to 5%/15% under the Canada-US treaty) provided Form NR301 or equivalent certificate is submitted. Any profit-distribution payment must be processed with full source-country tax documentation and the relevant bank memo field must identify the income character to avoid misclassification by correspondent banks.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement exists. Exporters and trading companies in Canada are free to receive export proceeds in CAD or any foreign currency, hold funds offshore or onshore, and are not subject to any minimum or mandatory conversion ratio. There are no Bank of Canada directives requiring repatriation of export earnings within a specified timeline. Compliance obligations remain limited to general record-keeping, FINTRAC reporting of large cross-border wire transfers (CAD 10,000 or more), and proving the source of funds to the receiving bank upon request.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with the Department of Finance and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) exercising related oversight for foreign exchange and anti-money-laundering supervision.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

High as a secondary clearing/FX utility but Low as a primary banking relationship. Offshore digital EMI platforms such as Airwallex, Wise Business, and Currencycloud can support cross-border vendor payments, mass disbursements, and contract-cadence FX conversion, particularly for kitting/assembly suppliers located in the United States and Asia-Pacific. Legal constraints are material: a foreign EMI that is not a Bank Act chartered institution cannot take Canadian retail deposits, cannot offer deposit insurance, and, if undertaking money-transfer services in Canada, must be registered as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC with a Canadian agent. For treasury functions, the ULC should maintain one top-tier Canadian bank operating account and use an EMI for short-term payment-rail efficiency rather than as the bank of record.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal corporate income tax is generally 15%. British Columbia general corporate income tax is generally 12%, producing a combined general rate of 27%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may obtain a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, subject to federal and British Columbia eligibility rules. A ULC does not automatically qualify as a CCPC or as a pass-through entity for Canadian tax purposes.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. An applicable tax treaty commonly reduces the rate to 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the treaty ownership and other conditions. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% otherwise, subject to limitation-on-benefits and other treaty requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due no later than six months after the end of each taxation year. Any remaining balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying CCPC may generally pay its balance three months after year-end if the statutory conditions are met. Tax instalments are due monthly or, where permitted, quarterly. Information returns have separate deadlines; for example, Form T106 is generally due by the corporate income tax return filing deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary for a non-arm's-length cross-border transaction. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents to be priced on arm's-length terms and requires reasonable documentation to be prepared by the filing deadline. Form T106 generally applies when reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate or otherwise fall within the statutory reporting rules. Canada does not generally impose a separate OECD-style Master File filing requirement, although relevant group and transaction documentation should be retained.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone regime formally designated as Economic Substance Regulations comparable to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership requirements, residence and central-management analyses, interest deductibility rules, and the characterization of services, royalties, financing, and agency arrangements. The ULC should maintain Canadian employees, premises, operational authority, books, contracts, inventory controls, customer and supplier records, bank activity, and board-level decision evidence proportionate to its claimed functions and risks. A warehouse or fulfillment entity that performs only routine services should not claim residual entrepreneurial returns without personnel, assets, and decision-making supporting those returns.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed benefit, and possible prosecution. Tax evasion can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction or up to five years on indictment.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil liability for unremitted source deductions and certain other trust amounts under the Income Tax Act, subject to a due-diligence defense and statutory limitation periods. Criminal liability may attach to directors or officers who participated in the offence. A corporate veil is not routinely pierced for ordinary corporate tax debts, but fraud, knowing participation, sham transactions, or statutory director-liability provisions can create personal exposure. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty, although criminal proceedings, enforcement orders, or immigration consequences may create practical travel risks.
Shell company, sham residence, and beneficial-ownership abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, reassessment of tax, 25% Part XIII withholding where reduced rates were improperly applied, interest, penalties, application of the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership and information-reporting enforcement, and potential prosecution for fraud or tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in false residence certificates, sham arrangements, or concealment may face criminal charges, fines, imprisonment, director-liability claims, and reputational or banking consequences. Corporate separateness does not protect individuals from their own fraudulent conduct.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Income adjustment under section 247, denial or reduction of deductions, transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where statutory conditions are met, interest, reassessment, and potential concurrent withholding-tax exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a bona fide transfer-pricing adjustment merely because the company loses a dispute. Personal exposure can arise from deliberate falsification, obstruction, fraudulent records, or failure to remit statutory trust amounts.
Unremitted payroll, GST/HST, and Part XIII withholding

Statutory sanction: Trust-account assessment, interest, late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence or failure-to-remit penalties, denial of credits, collection action, and prosecution for serious conduct. GST/HST and payroll trust amounts generally receive priority in enforcement.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under statutory director-liability provisions for unremitted payroll deductions, GST/HST, and certain other trust amounts if statutory conditions are met. A due-diligence defense may be available where the director exercised the required care, diligence, and skill.
Customs, inventory, and import-value misstatement

Statutory sanction: Customs reassessments, duties, GST at importation, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, forfeiture, and prosecution for intentional false declarations. Related-party transfer-pricing adjustments may arise independently of customs enforcement.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic for ordinary customs reassessments, but directors, officers, customs brokers, and responsible employees may face penalties or prosecution for knowing false declarations, obstruction, or participation in fraudulent import arrangements.
Anti-money laundering and sanctions compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, public notices, recordkeeping and reporting enforcement, seizure or forfeiture under applicable law, and criminal penalties for money laundering or sanctions violations. Penalty amounts depend on the statute, violation classification, compliance history, and whether the offender is an individual or entity.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face individual administrative or criminal exposure where they authorized, directed, acquiesced in, or personally committed the conduct. Money laundering and sanctions offences can result in substantial fines, asset forfeiture, and imprisonment.
Corporate records and transparency-register failure

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, compliance orders, regulatory investigation, loss of good standing, banking de-risking, and potential criminal or tax consequences where the inaccurate information facilitates fraud, evasion, or money laundering.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal penalties or criminal exposure for knowingly false filings or concealment. Ordinary corporate-law non-compliance does not automatically create personal liability for all corporate obligations, but fraudulent conduct and statutory offences remain personal.
