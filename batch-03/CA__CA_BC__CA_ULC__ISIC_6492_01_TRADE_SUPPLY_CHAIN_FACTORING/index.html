Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html)

        
E-government filing portal
A foreign-owned BC ULC obtains its federal Business Number (BN) and GST/HST program account (RT) by filing Form RC1 with the CRA or registering online through Business Registration Online (BRO). Non-resident ULCs that make or intend to make taxable supplies in Canada must register; the CRA will issue a 9-digit BN followed by a two-letter program identifier. If required, the entity also opens CRA program accounts for corporate income tax (RC), payroll (RP), and import/export (RM). Provincial registry and PST accounts are coordinated separately through the British Columbia Corporate Registry and OneStop BC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation

        
Available election forms
Under Canadian domestic tax law, a BC ULC is automatically classified as a corporation and subject to entity-level taxation; no domestic check-the-box election exists. For U.S. federal tax purposes, eligible members may file IRS Form 8832 to elect classification of the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership, but that election is not recognized by the CRA as converting the entity into a flow-through for Canadian tax. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ITA s. 18.2 and s. 18.3) require alignment where an entity or instrument is treated differently by Canada and another jurisdiction.

Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (including zero-rated supplies but excluding exempt supplies; voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold)

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Goods qualify for 0% GST/HST if exported by the supplier within a statutory or Minister-approved period, supported by documentary proof of export. Financial services, including accounts-receivable factoring and trade-discount services provided by a Canadian registrant to a non-resident, may be zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part IX of the Excise Tax Act if the supply is made to a non-resident and is not for consumption, use, or enjoyment in Canada; the supplier must retain evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and the foreign character of the underlying receivable. Factoring solely of Canadian domestic receivables remains exempt rather than zero-rated.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

      
Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

    

    

      
Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
Canada operates a fully liberalized, freely floating CAD regime with no exchange controls on current or capital account transactions (IMF Article VIII status). There is no central-bank approval, quota, or repatriation surcharge applicable to a BC ULC. Key legal hurdles affecting a ULC: (i) subsections 137.1–137.2 of the Income Tax Act treat the ULC as a Canadian corporation for domestic tax purposes but deny its shareholders certain flow-through and rollover protections; (ii) non-resident shareholders of a Canadian ULC are denied dividend treaty benefits under Article IV(6)(b) of the Canada–US Income Tax Convention, causing Part XIII withholding at the statutory 25% rather than the usual 5%/15% treaty rates unless structural mitigation (e.g., a treaty-eligible Canadian holding corporation) is introduced; (iii) profit remittance by dividend, interest, or management fee is otherwise unrestricted; (iv) spot and forward foreign-exchange transactions may be executed with any Schedule I bank without a non-bank FX licence.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters may hold export proceeds indefinitely in CAD or any foreign currency within Canada or abroad. The only obligations are: (i) statistical and economic reporting through Statistics Canada (export declarations and balance-of-payments surveys); (ii) FINTRAC reporting of international electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more; and (iii) CBSA trade-compliance documentation under the Customs Act. There is no requirement to convert hard-currency receipts into CAD; however, a factoring ULC may voluntarily hedge to eliminate CAD/USD mismatch and must recognize FX gains or losses under ITA section 39(1).

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — the sole central monetary authority; exchange-control policy is administered by the Department of Finance Canada under the Currency Act, the Bank Act, and the Public Bank Supervision framework. OSFI supervises Schedule I/Schedule II banks for prudential conduct; FINTRAC administers the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); Export Development Canada (EDC) provides official export-credit and trade-insurance support for cross-border factoring portfolios.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 filing threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value for purchase/sale of goods and CAD 50,000 for services, interest, royalties, rents or other payments with non-arm's-length non-residents. Mandatory Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) for an MNE group with consolidated revenue equal to or exceeding CAD 750 million. Contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation must be finalized no later than the T2 filing deadline; however, CRA may request historical documentation at any time, and the documentation must demonstrate the arm's length price.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada does not use the Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR), but enforces substance through the GAAR (ITA s.245), sham doctrine, and the Limitation on Benefits provision of the Canada–US Treaty (Article XXIX-A). Since April 2022, hybrid mismatch rules (ss.17.1–17.3 and 18.4–18.6) directly combat double deductions arising from a US check-the-box election on a BC ULC. The CRA requires that the ULC maintain genuine commercial presence: physical premises in BC, resident directors/managers, independent decision-making, capital at risk, and substantive factoring operations (diligence, collection, funding). Treaty relief may be denied where the ULC is a conduit with no real control over the Canadian receivables portfolio.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion (Criminal)
        
Statutory sanction: Prosecution under ITA s.239(1): on indictment, a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment up to 5 years; on summary conviction, fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years. CRA will also assess the civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) of 50% of the understated tax.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in evasion face joint and several liability for corporate tax debts, personal restitution orders, criminal forfeiture, travel bans and passport seizure as bail or sentencing conditions, and potential extradition pursuant to mutual legal assistance treaties.

      

      
      

        Shell Company / Sham / Treaty Shopping
        
Statutory sanction: CRA recharacterization under GAAR (ITA s.245) and the sham doctrine; denial of treaty reductions; reassessment of the full 25% Part XIII withholding plus interest and penalties; loss of BC corporate rate benefits; the ULC's unlimited liability means shareholders are personally liable for corporate debts, including CRA claims, under BC law.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders of a ULC face full personal civil liability for the corporation's tax obligations because of the unlimited-liability legal structure; CRA may garnish personal assets, initiate bankruptcy proceedings against directors, and obtain court orders restricting directors from leaving Canada where fraud is alleged.

      

      
      

        Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance
        
Statutory sanction: TP adjustment under ITA s.247(2); penalty under s.247(3) of 10% of the net upward adjustment if no reasonable efforts are demonstrated; additional Part XIII withholding on recharacterized distributions; double-counting and gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2); CRA's application of s.17(1) on indirect benefits.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who personally approve non-arm's-length terms may be named in a director's liability assessment under s.227.1; where TP adjustments result from deliberate falsification, directors face criminal exposure under s.239 and cross-border asset seizure under s.244 reciprocal recovery provisions.

      

      
      

        AML / MSB Licensing / FINTRAC Compliance
        
Statutory sanction: Administrative Monetary Penalties (AMPs): up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for a corporation and CAD 100,000 per violation for individuals; criminal prosecution for knowingly executing an offence: fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; FINTRAC can also issue compliance orders, freeze business bank accounts, and the BC Civil Forfeiture Office can seize property.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who fail to exercise due-diligence oversight are individually liable under the PCMLTFA; directors can be prosecuted personally for beneficial-ownership omissions, and travel restrictions may be imposed under criminal bail conditions where there is a risk of flight; personal assets may be subject to restraining orders and final seizure upon conviction.
