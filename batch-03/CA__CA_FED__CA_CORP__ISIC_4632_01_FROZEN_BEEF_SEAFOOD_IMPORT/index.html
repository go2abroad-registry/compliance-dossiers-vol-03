Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; official portal: CRA My Business Account and the 'Register Your Business' online service at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and the appropriate Program Accounts (RA for corporate income tax, RC for GST/HST, RP for payroll, RM for import/export). Application is made via Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) filed online with CRA, or by mail; for customs purposes the entity must also register with the CBSA under the Import/Export system. The non-resident corporation should file Form RC1410 (Application to Obtain a Certificate of Compliance) if it intends to rely on Treaty protection, and must provide evidence of non-resident status, permanent establishment analysis, and Canadian representative contact details. Where the corporation is incorporated in Canada, CRA will issue the BN upon incorporation filing from Industry Canada; otherwise, registration is required before invoicing Canadian customers or paying Canadian employees.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Canadian business corporation is a separate legal person subject to entity-level federal and provincial corporate income tax; shareholders are taxed only on actual dividends, not on corporate earnings. Non-resident corporations are taxed in Canada on Canadian-source business income earned through a permanent establishment; the entity cannot elect to be treated as a flow-through. A domestic corporation resident in Canada is taxable on worldwide income; a non-resident corporation is taxable only on specific Canadian-source income.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election; an entity's tax classification is determined by its legal form and the Income Tax Act. Available statutory elections include: (i) the section 249.1 election to adopt a non-calendar fiscal period, (ii) the subsection 161.1 and capital gains deferral elections, (iii) the associated-corporation GST/HST grouping election under ETA section 156, (iv) the section 273 election for closely related corporations to elect to have supplies treated as internal, and (v) the hybrid mismatch adjustment elections under ITA sections 18.4 through 18.6 and 19.1 through 19.4. No election can alter overall entity-level taxation for a corporation incorporated in Canada.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

30,000 CAD (small supplier threshold: registrant is required to register when taxable supplies in Canada exceed $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the trailing four consecutive quarters; non-residents with no nexus in Canada may register voluntarily and collect GST/HST on Canadian supplies)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated for GST/HST under ETA Schedule VI Part V where the supplier sells goods to a non-resident who is not a GST/HST registrant, or where the goods are exported from Canada as a condition of the supply. For offshore services, zero-rating applies to services supplied to a non-resident person unless the service relates to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or is performed in Canada for the non-resident? (Careful application of ETA section 7 and related rules is required). In the cold-chain meat and seafood context, where imported frozen beef/seafood carcasses are subsequently sold to foreign buyers and exported in documented export transactions, the sale qualifies for zero-rating if the exporter retains evidence of export. The importer of record must, however, first pay GST/HST on the importation at the border and may claim an input tax credit only if the goods are imported for commercial consumption or exported with appropriate supporting documentation.

  • Canadian customs export declaration (Form B13A / Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) EDI output or CBSA e-manifest)
  • Customs commercial invoice and packing list
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, or equivalent shipping document showing Canadian port of lading and foreign destination
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank/foreign currency transfer proof showing payment from the non-resident buyer
  • Copy of the signed supply agreement or purchase order identifying the foreign buyer and the export terms (Incoterms)

Canada does not currently impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government-linked invoice transaction system. GST/HST registrants with annual gross revenues exceeding $1.5 million must file returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE; electronic remittances are required for payments over 50,000 CAD. CRA is progressively aligning with the Pan-Canadian e-invoicing standards, but issuance of e-invoices remains voluntary; no pre-clearance API mandate applies. All taxable suppliers must issue invoices containing prescribed information, and must retain full electronic records in a CRA-accessible format.

CRA and CBSA may audit export claims for up to four years from the end of the normal reassessment period; in cases of fraud or wilful misrepresentation, the reassessment period is unlimited. Where zero-rating is claimed without adequate evidence, CRA will reassess the supply at 5% GST/HST, add arrears interest, and may impose penalties under ETA section 285 (false statements) and ITA section 163(2) (gross negligence penalties of 50% of the net tax understated). Criminal prosecution for deliberate evasion under ETA section 306 and ITA section 239 can lead to fines up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment. Retroactive clawback can extend to input tax credits claimed on the related imported goods, creating a cumulative liability.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII. Canada maintains no general foreign exchange controls; both current-account and capital-account transactions may be effected in CAD or foreign currency without central bank approval. Profit remittance, including dividends, interest, royalties, and branch repatriations, is legally permitted but subject to the Canada Business Corporations Act solvency tests, Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory default 25%, treaty-reduced as applicable), and FINTRAC cross-border transfer reporting thresholds. Non-bank entities that provide currency exchange or money transfer services to the public must register as Money Services Businesses and comply with FINTRAC and provincial licensing regimes.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules, no mandatory conversion ratios, and no prescribed timeline for repatriation of export receipts. Canadian exporters may hold foreign-currency accounts offshore or in domestic banks. Trade data reporting to Statistics Canada via the Canada Border Services Agency (G13 export declaration) remains obligatory, and any cross-border EFT or wire of C$10,000 or above must be reported by the sending financial institution to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential banking supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), anti-money laundering supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and no dedicated foreign exchange administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium only as a supplementary payment gateway and Low as a primary banking rail. Canada lacks a distinct Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licensing category; offshore digital EMIs are not deposit-taking institutions under the Bank Act and cannot obtain direct membership in Payments Canada's Lynx or Automated Clearing Settlement System. An offshore EMI may offer virtual accounts, but CAD settlement must traverse a sponsoring Canadian Schedule I bank, and client balances are not eligible for CDIC coverage. Without FINTRAC MSB registration and, where applicable, provincial money-services licensing, an offshore EMI's acceptance of Canadian-dollar settlement may constitute unlawful deposit-taking or unlicensed MSB activity. Use only for low-value disbursements with enforceable warranty and no reliance on CDIC deposit insurance.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally access the 9% federal small business rate on eligible active business income within the CAD 500,000 business limit, subject to associated-corporation and passive-income restrictions. Provincial or territorial corporate tax applies separately.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to statutory exemptions and an applicable tax treaty. Under the Canada-China tax treaty, the rate is generally 10% where the beneficial owner is a company holding at least 10% of the payer's capital; otherwise, the treaty rate is generally 15%, subject to treaty conditions and any later protocol or applicable domestic limitation.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A CCPC meeting the statutory conditions for the extended payment period may pay its balance within three months after year-end. Tax instalments are due monthly unless the corporation qualifies for quarterly instalments.

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall

Use the Canadian business number and identify the Canadian permanent establishment. A non-resident corporation must not claim Canadian residence merely because it has a Canadian customer, bank account, or agent. State the jurisdiction of incorporation and apply treaty-residence rules separately. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a formal OECD-style Local File or Master File filing threshold. Canadian taxpayers must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices and maintain contemporaneous documentation under Income Tax Act section 247. Form T106 generally applies where the aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million or another statutory reporting condition is met. The documentation must be prepared by the income-tax return filing date and supplied to the CRA within three months of a written request. Country-by-country reporting may apply to an in-scope multinational enterprise group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million and a Canadian constituent entity or surrogate filing obligation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates or certain other jurisdictions. Economic substance remains relevant under the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, foreign-affiliate rules, interest deductibility, thin capitalization, and the principal-purpose test where applicable. CRA may challenge a paper intermediary, conduit financing structure, unsupported management charge, circular payment, or low-substance distribution entity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and prosecution under the Income Tax Act. Tax evasion may result in fines between 50% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability where they participated in, authorized, acquiesced in, or directed the conduct. Criminal conviction may produce fines, imprisonment, restitution, and a criminal record. Imprisonment and travel restrictions are not automatic for an ordinary assessment.
Failure to Remit Payroll or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The corporation may owe the unremitted amount, arrears interest, late-remittance penalties, and information-return penalties. Part XIII liability can include the full withholding shortfall, interest, and penalties even where the recipient received the gross amount.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally under the director-liability provisions for unremitted source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. Personal liability can include civil recovery proceedings and collection measures; imprisonment requires separate criminal conduct.
GST/HST and Import Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Assessment of GST/HST, customs duties, interest, civil penalties, seizure, reassessment, denial of input tax credits, and potential criminal prosecution under tax and customs legislation. False customs declarations may create substantial administrative monetary penalties and forfeiture exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for unremitted GST/HST and source deductions where statutory conditions are met. Knowing participation in customs or tax fraud may result in fines, imprisonment, restitution, and asset seizure.
Transfer Pricing and Related-Party Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, and apply interest and reassessment penalties. Treaty competent-authority procedures may be available but do not suspend all filing or payment obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is made. Personal civil or criminal exposure arises from knowing false filings, fraud, obstruction, or participation in evasion.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, and Conduit Arrangements

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, withholding-tax reassessment, GAAR reassessment, interest, penalties, information demands, and potential application of anti-money-laundering, tax-evasion, or proceeds-of-crime offences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, regulatory enforcement, personal tax assessments, criminal prosecution, asset restraint, restitution, and imprisonment where they knowingly participated. Corporate separateness is not automatically disregarded; veil piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent.
Corporate Record and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate and individual offences under the Canada Business Corporations Act may result in fines and, for certain offences, imprisonment. Separate provincial corporate-registry obligations may apply if the company is provincially incorporated or registered.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted personally for knowingly authorizing, permitting, or acquiescing in non-compliance. A travel ban is not a routine corporate-record sanction.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Suspicious Trade Activity

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, public naming, transaction restrictions, and prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act or Criminal Code. Proceeds-of-crime offences can lead to forfeiture and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers and directors may face personal enforcement or criminal liability for knowing participation, consent, or willful blindness. Asset restraint, restitution, and imprisonment may follow conviction.
Food, Import, and Cold-Chain Regulatory Breach

Statutory sanction: The Canadian Food Inspection Agency may refuse entry, detain, recall, seize, suspend or revoke licences, issue administrative monetary penalties, and prosecute offences under the Safe Food for Canadians Act, Food and Drugs Act, and related regulations.

Director personal liability: Corporate officers and directors may be prosecuted where they directed, authorized, or acquiesced in the offence. Penalties may include fines and imprisonment depending on the statute and conduct; personal travel bans are not automatic.
Customs Valuation, Origin, and Sanctions Evasion

Statutory sanction: CBSA reassessment, duties and taxes, interest, penalties, seizure, forfeiture, AMPS penalties, import privileges suspension, and prosecution under customs, export-control, sanctions, or criminal legislation.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly direct or participate in evasion or sanctions violations may face significant fines, imprisonment, asset freezing, and forfeiture. Corporate limited liability does not protect a director from personal criminal conduct.
Dividend and Capital Repatriation Mischaracterization

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization as a taxable dividend, shareholder benefit, deemed dividend, interest benefit, or withholding-tax liability, together with corporate-law remedies, interest, penalties, and potential reassessment of related deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face claims for unlawful distributions, creditor prejudice, breach of fiduciary duty, or knowing tax misconduct. Personal repayment or restitution may be ordered; imprisonment requires a separate criminal offence.
