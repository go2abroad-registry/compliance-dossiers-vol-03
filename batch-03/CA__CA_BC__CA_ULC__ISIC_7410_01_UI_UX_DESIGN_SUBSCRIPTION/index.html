Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (for Canadian federal and provincial income tax, a BC ULC is a corporation resident in Canada under ITA s.250(4)(c) and must file a Part I T2 corporate return; Pass_Through_Eligible applies only to the parallel US check-the-box election, which has no effect on CRA treatment; Territorial_Scope and Tax_Exempt_Zone do not apply in Canada)

Available election forms

(1) US check-the-box election on IRS Form 8832 (filed within 75 days of formation) treating the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for US federal tax; this is not a Canadian election and does not alter CRA entity-level taxation. (2) GST/HST Quick Method election (Form GST74) available to qualifying small businesses whose annual worldwide taxable supplies do not exceed CAD 400,000, simplifying GST remittance on design-subscription revenue. (3) Election under ETA s.156 for nil-consideration taxable supplies between closely related corporations once a corporate group exists. (4) ETA s.238 reporting-period election permitting annual or quarterly GST/HST filing for small registrants. (5) T2 attribute elections (e.g., ITA s.89(11) paid-up capital maintenance elections) to preserve capital tax attributes during ULC restructuring; all Canadian elections must be filed on prescribed forms and are subject to CRA administrative interpretation of hybrid ULC status.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: small-supplier threshold under ETA s.148(1), measured on worldwide taxable supplies (and for non-residents, Canadian-source taxable supplies) in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and the current calendar quarter; a ULC whose Canadian taxable design-subscription supplies exceed CAD 30,000 must register for GST/HST and charge 5% GST. Voluntary registration before the threshold is reached is available and is generally advisable to recover input tax credits on Canadian operating costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under ETA Schedule VI, Part V (s.16 for services supplied to non-residents): the UI/UX design subscription must be supplied to a non-resident of Canada who is not registered for GST/HST at the time of performance, and the service must not be rendered in connection with real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed. Digital delivery of design-as-a-service to foreign commercial clients qualifies for the 0% rate with full input tax credit entitlement; if the subscription is framed as a licence of digital design assets, the CRA may characterize the supply as intangible personal property under ETA s.123(1), which is likewise zero-rated as an export under Schedule VI, Part V. The supplier must retain written evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (foreign address, foreign business registration, contract governed by foreign law) and must not charge GST on the export invoices; Stripe Billing and Wise payment rails are acceptable settlement channels.

  • Customs Export Declaration (not applicable to intangible digital services; no B13 or GCA export declaration is filed for UI/UX subscription exports — substitute a signed Design-As-A-Service subscription agreement, the commercial invoice, and a written statement describing the digital delivery method)
  • Bill of Lading (substitute evidence of electronic delivery: server/IP delivery logs, recipient-domain email confirmation, user-licence issuance records, and acceptance records from the foreign counterparty)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank foreign-exchange credit voucher (CRA-accepted bank statement, SWIFT MT103, or Stripe/Wise settlement report demonstrating inbound foreign-currency payment to the ULC's Canadian bank account)

None: Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, API clearance, or digital stamping regime for GST/HST or BC PST. Vendors must issue compliant invoices under ETA s.223 and the Input Tax Credit Information (GST/HST) Regulations (SOR/91-30), showing supplier business name, BN and GST/HST registration number, invoice date, description of supply, consideration, and tax rate/amount. Electronic invoicing is permitted and PEPPOL-based e-invoicing is voluntary in Canada, with federal government-procurement mandates phasing in gradually.

CRA audit of zero-rated export claims focuses on (i) non-resident identity of the recipient, (ii) absence of connection to Canadian realty or goods, and (iii) provenance of payment. If zero-rating is denied, the CRA may reassess GST at 5% with compounding interest and impose penalties: ETA s.280 (failure to register or remit — the greater of CAD 250 and 1% of the value of taxable supplies), ETA s.285 (false statement or omission — 50% of the tax sought to be evaded), ITA s.163(2) (gross negligence — 50% of understated tax), and ITA s.239 (criminal evasion — fine of 50% to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years). The standard reassessment window is 4 years under ETA s.298, extended to 7 years for special reassessments and unlimited in cases of misrepresentation or fraud. Canadian tax law imposes no statutory FX repatriation deadline of 180 days; however, the 180-day benchmark should be used as an internal compliance control because delayed foreign remittance weakens the export-evidence record and heightens CRA export-verification and transfer-pricing scrutiny.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a fully convertible currency under a floating exchange rate regime. The Bank of Canada does not impose exchange controls or repatriation requirements on capital or current account transactions. Canada maintains liberalized cross-border capital movements under the OECD Codes of Liberalisation and the WTO commitments. Profit remittance, dividends, and capital repatriation by foreign-owned entities are generally unrestricted, provided that such payments are supported by substantiated financial statements, arm's-length transfer pricing documentation (under section 247 of the Income Tax Act), and evidence of tax compliance under Part XIII non-resident withholding tax and the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) requirements. A Canadian ULC, depending on its tax classification, may be treated as a partnership or disregarded entity for US federal tax purposes, but for Canadian tax purposes, it is a resident corporation subject to the Income Tax Act; dividend payments to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at 25% unless reduced under an applicable tax treaty. There is no Canadian legislation restricting current account convertibility, and no material hurdle exists for the repatriation of genuine retained earnings from a British Columbia ULC engaged in design services, provided the applicable corporate filings are in good standing and all tax remittances are current.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada maintains no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. All export proceeds may be retained in Canadian dollar or foreign currency accounts without forced repatriation or conversion. However, for reporting and tax compliance, Canadian export revenue arising from a BC-resident ULC must be included in the corporation's taxable income, and export transactions with non-resident related parties must meet the arm's-length standard under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. Where revenue is received in foreign currency (e.g., USD), the corporation is required under Canadian GAAP / IFRS and the Income Tax Act to translate such amounts into CAD using the Bank of Canada annual average exchange rate or the spot rate at the transaction date for tax purposes. Payments received via cross-border PSPs such as Stripe or Wise must be recorded in the ULC's books, and any foreign currency balances are subject to unrealized exchange gain or loss recognition for tax purposes. No surrender, repurchase, or conversion deadline applies under the Foreign Exchange Control regime, which was dismantled in the early 1950s. The relevant compliance obligation is the E003 electronic funds transfer reporting to FINTRAC for incoming or outgoing remittances of CAD 10,000 or more in a single transaction or within 24 hours.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Department of Finance Canada, with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the financial intelligence unit and AML/CFT supervisor

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) are not recognized as licensed Canadian banks and generally cannot provide native Canadian deposit accounts. A BC-incorporated ULC must open at least one domestic Canadian bank account to satisfy the 'Canadian resident corporation with a permanent establishment in BC' substance requirements. Offshore fintech EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, or Airwallex can be used as auxiliary payment collection and disbursement settlement rails in parallel with the domestic account, but they should not be the sole banking infrastructure. These EMIs are typically contractually regulated by their home-state financial authorities (e.g., FCA, ASIC, MAS) and do not benefit from Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) protection. Certain EMIs may offer local CAD virtual account numbers or routing numbers through a Canadian partner bank if they hold a money services business (MSB) registration in Canada; as of the current regulatory perimeter, FINTRAC MSB registration is required if the EMI operates in Canada, and several non-resident EMIs do not maintain such registration. To remain jurisdictionally safe, the ULC shall execute an operating agreement with the EMI that restricts services to collection of subscription payments from foreign customers and settlement of foreign vendor invoices, while Canadian-source operating cash flows are swept into the domestic RBC or TD account. Sustainable financial audit evidence requires full bank statements from all rails, so transaction-level record synchronization between EMI platform ledgers and the ULC general ledger must be implemented.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan funding to the BC ULC must be wired to the primary operational account at RBC or TD using the SWIFT/RTGS rails with the correct Canadian clearing code and institution number. For CAD-denominated injections, a domestic wire transfer using the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) or a real-time rail such as the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) is required. For USD injections, remit through correspondent banks using a Fedwire or SWIFT MT103 to the ULC's domestic USD account. Purpose codes and transmission matter: (i) a subscription for shares shall be documented by a board resolution, subscription agreement, and share issuance ledger; (ii) a shareholder loan shall be evidenced by a written promissory note with arm's-length interest rate and repayment term, respecting the mandatory thin capitalization rules under subsection 18(4) of the Income Tax Act for loans from non-Canadian residents; (iii) to avoid application of section 212.3 (foreign affiliate dumping) if the ULC has a foreign affiliate parent, the ULC should maintain a robust repatriation plan and treaty-based capital-structure analysis. Incoming wire instructions must reference a clear cross-border purpose code; the underlying documentary package includes a copy of the incorporation document, a CRA Business Number confirmation, and the bank's FATCA/CRS self-certification form. Foreign injections are not subject to prior central bank approval, but a wire at or above CAD 10,000 within a 24-hour period triggers a FINTRAC electronic funds transfer report (EFTR), and an optional suspicious transaction report (STR) may follow if the source-of-funds narrative is inadequate. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate for a British Columbia corporation: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may obtain a combined small-business rate of approximately 11% on eligible active business income up to the applicable CAD 500,000 business limit, but a ULC controlled by non-residents will generally not qualify as a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to a lower treaty rate. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the ordinary rate is generally 15%, with a 5% rate generally available where the beneficial owner is a company that directly owns at least 10% of the voting power of the payer. Treaty relief requires beneficial ownership and all other treaty conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 is the principal reporting threshold commonly relevant to Form T106 aggregate non-arm's-length non-resident transactions and to the transfer-pricing documentation penalty exception. Canadian law does not create a blanket exemption from the arm's-length principle below CAD 1,000,000. Contemporaneous documentation should be prepared whenever material related-party cross-border transactions exist. Canada generally does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File and Local File filing requirement for every taxpayer; the taxpayer must maintain documentation sufficient under Income Tax Act section 247. A country-by-country report generally applies to an international group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, subject to Canadian filing and notification rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership requirements, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, principal-purpose analysis, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and legal form. The ULC should maintain real commercial functions, decision-making, personnel or appropriately documented outsourced capabilities, customer and vendor contracts, IP ownership or valid licensing, bank-account control, accounting records, board approvals, and evidence that intercompany charges reflect functions, assets, risks, and benefits. A shell ULC receiving subscription revenue while strategic, operational, IP, and customer functions are performed elsewhere presents material reassessment and treaty-denial risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credits attributable to the conduct, collection measures, and possible prosecution. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal liability where they participated in, authorized, acquiesced in, or directed the offence. The corporation normally bears assessed corporate income tax, but directors can face personal exposure for source deductions and certain other statutory remittances, fines, restitution, and imprisonment. A travel ban is not an automatic tax sanction but may arise through criminal bail, sentencing, or enforcement processes.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer can be assessed for the unwithheld amount, related interest, and penalties. Failure-to-deduct penalties can generally equal 10% of the amount that should have been withheld, increasing to 20% for repeated failures where statutory conditions apply. Separate penalties may apply for late remittance and information-return failures.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally under the director's liability rules for unremitted source deductions if the statutory conditions are satisfied and the corporation's collection is ineffective. A director may defend the assessment by proving due diligence. Imprisonment is possible for wilful criminal conduct; ordinary assessment does not automatically create a travel ban.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust Canadian income under Income Tax Act section 247, deny deductions, impose Part XIII withholding consequences, and apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met and reasonable efforts are absent. Repeated or deliberate misrepresentation can trigger gross-negligence or prosecution exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, reassessment-related consequences, and criminal exposure if they knowingly participate in fraud or false reporting. Corporate transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically pierce the corporate veil or make directors personally liable for ordinary corporate tax.
Treaty Abuse, Conduit, or Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty relief, apply the full 25% Part XIII rate, reassess withholding tax, impose interest and penalties, and invoke the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, or principal-purpose rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly participating in false treaty certifications or failure to remit withholding taxes. Criminal liability requires the applicable mens rea and statutory proof; treaty denial alone does not automatically create imprisonment or a travel ban.
Shell Company and Economic-Substance Deficiency

Statutory sanction: Possible denial of deductions, transfer-pricing adjustments, treaty-benefit denial, GAAR application, reassessment of residency or source, withholding exposure, and penalties for inaccurate returns or information filings. Canada does not impose a standalone ESR penalty merely because a company has limited physical substance.

Director personal liability: Directors can face fiduciary, tax-remittance, and criminal exposure for intentional deception or misuse of the corporation. A ULC's unlimited-liability structure increases shareholder exposure under applicable corporate law but does not automatically impose all corporate tax debts on directors.
Corporate Residence and Central-Management Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may determine residence under Canadian law and treaty tie-breaker rules, reassess worldwide income, deny treaty positions, impose interest and penalties, and require additional filings. The Canada-US treaty contains special rules relevant to hybrid entities and dual-residence situations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for deliberate false statements, source-remittance failures, and statutory offences. Civil veil piercing remains exceptional and generally requires fraud, improper conduct, or a specific statutory basis.
Prohibited Corporate Distributions and Insolvency Conduct

Statutory sanction: Potential recovery of improper distributions, shareholder-loan inclusion, denial of deductions, withholding reassessment, creditor remedies, and director liability under applicable British Columbia corporate and insolvency legislation. Transactions designed to defeat creditors may be challenged or set aside.

Director personal liability: Directors may face repayment orders, statutory compensation, breach-of-duty claims, and, in fraudulent or criminal cases, fines or imprisonment. Canadian tax law does not impose a general travel ban solely for an improper dividend.
AML, Sanctions, and Payment-Rail Misuse

Statutory sanction: Account suspension, payment seizure, suspicious-transaction reporting, asset restraint, administrative monetary penalties, forfeiture, and criminal prosecution under Canadian anti-money-laundering, proceeds-of-crime, terrorist-financing, and sanctions legislation. Penalties vary by offence and enforcement authority.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, beneficial owners, and authorized signatories may face personal fines, forfeiture, criminal prosecution, and imprisonment where they participated in or knowingly facilitated the conduct. Travel restrictions can arise from arrest, bail, court orders, sanctions designations, or forfeiture proceedings, but are not automatic for ordinary AML deficiencies.
GST/HST and Indirect-Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted GST/HST, interest, late-filing and gross-negligence penalties, denial of input tax credits, and possible prosecution for tax fraud. British Columbia PST treatment must also be assessed where taxable software, taxable services, or related goods are supplied.
