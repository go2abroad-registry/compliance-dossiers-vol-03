Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

For Canadian domestic income tax, a BC ULC is a resident corporation subject to entity-level taxation under the Income Tax Act; there is no domestic Canadian check-the-box election. For United States federal tax purposes, a Canadian ULC wholly or majority owned by a U.S. person is eligible to check the box under Treasury Regulation §301.7701-3 to be classified as a disregarded entity (single-member) or a partnership (multi-member), producing pass-through treatment in the United States. Such divergent classification creates hybrid entity status and triggers OECD BEPS Action 2 / hybrid mismatch rules, Canadian GAAR, and reporting obligations under Part XVIII. Canada also permits certain deemed-status or fair-value elections in connection with foreign affiliate and cross-border transactions, but these do not alter the fundamental domestic corporate tax treatment of the ULC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in a calendar quarter and over the preceding four calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold; non-resident remote digital suppliers also use CAD 30,000). BC PST: CAD 10,000 of taxable sales to customers in British Columbia in a calendar year (PST small-seller threshold); once exceeded, a non-resident or remote vendor must register and collect BC PST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act, Schedule VI, supplies are zero-rated when: (i) physical goods are exported from Canada by the supplier or for the supplier and satisfy the documentary evidence requirements; (ii) services are supplied to a non-resident recipient, provided the service is not performed in respect of Canadian real property, not rendered to an individual physically in Canada, and not directly connected to Canadian tangible personal property; and (iii) intangible personal property, including SaaS and digital no-code website-builder subscriptions, is supplied to a non-resident and the evidence establishes the non-resident status of the recipient and the foreign place of use or receipt. Digital subscription services delivered online to non-resident customers on platforms such as Stripe Billing and Paddle generally qualify as zero-rated exports. BC PST does not apply to zero-rated exports of goods or to software delivered to non-residents outside BC.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CERS/B13A) for physical exported goods
  • Commercial Invoice describing the goods, digital service, or software licence
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (for physical goods)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/SWIFT payment settlement evidence
  • Written contract or purchase order identifying the non-resident recipient
  • Non-resident status evidence, such as foreign tax identification number or passport/address proof
  • Delivery and usage logs for digital services, including subscriber IP addresses and server logs
  • GST/HST export record or self-declaration of export intent

Canada does not impose mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control e-invoicing, API pre-authorization, or digital stamping at the federal level. CRA accepts electronic invoices and records if they are complete, legible, and retained in a reproducible format. GST/HST registrants must remit returns electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or CRA-certified software. BC PST registrants must file and remit through eTaxBC. Voluntary PEPPOL-compliant e-invoicing is available but not legally mandated. No pre-clearance or stamping requirement applies before issuing an invoice.

CRA may deny a zero-rating claim where export substantiation is incomplete or the payment evidence is not retained within the prescribed documentary timeframe. A retroactive reassessment may impose uncollected GST/HST plus interest at prescribed rates. Penalties include administrative fines for false statements or omissions, gross-negligence penalties calculated with reference to the net tax misstatement, and, in serious cases, prosecution for wilful evasion. Where a ULC claims tax-free export status for internal digital services and fails to demonstrate non-resident receipt, CRA may impose a 25% or higher civil penalty on the understated tax, extend the normal four-year reassessment period to seven years for misrepresentation, and disallow input tax credits. The BC Ministry of Finance may claw back PST exemptions and charge interest and penalties on unremitted PST for non-compliant remote software vendors.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible with a fully open current account and capital account. There are no general exchange controls, capital controls, prior licenses, or approval requirements for foreign direct investment, shareholder equity injections, intercompany loans, dividend remittance, or profit repatriation. Convertibility is constrained only by standard AML/KYC requirements, FINTRAC cross-border reporting thresholds, FATCA/CRS self-certification, and ordinary banking due diligence. Profit remittance may be subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends, interest, rents, or royalties and to the Canada-US tax treaty if applicable; however, no central bank or exchange administration obstacle exists.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds, cross-border software licensing revenues, or SaaS subscription receipts. Exporters and service providers may retain foreign currency in offshore or onshore accounts and convert at their discretion. All incoming and outgoing wire transfers and EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported by banks, money service businesses, and authorized financial institutions under FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting rules. The ULC must therefore maintain full supporting commercial documentation for all cross-border receipts and disbursements to satisfy anti-money-laundering due diligence.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) and the Department of Finance Canada; foreign exchange administration in Canada is not centralized in a single control authority. The Currency Act, the Exchange Control Act (largely repealed), the Bank Act, and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act govern cross-border currency and reporting. Operational oversight for banking and solvency is conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while market conduct in the securities and derivatives space falls under the Canadian Securities Administrators and the Bank of Canada for clearing and settlement system oversight.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low to moderate for primary treasury purposes. Foreign fintech EMIs and digital banking platforms, including UK-style e-money institutions or U.S.-licensed digital banks, are not authorized to act as Canadian Schedule I or II banks and do not have direct membership in Payments Canada payment systems. They may be used as auxiliary collection or payout rails, but only if registered as a money services business with FINTRAC in Canada and compliant with the Retail Payment Activities Act once fully implemented. A ULC must maintain its core settlement and treasury accounts with a Canadian chartered bank to ensure LVTS clearing, SWIFT correspondent access, and full regulatory certainty. Use of EMIs is acceptable only for low-value subscription collection and contractor payouts.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The ULC should maintain a CAD operational account and a USD operational account with a Schedule I Canadian bank to receive cross-border funding. Equity subscriptions and shareholder loans should be routed by SWIFT MT103 from the parent or shareholder to the ULC's account, using the standard bank BIC; field 70 should identify the transaction as 'SHARE CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' or 'INTERCOMPANY LOAN' because Canada does not assign standard product purpose codes. The receiving bank will request the executed share subscription agreement, corporate resolution, board consent, source-of-funds confirmation, and FATCA/CRS self-certification before releasing funds into the operating account. Once cleared, funds are settled through Payments Canada LVTS for Canadian-dollar wires or through the respective USD clearing channel and the bank's internal ledger for US-dollar deposits. All transfers equal to or above CAD 10,000 are subject to FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting.Outbound trade and service payments should be cleared using SWIFT MT103 for high-value and one-off vendor settlements, SWIFT MT202 for corporate-to-corporate treasury transfers, and through the bank's USD correspondent network via Fedwire or CHIPS where required. Domestic Canadian dollar disbursements may be cleared through Payments Canada ACSS and LVTS, and lower-value payments can be made via Interac e-Transfer, wire transfer API, or automated clearing entry. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is required where the aggregate fair market value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceeds C$1,000,000 per year. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required for multinational groups with consolidated revenue equal to or exceeding C$750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate Economic Substance Regulations in the Caribbean/ESR sense, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245) and transfer-pricing provisions (s. 247) impose a genuine economic-substance standard. A ULC that exists solely as a tax-transparent shell, with no Canadian office, employees, or business functions, is exposed to CRA recharacterization, treaty-benefit denial, and reassessment with interest and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Gross Negligence

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under s. 239 ITA carries a fine between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded and up to 5 years imprisonment. Civil gross negligence penalty under s. 163(2) is 50% of the understated tax plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who authorized false statements can be criminally prosecuted and personally fined. Under BC ULC statutes, shareholders have unlimited liability for the company's obligations, including federal tax debts, when the corporation is unable to pay.
Transfer Pricing Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may reprice the transaction under s. 247, assess additional tax plus arrears interest, and impose a penalty equal to 10% of the reassessment amount (the greater of C$5,000 or 10% of the adjustment).

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for TP penalties, but if the mispricing amounts to negligence or willful blindness, directors can be held jointly and severally liable under the federal Income Tax Act for source deduction failures and, in BC, under the ULC unlimited liability regime.
Shell Company / GAAR / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The CRA can invoke GAAR (s. 245) to deny the treaty benefit and re-dissect the transaction, resulting in full Part XIII withholding (25%), accumulated interest, and a 50% misrepresentation penalty where falsified documentation exists.

Director personal liability: Directors remain exposed to non-compliance under the BC B.C. Unlimited Liability Company Act; the unlimited liability of shareholders and directors is not capped where corporate formalities are ignored and personal guarantees or nominee arrangements veil the reality of control.
AML / Registry Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: BC Registrar can dissolve the ULC for non-filing; failure to comply with AML obligations can result in administrative monetary penalties up to C$10 million for repeated corporate non-compliance, and criminal fines for directors and officers.

Director personal liability: A dissolved ULC exposes shareholders to joint personal liability for liabilities incurred after dissolution. Directors who knowingly allow the company to be used for laundering proceeds face forfeiture of assets, travel bans, and imprisonment under the Criminal Code of Canada (s. 462.31).
