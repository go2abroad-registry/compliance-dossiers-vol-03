Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); official portal: My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html), Business Registration Online (BRO), GST/HST NETFILE; customs and export reporting supervised by Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and Global Affairs Canada.

E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Territorial_Scope: a Canadian-resident corporation is taxable under Part I on worldwide income, whereas a non-resident corporation is taxable on Canadian-source income derived from carrying on business in Canada, from dispositions of taxable Canadian property, and from certain passive Canadian-source payments; Pass_Through_Eligible: false; Tax_Exempt_Zone: not applicable (no federal tax holiday zones for this trade); the entity is a per se C-corporation for Canadian tax purposes.

Available election forms

No check-the-box entity classification election exists in Canada; all business corporations are taxed at entity level by statute. Available elections include: (1) GST/HST election under ETA s. 225.1(7) to allocate net input tax credits using a methodology based on qualifying use; (2) ITA s. 216 election for non-resident recipient of rental or royalty income to compute tax on net income; (3) ITA s. 219 deemed-taxable-Canadian-corporation election for foreign corporations with a Canadian permanent establishment; (4) election under ETA Part IV to treat certain taxable services supplied by a non-resident as a taxable self-supply; (5) hybrid mismatch imputation elections under ITA ss. 18.2, 18.3 and 18.4 for dual-inclusion income and controlled foreign entity distributions; (6) GST/HST combined fiscal year election and closely related corporation election under ETA s. 156.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: a supplier ceases to be a small supplier and becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant once total worldwide taxable supplies (including those of associates) exceed CAD 30,000 in either a single calendar quarter or in the sum of the immediately preceding four calendar quarters (ETA s. 148(1); ITA meaning of small supplier). Voluntary registration is available below the threshold. Non-resident persons carrying on business in Canada face the same CAD 30,000 registration threshold. In participating provinces the provincial component raises the combined HST headline rate to 13% (Ontario), 15% (New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island); British Columbia, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba apply 5% federal GST plus provincial PST at 7%, 6%, and 7% respectively.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under s. 1, Part V, Schedule VI to the ETA where the supplier sells and exports the goods in commercial quantities to a non-resident or to a registered recipient intended to export the goods, and proof of export is obtained within 60 days (or up to two years where goods are exported after processing abroad). Services supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and who is not carrying on business in Canada are zero-rated under s. 7, Part V, Schedule VI, subject to the 'use in Canada' carve-outs. For the wholesale distribution of multilayer ceramic capacitors and resistors (ISIC 4652 / NAICS 423690), zero-rating applies where title and risk pass outside Canada, the goods are physically exported, the exporter holds contemporaneous export evidence, and the supplier maintains a full input tax credit ledger demonstrating the export-link; warehousing arrangements that delay export beyond the statutory period will trigger mandatory GST/HST self-assessment.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CBSA B13A / Canadian Export Declaration (non-U.S. destinations) or ACE/CARM electronic export reporting for shipments to the United States; Global Affairs Canada export reporting references
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill showing shipper, consignee, port of exit, commodity description, and date of export
  • Commercial Invoice with full HS classification (e.g., HS 8532.24 for fixed ceramic capacitors, HS 8533.21 for resistors), quantity, unit value, and total export sale consideration
  • Packing List and cargo control documents (ACI eManifest / PARS for highway shipments, or ACI marine/air manifests)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, bank ledger entry, or letter of credit settlement evidencing receipt of export proceeds
  • Incoterms and contractual evidence (FOB, CIF, FCA) demonstrating transfer of risk and title outside Canada
  • GST/HST input tax credit matching ledger and the exporter's internal zero-rating worksheet tracing the exported goods to the corresponding GST/HST-paid inventory purchases

Canada does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control regime, API-based e-invoicing clearance, or digital stamping requirement. E-invoicing is voluntary and aligned with the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing initiative; the Government of Canada expects to receive electronic invoices through the Peppol network beginning in 2026. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via NETFILE/EFILE when registered; all accounting records and export substantiation documents must be retained in Canada or be available electronically to the CRA upon request (ETA s. 286) for a period of at least six years. The CRA uses post-audit data extraction and international exchange mechanisms (CbC reports, Common Reporting Standard) rather than pre-clearance fiscal approval.

The CRA cross-verifies zero-rating claims against CBSA export manifests, carrier air/sea waybills, customs broker records, banking statements, and non-resident counterparty registration status. Where export substantiation is missing or non-contemporaneous, the CRA reassesses within the normal four-year period, or without limitation where misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default is established, and applies uncollected GST/HST, daily compound interest at the prescribed statutory rate under ETA s. 280, and penalties. Specific penalties: false statements or omissions in a return attract the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax under ETA s. 285(1)(a); gross negligence gives rise to a 50% penalty on the understated tax or overstated refund under ETA s. 285(1)(b); an overclaimed refund triggers a separate refund penalty of 10% of the excess under ETA s. 281.5; deliberate fraud may be prosecuted criminally under ETA s. 327, with fines and imprisonment up to five years. Transfer pricing scrutiny for intercompany export sales applies under ITA s. 247 with contemporaneous documentation mandated within six months of filing; failure to provide documentation can expose a corporation to adjustment of export profit and the 10% transfer-pricing penalty.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible, floating Canadian-dollar regime. No exchange-control permits, central-bank approvals, or foreign-exchange approvals are required for current-account or capital-account transactions, including dividend distributions, interest, royalties, management fees, capital injections, or liquidation proceeds payable to non-resident shareholders. The Foreign Exchange Control Act expired in 1951 and was never reinstated. Profit repatriation is subject only to Part XIII withholding tax of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, generally reduced by treaty, e.g., 5% on active-business dividends under the Canada-U.S. Treaty and 0-5% or other reduced rates under CUSMA or other applicable income tax treaties), plus standard bank KYC documentation. The Investment Canada Act requires a simple notification (not prior approval) when a non-resident establishes a new Canadian federal business corporation or acquires control of a non-cultural Canadian business below the applicable financial thresholds; the notification does not involve FX approvals or remittance permits. All conversions of CAD to USD or other currencies are executed at market rates through a bank or registered foreign-exchange dealer.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no statutory requirement to surrender, convert, or repatriate export proceeds within any defined timeline, and no mandatory conversion ratio applies to export receipts. Exporters in the ISIC 4652 / NAICS 423690 passive-electronic-components trade may retain CAD, USD, or any foreign-currency balances indefinitely in foreign-currency accounts at Canadian chartered banks. The only relevant statutory obligations are: (i) FINTRAC reporting by the financial institution of any electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more entering or leaving Canada on the same business day; and (ii) retention by the exporter of commercial documents (sales invoices, bills of lading, packing lists, supplier debit/credit notes) for at least five years to permit AML, tax, and trade-investigation audit trails.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC), together with the Department of Finance Canada (Currency Act), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML enforcement and cross-border fund reporting, and Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) for foreign-investment notification under the Investment Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium, conditional. Canada does not recognise a standalone electronic-money-institution (EMI) licence; deposit-taking is reserved for chartered banks under the Bank Act (Canada). Offshore digital EMIs and payment processors (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex) may onboard the Canadian federal corporation remotely for payment initiation, corporate cards, or vendor disbursements, but they operate under money-services-business (MSB) registration with FINTRAC and provincial money-services regulation. They cannot provide a true Canadian clearing account with a Lynx settlement membership, a Canadian bank number for CAD direct deposits, or CDIC-insured deposits. For the ISIC 4652 passive-components distribution model, EMIs may be viable as a secondary low-value disbursement rail for small suppliers or reimbursements, but they are not a lawful substitute for the primary Schedule I chartered-bank treasury account, particularly for commercial letters of credit, USD wire concentration, and revolving credit facilities.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Root cause: Non-resident parental shareholding structured through a Cayman Islands or BVI intermediate entity, making the ultimate source of wealth opaque

Defense evidence: Complete corporate-ownership chain from the ultimate individual beneficial owner to the Canadian entity; register of individuals with significant control (Corporations Canada or provincial registry); notarised certificate of incumbency; passport copies and source-of-wealth declaration from the ultimate beneficial owner; bank references from the parent's home bank; the board resolution authorising share issuance; and the share-subscription agreement referencing the inbound capital.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant pathway: the non-resident parent initiates a SWIFT MT103 (or Fedwire/CHIPS for USD) in favour of the Canadian federal corporation's Schedule I bank account, with payment field 70 coded as 'EQUITY INJECTION / SHARE SUBSCRIPTION' and referencing the board resolution. The funds may initially be placed in a designated Capital Escrow Account at the same bank and released to the operating account only after the bank verifies: (i) the board resolution approving the share issuance; (ii) the share-subscription agreement; (iii) the ISED notification confirmation under the Investment Canada Act where thresholds apply; and (iv) FINTRAC's 24-hour EFT report obligation if the transfer equals CAD 10,000 or more. No Bank of Canada approval is required. For USD funding, the bank converts at the applicable spot rate under a signed FX confirmation which must be retained for audit. The corporation must maintain records of the capital receipt, and must respect Canadian thin-capitalisation and transfer-pricing rules where the inbound funding is structured partly as shareholder debt.Two-layer clearing architecture is recommended. Layer 1 (routine disbursements): SWIFT MT103 wire transfers through the Canadian bank's Lynx/SWIFT gateway for supplier invoices below CAD 100,000, using invoice numbers in field 70 and settling same-day in CAD through Lynx or in USD through the bank's correspondent network (e.g., RBC New York branch, BMO Harris, TD Bank N.A.). Layer 2 (high-value or contractually safeguarded purchases of MLCCs and resistors from Asian or European manufacturers): documentary collections (CAD/USD D/P at sight) or confirmed commercial letters of credit issued via SWIFT MT700 and amended via MT707 under UCP600. The bank requires supplier invoices, packing lists, shipment evidence, and, for letters of credit, presentation documents (commercial invoice, bill of lading, certificate of origin, inspection certificate) before honouring payment. CAD settlement above CAD 25,000,000 uses Lynx directly; practical corporate treasury use relies on SWIFT MT103. A CAD/USD dual-currency operating account is recommended to minimise conversion leakage and to preserve credit-line capacity.Outright (vanilla) FX forward contracts (CAD/USD, CAD/EUR, CAD/CNH) with physical settlement of the principal amounts; fully permitted for Canadian corporate treasuries.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) on restricted or non-convertible currencies (e.g., CNY, INR, KRW) where settlement is in CAD or USD without conversion of the underlying currency; permitted where the bank or dealing counterparty offers the product.Cross-currency interest-rate or principal swaps (CIRS) for long-term intercompany loan and trade-debt hedging between CAD and USD, documented under an ISDA 2002 or Canadian master agreement.Plain vanilla FX options and zero-cost collars (risk reversals or seagulls) to cap import-cost exposure while preserving upside; permitted under Canadian securities laws when executed with a registered Canadian bank or investment dealer.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium, conditional. Canada does not recognise a standalone electronic-money-institution (EMI) licence; deposit-taking is reserved for chartered banks under the Bank Act (Canada). Offshore digital EMIs and payment processors (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex) may onboard the Canadian federal corporation remotely for payment initiation, corporate cards, or vendor disbursements, but they operate under money-services-business (MSB) registration with FINTRAC and provincial money-services regulation. They cannot provide a true Canadian clearing account with a Lynx settlement membership, a Canadian bank number for CAD direct deposits, or CDIC-insured deposits. For the ISIC 4652 passive-components distribution model, EMIs may be viable as a secondary low-value disbursement rail for small suppliers or reimbursements, but they are not a lawful substitute for the primary Schedule I chartered-bank treasury account, particularly for commercial letters of credit, USD wire concentration, and revolving credit facilities.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% net federal Part I corporate income tax rate (after provincial abatement and general rate reduction) on taxable income earned by a general business corporation in Canada; provincial corporate tax is imposed separately on top of this federal rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless reduced under an applicable tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not maintain a statutory monetary safe-harbour threshold for transfer-pricing documentation. Form T106 must be filed where reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions aggregate to more than CAD 1,000,000 in the taxation year; contemporaneous local-file documentation and master-file information should nevertheless be prepared for all material related-party transactions because CRA may request it at any time.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations like those in offshore holding-company regimes. The CRA applies substantive commercial-activity analysis, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR), and beneficial-ownership doctrines to disregard arrangements lacking real business purpose. Passive electronic inventory distribution housed in a shell with no Canadian employees, operational premises, or inventory management control will be closely examined for treaty abuse and may be reassessed as a conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful False Statements

Statutory sanction: 50% gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2); on conviction under subsection 239(1), fines of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years on indictment.

Director personal liability: Corporate officers and directors who are the directing mind of the evasion may be prosecuted as principals or parties; civil restitution and asset confiscation orders may be made against them personally, and severe cases involving flight risk support travel bans.
Transfer Pricing Misalignment

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment of Canadian taxable income, consequential foreign-exchange and expense adjustments, and a transfer-pricing penalty under subsection 247(4) equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment threshold is met.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve fabricated transfer-price invoices or participate in a false documentation scheme may be liable as participants in gross negligence; CRA can seek civil judgment against individuals who direct the under-reporting of Canadian income.
Profit Repatriation WHT Evasion / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, reassessment to the full 25% statutory withholding tax, daily instalment interest, and gross-negligence penalties; back-to-back financing or untouched passive shell entities will attract GAAR as well.

Director personal liability: Directors who execute the conduit arrangement may be assessed for knowing participation in false returns; civil penalties attach to the corporation, while executives are exposed to liability through the offence provisions of the Income Tax Act and possible extradition on tax fraud.
Trade-Based Money Laundering / False Invoicing

Statutory sanction: Offences under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code money-laundering provisions can result in imprisonment for up to fourteen years; the Canada Border Services Agency may impose administrative monetary penalties and forfeiture of involved goods.

Director personal liability: Executives who control invoice creation and trade settlements can be charged personally for laundering proceeds, subjected to asset restraint and forfeiture, prohibited from travel by criminal-justice orders, and exposed to director liability for participation in a false commercial paper trail.
