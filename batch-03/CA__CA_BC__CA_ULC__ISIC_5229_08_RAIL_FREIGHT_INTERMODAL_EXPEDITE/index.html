Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST NETFILE and My Business Account portal; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for export documentation

E-government filing portal

For a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Company (BC ULC) owned by non-residents, the incorporating attorney files an Incorporation Application under the BC Business Corporations Act. Immediately thereafter, the ULC must apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Form RC1, online through the Business Registration Online (BRO) service, or by mail. The 9-digit BN is the statutory tax identification number for income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and import/export programme accounts. A GST/HST account is opened under the same BN by selecting the GST/HST programme; registration is required once taxable supply revenue exceeds the small supplier threshold or if the corporation elects voluntarily to claim input tax credits. Non-resident officers may sign the application; no Canadian residency or shareholding is required for a ULC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box regime for domestic tax purposes. A BC ULC remains a corporation resident in Canada and is subject to entity-level federal and provincial income tax. However, the 'Pass_Through_Eligible' status arises only under foreign (notably US) entity classification rules: a Canadian ULC may be treated as a partnership or disregarded entity under the US check-the-box election. In Canada, available elections include (i) the section 216(1) Income Tax Act election for non-resident owners to file returns on Canadian-source income net of expenses, (ii) the GST/HST closely-related corporation election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act to simplify intercompany transfers, and (iii) for a ULC with foreign owners, a formal documentation package under the Canada-US treaty to establish hybrid mismatch treatment (subject to BEAT/GILTI/CFC rules). No election can convert the ULC into a fully tax-transparent vehicle for Canadian income tax.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in any single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters; registration is mandatory once exceeded, and voluntary registration is available for persons making taxable supplies, including exporters and non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST/HST zero-rating applies to the supply of goods exported from Canada (Schedule VI, Part V, section 1) and to a supply of a transportation service for goods from a place in Canada to a place outside Canada (Schedule VI, Part VII, section 7). A combined intermodal rail container block train expedition service qualifies for zero-rating when the freight forwarding and transport services are directly attributable to exported goods that are physically removed from Canada, the supplier is a GST/HST registrant, the recipient is a non-resident (for offshore services), and documentary evidence of export accompanies the accounting records. For a transcontinental container block train, the zero-rating claim covers rail haulage within Canada and the downstream international carriage that moves the goods to a foreign destination. Ancillary services are zero-rated only if they are reasonably and proportionately related to the export transportation and billed as part of the same supply.

  • B13A Export Declaration (also filed via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for goods transported out of Canada)
  • Original or electronic Bill of Lading (rail waybill, intermodal through bill, or ocean bill of lading for port-gate transit)
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Carrier's proof of physical export (rail company certificate of exit, customs stamped waybill, or freight manifest)
  • Foreign-trade payment remittance evidence (wire transfer, letter of credit settlement, or bank advice demonstrating inward payment for the export)
  • Freight forwarder service contract and written documentation linking the Canadian rail expedition service to the international export movement

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing, or fiscal stamping requirement. The GST/HST invoicing rules under the Excise Tax Act require a registrant to issue a tax invoice containing: supplier name and BN, recipient name, date, a description of the supplied goods/services, the total amount, the GST paid/payable, and tax rate. Electronic invoices are valid if the electronic transaction is authentic and the source documentation is preserved. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through the CRA's GST/HST NETFILE, GST/HST TELEFILE (for certain returns), or via a third-party certified software. Export bills are reported to the CBSA through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) without a tax clearance step.

CRA and CBSA jointly audit export zero-rating claims. If the documentary evidence is incomplete or the goods are diverted into the Canadian domestic market, the claimed zero-rating is denied, and CRA assesses the GST (currently 5%) as tax not collected, plus remote interest at the prescribed rate and penalties under the Excise Tax Act for failure to collect or remit, and for making false statements. For gross negligence, the penalty is 25% of the understated tax; fraud can lead to criminal prosecution with imprisonment and fines. Retrospective clawback is not time-limited where the tax had been claimed on a fraudulent or materially misleading application, and the responsible officers, including directors of the ULC, can be personally liable for GST/HST amounts unremitted or improperly claimed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Current Invisible Operations and the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements. The Canadian dollar is freely convertible; there is no governmental approval, licensing, or economic-needs test for converting currency, opening foreign-currency accounts, or remitting profits. A BC unlimited liability corporation may remit dividends or repay shareholder loans without Bank of Canada or OSFI authorization. The only applicable constraint is Canadian income withholding under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, typically 25% on dividends paid to non-residents unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty, and such withholding operates as tax compliance, not exchange control. Treaty-relevant documentation includes Canada Revenue Agency forms, a valid Certificate of Compliance where applicable, and the ULC's corporate records substantiating the amount and source of the distribution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender regime. Exporters are not obliged to repatriate, hold, or convert export receipt proceeds into Canadian dollars, and there is no mandatory conversion ratio or prescribed timeline for bringing export receipts onshore. A BC ULC may retain export proceeds in foreign-denominated bank accounts, use them directly for outbound operating expenses, or convert them at negotiated commercial rates. The only reporting duty is indirect, through the reporting of international wire transfers by financial institutions to FINTRAC for amounts above CAD 10,000, and through Bank of Canada balance-of-payments surveys where directed.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating under the Bank of Canada Act and the Currency Act, with prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money-laundering supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign exchange administration agency or exchange control ministry.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium — offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise Business, Airwallex, and Currencycloud are feasible for ancillary treasury operations, particularly for making small-value cross-border disbursements to foreign rail operators and freight partners at competitive FX margins. However, an offshore EMI is not licensed as a Canadian Schedule I bank under the Bank Act, does not qualify for Canadian Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) coverage, and cannot serve as the sole or primary treasury account for a Canadian unlimited liability corporation. Where a foreign EMI provides payment services into Canada, it generally must register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, including transaction reporting and record keeping. For the BC ULC, the conservative structure is a Schedule I bank operating account as the treasury hub, supplemented by an EMI only for targeted outbound disbursements under a defined risk-control framework.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections for the BC ULC, including share capital and shareholder loan funding, should be settled through SWIFT MT103 transfers from the parent or investor bank to the ULC's Canadian Schedule I bank operating account. The wire narrative must clearly state: 'Share capital contribution in accordance with Board Resolution dated [date]', or for shareholder loans, 'Unsecured shareholder loan in accordance with loan agreement dated [date]'. Canada does not impose a statutory FX purpose code, but the remitter should use the receiving bank's prescribed purpose or narrative classification for 'direct investment in Canada' or 'capital contribution' to align with Bank of Canada balance-of-payments reporting. Verification procedures for the bank and the ULC should include confirmation of the incoming SWIFT gpi message, matching the remitter name and bank account to the executed share subscription or loan agreement, validating all corporate signatory authorities, and recording the entry in the ULC's capital register and internal ledger. Funds intended to be held for statutory or escrow purposes, such as regulatory deposits or documentary confirmation for trade obligations, may be held in a designated Capital Escrow Account at the same bank to preserve traceability and avoid commingling with operating cash flows.The BC ULC's outbound trade disbursements should use layered clearing rails: (1) domestic Canadian-dollar payments for intra-country rail, drayage, and terminal charges clear through the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and Real-Time Rail or direct clearing using SWIFT-standard MT103 payment instructions; (2) cross-border disbursements to non-Canadian rail carriers, intermodal operators, and expedited freight partners settle through SWIFT MT103 direct wires with SWIFT gpi tracking for real-time payment status; (3) large-scale or strategically committed international block-train and freight-forwarding purchase obligations may be structured through commercial letters of credit issued via SWIFT MT700/MT707, with documentary presentations against bills of lading, rail waybills, and certificate-of-shipment documentation; and (4) urgent or small-value trader fee payments can be aggregated and settled through approved EMI platforms operating under FINTRAC registration, but must be reconciled back to the primary bank account. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (15% federal general + 12% British Columbia general; a Canadian-controlled private company may claim small business rates, but a ULC controlled by non-residents is ineligible)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII for dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to non-residents; treaty may reduce rate to 15% generally and 5% for a company that owns at least 10% voting rights; a non-resident carrying on business in Canada via a branch instead faces Part XIV branch tax of 25% on after-tax branch profits not reinvested in Canadian property, subject to treaty reduction.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 must be filed on or before the day that is six months after the end of each fiscal period; instalments are due monthly or quarterly under ITA s.156 based on prior-year estimated taxes.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair-market value of related-party non-resident transactions in a taxation year triggers Schedule T106 filing and CRA's expectation of contemporaneous local file and master file under the 'reasonable efforts' penalty relief framework.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate an Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime; however, the CRA tests economic reality through GAAR (ITA s.245), treaty-benefit principal-purpose tests, and the common-law doctrine of sham; a ULC used in Eurasian rail intermodal freight must demonstrate genuine management, staff, operational premises, billing, risk-bearing, and decision making in BC, otherwise exemptive treaty claims can be collapsed and Part XIII withholding reinstated.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA re-prices the transaction; the s.247(4) penalty is 10% of the gross TP adjustment where the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts, and the net adjustment may also trigger interest and reassessment beyond the normal reassessment period if deliberate.

Director personal liability: Directors are not personally liable for TP penalties absent a signed false declaration; however, directors who sign false returns may face gross-negligence penalties and criminal exposure for willful evasion.
Shell Company / GAAR Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: GAAR re-characterization of the transaction removes the tax benefit; treaty protection may be denied under the principal-purpose test; Part XIII withholding of 25% is restored, with arrears interest and GAAR penalties (ITA s.245(4)).

Director personal liability: The directors who approved the sham structure can be held jointly and severally for unpaid withholding and assessed penalties where the structure involves a culpable false representation; criminal sham findings may expose them to imprisonment for common-law fraud.
Tax Evasion / False Return

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under ITA s.239(1) carries a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and up to two years' imprisonment; civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) is 50% of understated tax plus arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are primary participants and may be sentenced to imprisonment, ordered to pay civil restitution, and foreign-director travel may be constrained by an outstanding warrant or a Canada Border Services Agency flag.
Money Laundering / Structured Payments

Statutory sanction: Procedure under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code s.462.31 exposes the corporation to fines of up to CAD 500,000 per summary conviction or CAD 1,000,000 on indictment; laundering charges may carry up to 10 years' imprisonment; CFSA suspicious-transaction non-reporting penalties apply to those with senior management authority.

Director personal liability: Executives and senior officers who instruct or authorize split payments are criminally liable as parties to the offense and may be sentenced to imprisonment; they also face civil and regulatory orders prohibiting future directorship, seizure of personal benefit, and financial-documentary personal liability for CRA/BC-related assessments.
