Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility for both current and capital accounts. Profit remittances to non-resident shareholders are unrestricted and require no prior regulatory approval, subject only to standard corporate tax filings and AML reporting.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds required. Receipts are settled directly at prevailing market exchange rates with no statutory timeline or ratio obligations imposed by the Bank of Canada.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. A BC ULC is a Canadian resident corporation. Treaty relief may reduce the rate: under the Canada-US treaty, a US corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of the voting shares qualifies for a 5% rate; portfolio dividends generally qualify for 15%. Under many other Canadian tax treaties (UK, Netherlands, Luxembourg, Germany), the rate is 5% for a 10% corporate shareholder and 15% for portfolio investments.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the end of the corporation's fiscal year. For a BC ULC with a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year. Quarterly instalments are due on the last day of each quarter, and the final balance of tax owing is due no later than three months after the fiscal year-end for a CCPC, or two months after the fiscal year-end for a non-CCPC, including a ULC that is not a CCPC. A non-CCPC ULC must pay its final balance by the two-month deadline regardless of the six-month filing deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 250,000: under Canadian transfer-pricing rules (sections 247 and 233.1), a taxpayer must prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's length transactions with non-residents exceeding CAD 250,000 in a fiscal year. The Master File and Local File requirements apply. Country-by-Country Report requirement applies for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750 million. A BC ULC engaged in related-party offshore vessel chartering, cross-border management fees, or financing must comply at or above the CAD 250,000 threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a separate economic substance regulation statute, but the doctrine is embedded in the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under subsection 245(2), which denies a tax benefit from a transaction that is an avoidance transaction and that results directly or indirectly in a misuse of the Income Tax Act. The CRA also applies transfer-pricing rules and the OECD principal purpose test under the MLI for treaty benefits. For a BC ULC leasing offshore supply vessels, the CRA will assess whether the ULC has a real commercial substance: actual employees in BC, operational management, vessel registration and ownership, bank accounts, and meaningful decision-making. If the ULC is a pure shell or conduit without substance, the CRA may disregard it as a sham or apply GAAR to deny treaty benefits and assess the non-resident shareholder directly. The ULC election itself must not be used to artificially avoid tax; the economic substance of the chartering business is key to sustaining the Canadian tax treatment of the ULC.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, tax evasion is an indictable offence punishable by a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for up to five years. On summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% and up to two years imprisonment. Civil penalties under section 163(2) include 50% of the understated tax plus interest. Failure to withhold under subsection 215(1) makes the ULC liable for the full amount of unwithheld tax plus interest and penalties, even if the non-resident shareholder later pays the tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, if they knowingly directed or allowed the failure. Directors can be personally assessed without leave of court, even while the corporation is bankrupt. Civil restitution may include recovery of discretionary dividends paid to directors while tax debt remained unpaid. Courts can issue travel bans under the Criminal Code (e.g., bail conditions or a s. 515(10) order) for directors charged with tax evasion. Imprisonment up to five years for tax evasion, and up to 14 years for fraud under Criminal Code section 380. Restitution, vessel forfeiture, and director disqualification can be ordered.
Transfer Pricing Misconduct / Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3), the CRA may adjust taxable income to reflect arm's length terms and impose a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment amount where the adjustment exceeds the greater of 10% of gross revenue or CAD 5 million, or where documentation requirements are not met. Documentation penalties under sections 233.1-233.3: CAD 1,000 per month for failure to provide documentation, maximum CAD 100,000. For failure to report a foreign affiliate's chartering income, CAD 2,500 per month up to CAD 25,000. Gross negligence penalties under section 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax may also apply, plus compounded daily interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not personally liable for a corporation's transfer-pricing adjustments unless they directed or participated in creating false documentation or abetting tax evasion. If so, they may face criminal fraud or conspiracy charges. Directors can be personally assessed if the corporation is wound up without paying, under the escape assessment rule. Under Canadian corporate law, a director who signs a false tax return is subject to a separate offence under section 238 (fine of CAD 2,000 to CAD 25,000 and/or six months imprisonment). Aiding tax evasion under section 239(1)(d) can result in fines of CAD 10,000 to CAD 50,000 and/or up to five years imprisonment. Contempt of court for failure to produce transfer-pricing documentation can lead to imprisonment.
Shell Company / ULC Abuse and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The CRA can apply the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under subsection 245(2) to deny the tax benefit, re-imposing full 25% withholding tax plus interest and a 50% gross negligence penalty. The CRA may also disregard the ULC as a sham and assess the non-resident shareholder directly on the charter income. Under the MLI principal purpose test, treaty benefits are denied where one of the principal purposes of the arrangement is to obtain treaty relief. The Canada-US Limitation on Benefits clause is strict and requires certain tests (e.g., publicly traded, tax-exempt, derivative benefits, active business or headquarters/profit test). Violations can trigger Part XV.1 information penalties of CAD 1,000 per foreign financial account per month for unreported foreign interests, and CRA's Offshore Tax Initiative may include asset freezes.

Director personal liability: Directors of MSBs are personally liable for corporate non-compliance only if they knowingly consented or were willfully blind to the money laundering activity. Criminal liability under PCMLTFA s. 74 and Criminal Code s. 462.31 can lead to imprisonment up to ten years. Directors' personal assets are subject to forfeiture and confiscation as proceeds of crime. Failure to comply with FINTRAC orders can result in personal contempt findings and imprisonment. Courts may also impose travel bans and surrender of passports in serious maritime money laundering cases. The corporate veil is pierced where the ULC is a sham used to launder charter funds, making shareholders personally liable to victims and the state.
