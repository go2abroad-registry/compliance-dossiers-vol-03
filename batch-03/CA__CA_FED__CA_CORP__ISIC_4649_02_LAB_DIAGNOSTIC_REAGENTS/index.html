Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a general check-the-box regime comparable to the U.S. entity classification election. Under federal tax law, a federal or provincial business corporation (Inc./Corp.) is a separate taxable entity subject to Part I corporate income tax and is not eligible for pass-through treatment. Available elections include: (i) GST/HST electoral choice under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act to net amounts between closely related corporations; (ii) an election under section 21 of the Excise Tax Act to treat the supply of certain financial services as taxable, thereby allowing input tax credit recovery on related inputs; (iii) a joint election for nil consideration and deemed consideration between resident individuals and corporations in respect of certain GST/HST non-arm's length supplies; and (iv) for hybrid mismatch purposes, Canada applies the Income Tax Act section 18.2 and 18.3 anti-hybrid rules (OECD BEPS Action 2), but there is no elective hybrid mismatch elimination mechanism; any such election must be substantiated under the relevant treaty residence rules, specifically Article IV of the Canada-U.S. or Canada-other tax treaties, to avoid deduction/non-inclusion mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 per calendar quarter and over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters for taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports and certain financial supplies) made in Canada by a non-resident carrying on a business in Canada; once the threshold is exceeded, registration is mandatory. There is no threshold for non-residents making taxable supplies in Canada without carrying on a business in Canada or for persons required to collect GST/HST on imports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under the Excise Tax Act (ETA) to supplies of goods exported by the supplier or by the purchaser (Section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI), and to certain supplies of services performed for non-residents where the primary benefit is outside Canada. A wholesale distributor of laboratory diagnostic reagents may zero-rate the supply if: (i) the goods are shipped by the supplier directly to a destination outside Canada; or (ii) the goods are delivered to a common carrier or other intermediary for export and the supplier retains documentary proof of export. Diagnostic goods under Schedule VI (e.g., medical devices, drugs, and diagnostics) may be zero-rated domestically, but for export zero-rating the supplier must establish that the goods were exported. Offshore services supplied to a non-resident can be zero-rated under section 9 of Part V of Schedule VI if the service is not supplied to another person while in Canada and the primary objective of the service is not to benefit the recipient while in Canada.

  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing export from Canada to the foreign consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire/TT payment evidence showing settlement in foreign currency and foreign-source counterparty disbursement

Canada has no national real-time e-invoicing clearance or digital tax stamp mandate for wholesale distribution of IVD reagents. The CRA requires registrants to issue prescribed GST/HST information in an invoice or receipt (e.g., vendor name, BN/RT account, invoice date, description, tax rates, and total tax) before the tax becomes payable. Electronic invoices are permitted if the CRA can access and read them in an electronic format; there is no mandatory pre-clearance API clearance. For imports of diagnostic reagents, the CBSA (Canada Border Services Agency) requires electronic customs declarations through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal, including a valid Business Number import account (RM), and duties/GST are assessed at the border. Province-specific HST rates apply in participating provinces (Ontario 13%, Nova Scotia 15%, New Brunswick 15%, Newfoundland and Labrador 15%, Prince Edward Island 15%); Quebec applies its own QST alongside GST, but federal zero-rating of exports still applies.

Under the Excise Tax Act, a registrant claiming zero-rating must substantiate export within the CRA's standard 4-year reassessment period (6 years in cases of misrepresentation or neglect). Failure to maintain documentary proof results in the CRA assessing uncollected GST/HST on the supply at 5% (or applicable HST rate) plus interest and penalties. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims are subject to gross negligence penalties under section 285.1 of the ETA, up to 25% of the net tax amount assessed, plus wilful evasion penalties under section 239 (fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years). Retrospective clawbacks can be made for every GST/HST return period affected, and the taxpayer also bears the full GST/HST amount in addition to the original transaction amount, reflecting tax in arrears. The CRA's International and Large Business Audit Directorate may perform e-audits, exchange information via the OECD and bilateral tax treaties, and request per-export documentation including banking records; failure to comply triggers recourse to a formal audit demand under section 289 of the ETA and subsequent collection under the Federal Crown's civil enforcement powers.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no foreign-exchange controls, no prior approval requirements for capital inflows or outflows, and no restrictions on profit or dividend repatriation, except for compliance with the Income Tax Act's Part XIII withholding tax on distributions to non-residents (statutory rate 25%, generally reduced under applicable tax treaties). Capital repayment upon liquidation is not subject to withholding tax. All cross-border transactions are subject to FINTRAC reporting thresholds and sanctions screening, but no exchange-control permit regime applies. Operating in a functional currency other than CAD is permitted for qualifying corporations under the Income Tax Act's functional-currency reporting election; unrealized foreign-exchange gains and losses are computed in accordance with Canadian GAAP/IFRS.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement. Export receipts denominated in any foreign currency may be retained in offshore accounts, converted to CAD, or transferred into domestic accounts at the exporter's discretion. No mandatory conversion ratio or repatriation timeline exists. For tax purposes, export revenues must be reported in CAD using the applicable exchange rate under the Canada Revenue Agency administrative rules; any delay in conversion does not trigger a deemed disposition. However, banks may require documentary evidence of export transactions for anti-money-laundering compliance when funds are received via foreign correspondent wires.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) serves as the monetary authority, while foreign exchange administration is not vested in a single dedicated exchange-control bureau; Canada abolished exchange controls in 1951 and operates a free-floating currency managed under the Bank of Canada's monetary policy framework. Banking and financial market supervision is undertaken by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and anti-money-laundering/counter-terrorist financing supervision is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low to Medium. Cross-border digital electronic-money institutions and foreign fintech accelerators do not have a standalone EMI licence category in Canada. A foreign digital bank or e-money service that wishes to clear CAD payments must either obtain a Schedule I or Schedule II bank charter under the Bank Act, operate as a provincially regulated credit union, or register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC. An unregistered offshore EMI cannot open Canadian settlement accounts nor obtain direct access to Payments Canada's clearing and settlement systems. FINTRAC-registered international MSBs can provide limited payment and remittance services, but cannot accept deposits or provide foreign-exchange hedging. For a wholesale distributor of diagnostic reagents, a regulated tier-1 commercial bank remains the only fully compliant clearing and liquidity connection.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder-loan capital is routed via SWIFT MT103 or SWIFT gpi to the Canadian corporate operating account at a tier-1 commercial bank. The remitter's field 50K must identify the legal name of the foreign parent or investor; the beneficiary field 59 must show the exact account name, account number, and institution/transit number of the Canadian entity. Field 70 (Remittance Information) must state the underlying purpose as 'Capital Contribution' or 'Director's Loan Injection', together with a reference to the relevant board resolution. The bank may request a copy of the capital injection resolution, a shareholder register, and a source-of-funds statement for any single transaction exceeding CAD 10,000 to satisfy FINTRAC large-transaction reporting. For transactions exceeding CAD 1,000,000, the bank may apply heightened review and expected to provide a certificate of incumbency, evidence of the foreign corporate's good standing, and a hyperlink or reference to the Canadian company's active federal incorporation profile. Settlement may be by sight payment, deferred payment, or acceptance; all LC liabilities are reflected on the bank's risk management books and may require collateral or foreign-exchange hedging. A foreign digital bank or e-money service that wishes to clear CAD payments must either obtain a Schedule I or Schedule II bank charter under the Bank Act, operate as a provincially regulated credit union, or register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC. An unregistered offshore EMI cannot open Canadian settlement accounts nor obtain direct access to Payments Canada's clearing and settlement systems. FINTRAC-registered international MSBs can provide limited payment and remittance services, but cannot accept deposits or provide foreign-exchange hedging. For a wholesale distributor of diagnostic reagents, a regulated tier-1 commercial bank remains the only fully compliant clearing and liquidity connection.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate rate under Part I of the Income Tax Act (R.S.C. 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.)); CCPC active business income up to CAD 500,000 is taxed at 9% federally; provincial corporate income tax applies in addition.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the ITA on dividends paid by a resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner; reduced to 5% for a US corporate shareholder holding 10% or more of the voting stock under the Canada-US tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File (Form T106/TP disclosures): mandatory when non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate in the taxation year. Master File / CbC report: mandatory for MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 1,000,000,000 in the preceding fiscal year (with CbC reporting under s.247).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not enact separate ESR legislation; however, economic substance is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245), the central-management-and-control test, the 'permanent establishment' doctrine, and the Limitation-on-Benefits clause in the Canada-US treaty (5th Protocol, Art. XXIX A). A Canadian distributor must have real board decisions, staff, inventory, premises, and banking in Canada; a non-resident distributor must demonstrate the same in its residence state before treaty benefits will be granted.
