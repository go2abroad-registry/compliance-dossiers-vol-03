Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA/ARC), the federal tax administrator. Compliance portal: CRA My Business Account via https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Business Registration Online (BRO) service; Corporations Canada (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada) for federal incorporation and corporate registry filings.

E-government filing portal

A federally incorporated Canadian Business Corporation automatically obtains its Business Number (BN) upon incorporation with Corporations Canada; the BN serves as the federal taxpayer identification number for the T2 corporate income tax return, GST/HST program account, payroll deductions, and customs declarations. A non-resident foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must procure a BN by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) by mail or fax, or by online enrolment through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) service. The GST/HST program account is identified by the two-letter suffix 'RT' appended to the 9-digit BN. GST/HST registration is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in the current or immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (the small-supplier threshold under Excise Tax Act subsection 148(1)), or where the non-resident makes taxable supplies in Canada and does not qualify for the small-supplier exception; voluntary registration is available to exporters of zero-rated goods to recover input tax credits. The same BN is also used for customs export reporting and excise purposes. A foreign-owned Canadian corporation must additionally maintain CBCA good standing, file an annual return with Corporations Canada, and obtain extra-provincial registration in any province where it carries on business.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation - A standard Business Corporation (Inc./Corp., ELF code 8615) is a separate statutory taxpayer under the federal Income Tax Act (ITA). It files a T2 Corporate Income Tax Return and pays federal corporate income tax at a 15% headline rate on taxable income earned in Canada, plus provincial/territorial corporate allocation where a permanent establishment exists. The corporation is not a pass-through vehicle: income, losses, and credits are realized and recognized at the entity level; distributions are taxed again as dividends in the hands of shareholders. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - A standard Business Corporation (Inc./Corp., ELF code 8615) is a separate statutory taxpayer under the federal Income Tax Act (ITA). It files a T2 Corporate Income Tax Return and pays federal corporate income tax at a 15% headline rate on taxable income earned in Canada, plus provincial/territorial corporate allocation where a permanent establishment exists. The corporation is not a pass-through vehicle: income, losses, and credits are realized and recognized at the entity level; distributions are taxed again as dividends in the hands of shareholders. This classification is consistent with the limited-liability corporate structure and the non-resident ownership allowance described in the composite key.

Available election forms

Canada has no 'check-the-box' or pass-through entity election comparable to the US LLC/Subchapter S regime. Material federal elections available to a cross-border export corporation include: (i) the election under Excise Tax Act subsection 156(1) to treat taxable supplies between closely related corporations as made for nil consideration in respect of GST/HST; (ii) the ITA subsection 85(1) rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of property to a newly formed corporation; (iii) the ITA subsection 22(1) election covering the transfer of trade accounts receivable; (iv) the ITA section 216 election permitting a non-resident to file a Part I tax return on Canadian-source property income in lieu of Part XIII withholding; (v) the ITA subsection 217(1) election for non-residents on tuition and scholarship income; (vi) hybrid-mismatch ordering rules and related elections under ITA sections 18.2 through 18.7 and the anti-hybrid regulations, which apply where an instrument or entity is classified inconsistently by Canada and a foreign jurisdiction; and (vii) capital cost allowance (CCA) elections on depreciable assets used in the export supply chain. All such elections must be made in prescribed form within statutory deadlines to be valid.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (small-supplier threshold under ETA subsection 148(1)). Registration becomes compulsory in the calendar quarter in which the threshold is exceeded. The test applies to worldwide taxable supplies of the supplier and its associates; non-residents making taxable supplies in Canada must register unless a specific small-supplier exception applies. Exporters of zero-rated goods may voluntarily register to claim full input tax credits (ITCs) on GST/HST paid on purchases and operating costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of dried goji berries (HS heading 0813.40) and dried herbal botanicals (e.g., HS heading 1211) qualify for zero-rating under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V (section 1) when the supplier sells or delivers the goods to a non-resident purchaser, the goods are exported by or on behalf of the supplier, and title and risk of loss pass to the foreign buyer. Services consumed wholly outside Canada by a non-resident, such as cross-border sourcing, quality control, and logistics management, are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V (section 7). Because botanical supplement products may fall outside the domestic basic-grocery zero-rating in Schedule VI, Part III, the export zero-rating route is the authoritative position for this ISIC scope. Registered exporters simultaneously claim ITCs on GST/HST paid on inputs (freight, warehousing, broker fees, testing) in the same GST/HST return; where ITCs exceed output tax, CRA issues a cash refund. Substantiation documentation must be retained for at least six years, and a defective export claim is recharacterized as a taxable supply at 5% GST or 13-15% HST on the full invoice value.

  • Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) electronic export declaration (formerly Form B13A) filed with the Canada Border Services Agency before export, evidencing the date, port of exit, HS classification, value, and exporter identification
  • Commercial invoice issued in English or French stating Incoterms, HS code (e.g., 0813.40 for dried goji berries), quantity, unit weight, aggregate value, and the non-resident consignee
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (air) showing the foreign port of discharge and an overseas consignee outside Canada
  • Proof-of-export evidence, including carrier cargo control documents, container freight station receipts, customs broker release records, or a Form E certificate of export where required by CRA
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT wire transfer confirmation or foreign exchange record evidencing remittance of the export proceeds to the Canadian exporter's bank account
  • Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) phytosanitary certificate and organic certification documentation where required by the destination country for plant materials or for 'organic' marketing claims
  • Executed sales contract and insurance certificate evidencing transfer of title, risk of loss, payment terms, and Incoterms allocation between the Canadian seller and foreign buyer

Canada does not impose a federal real-time fiscal clearance, API invoice-validation, or digital-stamping regime for GST/HST. E-invoicing is voluntary at the federal level; CRA accepts PEPPOL BIS Billing 3.0, EDI, and standard electronic invoices provided the registrant maintains complete and legible books and records under ETA section 286 for a minimum of six years. Quebec has enacted a mandatory electronic invoicing and e-reporting regime under Loi 20, with phased implementation from January 2026 through December 2028 for large enterprises; however, at the federal jurisdiction level (CA_FED), no statutory pre-clearance or digital certification of tax documents is required. Exporters must issue a valid GST/HST invoice identified by their BN and retain audit-ready records.

CRA audits zero-rated export claims by verifying physical export, reasonable export time, foreign consignee status, title transfer, and payment evidence. Failure to substantiate the export triggers reassessment at the applicable GST/HST rate (5% federal or 13-15% HST) on gross invoice value, accrual of default interest at the CRA prescribed rate (compound daily), and civil penalties under ETA sections 280 through 285, including the gross negligence penalty under ETA subsection 285.1 of 25% of the tax at risk where a false statement or omission is made knowingly. Criminal tax evasion under ETA section 327 is punishable on summary conviction by fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment for up to two years, or both. Clawback may extend to denied input tax credits on related inputs, transfer-pricing reassessments under ITA Part XVI.1, and application of the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA section 245) where export proceeds remain outstanding for an unusually long period or the purchaser is non-arm's length. Although the federal statute does not prescribe an express repatriation deadline, CRA applies a 180-day evidentiary benchmark for foreign currency receipts; unexplained overdues invite enhanced audit scrutiny. Exporters using a non-resident exporter/in-transit procedure through a freight forwarder may also face administrative monetary penalties under the Customs Act for inaccurate export declarations.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is fully convertible on both current and capital account; no regulatory approval, license, or central bank permit is required for capital inflows, outbound investment, trade payments, or profit repatriation. Non-resident dividend remittance is subject to Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) withholding at the statutory 25%, treaty-reduced (e.g., 10-15% under the Canada-China Income Tax Agreement; 5-15% under the Canada-US Treaty). Export sales of tangible dried goji berries and herbal botanicals are zero-rated for GST/HST under section 22 of the Excise Tax Act when the goods are physically exported, with the B/L, customs entry, and freight documents retained as proof.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for export receipts. Exporters may lawfully retain USD, CNH, or other foreign currency in foreign deposit accounts. Compliance obligations are limited to: (1) Form T1135 (Foreign Income Verification Statement) if specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000 aggregate cost at any time in the taxation year; (2) recognition of realized FX gains and losses in Canadian-dollar taxable income; and (3) full disclosure of foreign bank accounts in the corporate financial statements audited under ASPE or IFRS.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) issuing the free-floating Canadian dollar; FX control administration is functionally dispersed between the Minister of Finance (Exchange Fund Account), OSFI (prudential FX position risk limits for federally regulated banks), and FINTRAC (AML/CFT surveillance of cross-border wire transfers). Canada repealed its Exchange Control Act in 1951; no exchange-control statute exists today.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium feasibility for payment facilitation only, with material legal constraints. Foreign digital EMIs (Wise Platform, Airwallex, Payoneer, Corpay) operating in Canada must register as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and, where providing retail payment services, must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). EMIs are not Schedule I or Schedule II banks; balances held with them are not CDIC-insured, and correspondent banks frequently de-risk MSB-driven clearing flows. Recommended posture: use EMIs only as a supplementary rail for low-value wholesale receivables, while maintaining the primary operating and FX settlement accounts at a Schedule I bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate on taxable income, before provincial/territorial abatement and general rate reductions; combined federal-provincial general rate approximately 26% to 31% depending on province of deemed permanent establishment. For the purpose of this federal dossier, the federal statutory headline rate is 15%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory Canadian withholding tax of 25% on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to non-resident shareholders, unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the end of the corporation's fiscal period (e.g., a December 31 year-end must be filed by June 30 of the following calendar year). Tax balances are due no later than two months after fiscal year-end for most corporations, or three months for Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) with a specified business limit, unless the corporation is claiming the enhanced SBD or is a specified cooperative. Instalment payments are due monthly or quarterly as prescribed under subsection 156(1) and 157(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (a Local File and an Annual Update) is required where the total value of non-arm's length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a fiscal period for services or fees; for goods, inventory, and tangible property transactions the threshold is CAD 5,000,000; for loans and advances the threshold is CAD 5,000,000. A combined dollar threshold for all covered transactions is CAD 5,000,000 or CAD 10,000,000 depending on the CRA administrative guidance; in all cases, Schedule 125 seeks disclosure regardless of dollar value. CRA also requires a Master File under the OECD-aligned rules for multinational enterprises (MNE) with group revenue exceeding CAD 750 million (effective for fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2023, through updated country-by-country reporting rules).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone general Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) statute unique to financial jurisdictions, but the GAAR (general anti-avoidance rule under subsection 245(2)) and the transfer pricing rule under subsection 247(2) impose a functional economic substance requirement: an arrangement must not be structured primarily to avoid tax without real commercial substance. For cross-border wholesale and procurement in the herbal botanicals sector, the CRA will scrutinize the location of purchase contracts, inventory risk, quality assurance functions, and decision-making authority to ensure the Canadian entity has a meaningful operational presence, employees, and premises. In addition, CRA interpretations on circular transactions and conduit entities may deny treaty benefits under a substance-over-form analysis and OECD Base Erosion Profit Shifting (BEPS) action 6 rules. The entity must demonstrate that routine procurement, logistics, and storage functions are performed in Canada by adequate staff, rather than being a low-margin agent for a foreign principal.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statement Filed with CRA

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: fine of up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment of up to five years, plus civil penalties under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of tax evaded and full restitution of unpaid tax and gross negligence penalties; CRA may seize tax refunds, garnish bank accounts, and conduct criminal investigations with the RCMP and the Integrated Tax Enforcement Unit. Under the Criminal Code (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-46), money laundering and proceeds of crime charges may add additional imprisonment up to ten years.

Director personal liability: Piercing the corporate veil under the Canada Business Corporations Act and common law: misrepresentations or fraudulent conduct by directors allow civil restitution against the estate of the company, and criminal liability may attach personally. CRA has the power to charge directors with wilful blindness and personal liability for the 50% penalty; travel bans may be imposed on Canadian resident directors when under investigation for tax evasion, and extradition treaties may be invoked for cross-border tax fraud. A director who knew or ought to have known of false filings may be personally assessed for the corporation's taxes under subsection 227.1(1) for unremitted payroll and GST/HST amounts, up to full unpaid liabilities with no limitation period for fraud.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Penalty for Contemporaneous Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3): CAD 1,000 per month for each complete month the documentation is insufficient or not furnished, capped at CAD 24,000 per year; no reduction of tax is allowed if the penalty applies; CRA may re-compute the arm's length price and impose consequential tax interest and 10% or 5% penalties for large corporations. In addition, penalties under section 162 for failure to file Form T106 or T1134 apply, and foreign reporting penalties for non-resident affiliate shares start at CAD 25,000 for assets above CAD 100,000.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's failure to withhold and remit Part XIII tax on cross-border payments under subsection 227.1(1) if they participated in the decision to underwithhold or were negligent in supervising the finance function; personal assessment for transfer pricing penalties may arise only where the taxpayer is a specified person; however, criminal exposure for conspiracy to evade tax may attach to directors who sign a misleading schedule.
Shell Company / Circular Transaction and Piercing Corporate Veil (Conduit Arrangement)

Statutory sanction: CRA may disregard the shell and assess the foreign parent directly under the Canadian permanent establishment doctrine (Article 5 of the Canada-U.S. treaty and section 253 of the Income Tax Act). Taxable income is re-attributed to the beneficial owner, and the section 212.1 or subsection 84.1 rules may treat a dividend or deemed dividend with full Part XIII withholding of 25% on the entire amount; interest deductibility denied under subsection 247(2) and GAAR, plus a 10% penalty and daily interest; an entity whose core activity is merely holding assets and collecting flows is required to file a T2 return under subsection 150(1) even if it purports to be inactive; failure can trigger a failure-to-file penalty plus gross negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors of the shell may be personally liable for taxes withheld from non-resident shareholders and not remitted under subsection 227.1; the corporate veil will be pierced where the director knowingly creates fraudulent circular transactions, leading to personal seizure of personal bank accounts and real estate; travel bans and restraining orders may issue against directors under the financial investigation provisions of the Criminal Code, and up to 14 years of imprisonment can be imposed for laundering proceeds of tax evasion.
AML/CFT Red Flags for Bulk Cross-Border Wire Transfers (FINTRAC Reporting)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for non-compliance with reporting and record-keeping obligations; if a financial institution files a Terrorist Property Report, the CRA may instigate a full audit; credible allegations of money laundering trigger freezing orders and forfeiture proceedings under the Criminal Code, and penalties for each failure to report a suspicious transaction or large cash transaction can reach CAD 100,000 per violation; criminal money-laundering penalities are up to CAD 500,000 and/or ten years in prison for an indictable offence.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and compliance officers are personally subject to FINTRAC penalties where they knowingly or negligently cause the entity's failure to report; personal travel bans may attach to investigations related to the transfer of alleged proceeds, and court orders may impose civil forfeiture against personal property; the corporate veil is pierced under treaty and common law where the shell is used for a fraud on creditors, lenders, or tax authorities.
