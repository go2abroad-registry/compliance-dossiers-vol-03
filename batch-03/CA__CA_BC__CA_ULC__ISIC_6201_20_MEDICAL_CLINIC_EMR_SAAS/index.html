Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); corporate income tax and GST/HST registration portal: My Business Account (CRA) and the Business Registration Online (BRO) service

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a BC Unlimited Liability Company (ULC) must first complete provincial incorporation under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act, then obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through the CRA's Business Registration Online portal. The BN is assigned with program accounts including RT (GST/HST), RC (payroll), RP (corporation income tax), and RZ (import/export). For registration, the ULC must provide its incorporation number, registered office address in BC, directors' information, and its estimated annual worldwide gross revenue to determine whether mandatory GST/HST registration applies. Non-resident directors and shareholders are permitted; however, the ULC must designate a representative for service of notice if no resident director exists. If the ULC is treated as a disregarded entity for U.S. tax purposes and is a 'listed financial institution' or carries on a commercial activity in Canada, additional CRA program account applications and a Form RC459 (Canada-United States Enhanced Tax Information Exchange Agreement) filing may be required. The CRA will issue a BN confirmation letter (Form RC1 request or online confirmation) once registration is complete.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation for Canadian federal and provincial income tax purposes, with a Pass_Through_Eligible overlay: a BC ULC is generally a corporation for Canadian Income Tax Act purposes and pays corporate income tax at the federal and provincial (BC) rates on taxable income. However, the ULC's unlimited liability attribute permits the sole shareholder (often a U.S. parent corporation) to elect under IRC § 1504(d) or check-the-box regulations to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity for U.S. federal tax purposes. Within Canada, the ULC remains a separate taxpayer and cannot simply flow income to shareholders unless it elects to be a 'Canadian partnership' through a formal reorganization or carries on business as a specified 'taxable Canadian corporation' under the applicable anti-hybrid rules. For indirect tax purposes, the ULC is a 'person' that must account for GST/HST under Division V of Part IX of the Excise Tax Act; there is no pass-through for GST/HST except in a qualifying joint venture election or where the ULC acts as agent for a non-resident principal. — A foreign entity establishing a BC Unlimited Liability Company (ULC) must first complete provincial incorporation under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act, then obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through the CRA's Business Registration Online portal. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation for Canadian federal and provincial income tax purposes, with a Pass_Through_Eligible overlay: a BC ULC is generally a corporation for Canadian Income Tax Act purposes and pays corporate income tax at the federal and provincial (BC) rates on taxable income. However, the ULC's unlimited liability attribute permits the sole shareholder (often a U.S. parent corporation) to elect under IRC § 1504(d) or check-the-box regulations to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity for U.S. federal tax purposes. Within Canada, the ULC remains a separate taxpayer and cannot simply flow income to shareholders unless it elects to be a 'Canadian partnership' through a formal reorganization or carries on business as a specified 'taxable Canadian corporation' under the applicable anti-hybrid rules. For indirect tax purposes, the ULC is a 'person' that must account for GST/HST under Division V of Part IX of the Excise Tax Act; there is no pass-through for GST/HST except in a qualifying joint venture election or where the ULC acts as agent for a non-resident principal.

Available election forms

(1) Section 216 election under the Income Tax Act for a non-resident person (not a ULC itself) to file a return on Canadian-source business income net of expenses; (2) Check-the-box / disregarded entity election for U.S. federal tax purposes under IRC § 301.7701-3(d), which does not alter Canadian entity classification but may require filing Form 8832 and a Canadian T2 with a treaty-based disclosure; (3) BC ULC may elect to be a 'Canadian partnership' only if it has dissolved as a corporation or is otherwise not a person under the Canada Business Corporations Act analogue; (4) GST/HST joint venture election under Excise Tax Act subsection 273(1) for the ULC to participate in a GST/HST-registered joint venture as a co-venturer; (5) BC 'small supplier' election under ETA subsection 240(3.1) allowing early GST/HST registration before the $30,000 threshold is exceeded; (6) IFRS/ASP accounting policy elections do not constitute statutory tax elections, but a ULC can file Form T2035 (direct designation) to deem certain income as taxable income; (7) Election under subsection 256(1) for loss consolidation within an associated corporate group to permit transfer of losses between ULC and related corporations.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated exports) over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration is required within 29 days. Non-resident persons that 'carry on business in Canada' and make taxable supplies must register regardless of the threshold. A ULC incorporated in Canada is a resident corporation and is required to register for GST/HST if it makes any taxable supply in Canada in the course of a commercial activity, even below the threshold, unless it qualifies as a 'small supplier' with gross revenue from worldwide taxable supplies not exceeding CAD 30,000 and no taxable supplies made in Canada unless the CRA considers it to be carrying on business in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A BC ULC supplying EMR clinical workflow software as a SaaS may claim a zero-rated export supply under section 23 of the Excise Tax Act (supply of an intangible personal property made to a non-resident person who is not a Canadian GST/HST registrant that does not acquire the property for supply to a Canadian resident, and the property is not to be used in Canada at the time of supply). Additionally, if the supply qualifies as a 'service' supplied to a non-resident (other than an individual present in Canada) and is not a supply of advice, consulting, or a service in respect of real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or a service the value of which is attributable primarily to services performed in Canada, the supply may be zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI. For cloud-hosted EMR software, the place of supply for exported services is determined under Part 4 of the New Harmonized Value-added Tax Framework Regulations; if all or substantially all of the service is performed outside Canada and the recipient is non-resident, the supply is considered made outside Canada and is not subject to GST/HST at all, or is zero-rated if technically made in Canada. The supplier must obtain 'documentary evidence' within two years of the return due date, including a signed written agreement, the recipient's home jurisdiction tax identifier or business number, and banking records showing payment received from a foreign financial institution.

  • Customs Export Declaration (for goods; for pure SaaS there is no physical export, so a CCI/AES equivalent is irrelevant; however, the CRA non-resident supply evidence bundle applies): executed cloud services agreement stating the non-resident recipient and primary place of use, commercial invoice or billing statement showing CAD or foreign currency consideration, BN/GST/HST registration confirmation, the recipient's foreign GST/VAT registration certificate or written declaration of non-resident status, proof of offshore payment such as a SWIFT MT103 or Stripe payout record showing the funds originated from a foreign bank account, Form RC446 (GST/HST and QST: Registration of Non-resident) if applicable, and the ULC's trial balance ledger evidencing no Canadian gross revenue attributable to the export supply
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill (only if ancillary physical components such as pre-loaded tablets, firmware dongles, or installation media are exported with the SaaS licence; must be endorsed to the ULC and evidence the direct export from Canada to the recipient's foreign jurisdiction)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / IRC or equivalent bank receipt issued by the ULC's Canadian or U.S. banking institution showing conversion of foreign currency remittance to CAD, referencing the invoice number and the SaaS contract

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls (CTC), or digital stamping for GST/HST invoices. The CRA requires that GST/HST registrants maintain and issue, on request, valid GST/HST registration information and supporting documentation sufficient to substantiate the tax treatment of a supply. For B2B supplies, an invoice containing the supplier's BN and GST/HST registration number, the date of invoice, a description of the supply, the consideration, and the amount of tax (unless zero-rated) must be provided within 30 days of a written request. Electronic invoicing is permitted without prior CRA authorization, but the invoice must be in a readable format and must be produced as a searchable portable document format (PDF) or equivalent. There is no government-operated tax clearance API or e-invoice repository. The CRA may require a GST/HST registrant to file an RC179 (GST/HST Return) electronically through GST/HST NETFILE if taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5 million per annum; all registrants must report via GST/HST NETFILE at the required frequency and may use certified commercial accounting software with API integration to the CRA's 'My Business Account' for remittances and data retrieval.

The CRA can reassess a GST/HST return within four years from the due date (or longer if misrepresentation or fraud is established). Claiming zero-rating without adequate non-resident evidence exposes the ULC to a reassessment of 5% GST on gross revenue plus interest at the prescribed rate (currently compounded daily) under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act. Failure to file monthly/quarterly GST/HST returns attracts penalties under section 280.1: an immediate 1% penalty plus 25% of the unpaid tax (or 35% if a repeat failure). Knowingly making, or participating in, a false or deceptive export zero-rating claim constitutes tax evasion under subsection 327.1(1) of the Excise Tax Act and section 239 of the Income Tax Act, carrying fines of up to CAD 100,000 plus imprisonment for up to two years for an indictable offence. A fraudulent export claim may also be referred to the CRA's Audit, Collections, and Criminal Investigation Directorate, leading to a 50% gross negligence penalty under subsection 327(2) of the Excise Tax Act on the amount of tax sought to be evaded. Regarding repatriation, while Canadian law does not impose a strict 180-day FX-repatriation deadline for SaaS exports, if the ULC's underlying contract is financed or guaranteed by Export Development Canada or the supply involves 'export earnings' subject to the Canadian foreign affiliate deemed-dividend rules, documentary evidence of payment repatriation within 180 days is a customary audit expectation; failure to evidence repatriation may cause the CRA to treat the supply as taxable in Canada, disallow the zero-rating, and apply gross negligence penalties if repeated.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account under the International Monetary Fund Article VIII status. Canada is a member of the OECD and G20, committed to free capital movement with limited residual restrictions. Current-account convertibility, including dividend distributions, management fees, royalties, and interest payments from a Canadian non-resident-owned ULC, is unrestricted subject to statutory documentation (e.g., withholding tax forms, transfer-pricing documentation). Capital-account convertibility, such as inbound shareholder loans and outbound loan repayments, is also unrestricted but may trigger Section 212.1 of the Income Tax Act (thin capitalization, shareholder loan rules) and anti-avoidance provisions under the Canada Revenue Agency. Profit remittance to non-resident shareholders is not blocked; however, a ULC that has elected under Section 216 or is deemed to be a corporation for Part I tax purposes may face branch tax or withholding tax implications. The main practical hurdles are banking AML/KYC, not FX convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules, mandatory conversion ratios, or repatriation timelines in Canada. Exporters may hold foreign currency in Canadian or offshore bank accounts freely. For a BC ULC engaged in medical-clinic EMR SaaS, subscription payments and software licensing fees are treated as export revenue for GST/HST purposes and may be zero-rated under the place-of-supply rules, but no currency conversion or surrender is required. Funds may be repatriated or reinvested abroad without permission from the Bank of Canada.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange policy is administered jointly with the Department of Finance, while AML/ATF oversight for money services businesses is enforced by FINTRAC. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority and does not impose exchange controls under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Foreign digital payment platforms and Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Payoneer, or Airwallex may offer multi-currency accounts and SWIFT rails to a Canadian ULC, but they must register as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC unless they rely on a Canadian partner-bank arrangement. The ULC itself may maintain an account with a foreign EMI for ancillary disbursements; however, such accounts do not replace a domestic Canadian banking relationship for statutory escrow, payroll, and GST/HST remittance purposes. Legal constraints include (i) prohibited lending or deposit-taking activities under the Bank Act; (ii) FINTRAC MSB registration thresholds (only if the EMI is physically conducting business in Canada); and (iii) absence of Canadian deposit insurance (CDIC). For medical-clinic EMR operations, patient health information must never transit through unencrypted or US-only payment rails, and PIPEDA / BC PHIPA compliance may limit using offshore EMI sub-accounts without contractual data-processing safeguards. A pragmatic structure is to use a Tier-1 domestic bank as the primary liquidity hub and a regulated Canadian MSB (e.g., a payment platform licensed under provincial money-lender regulations) for B2B subscription billing.

Beneficial ownership complexity and unlimited liability structure

Root cause: Beneficial ownership complexity and unlimited liability structure

Defense evidence: Provide a clean corporate organizational chart showing all UBOs (individuals with ≥25% control) as required under the Canada Business Corporations Act (or BC Business Corporations Act), certified resolutions authorizing account opening, a unanimous shareholder agreement, and a legal opinion from a Canadian counsel explaining the ULC's unlimited liability nature, tax classification, and the business rationale for using a ULC. Also include current provincial registry extracts and a copy of the ULC's certificate of good standing.
Non-resident shareholder or director residency

Root cause: Non-resident shareholder or director residency

Defense evidence: Submit notarized and apostilled passports, proof of residential address, and tax identification numbers for all non-resident shareholders, together with Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) registration number, GST/HST account, and a description of the role each foreign director will play. If the non-resident shareholder is in a FATF high-risk jurisdiction, provide an independent source-of-funds report, a bank reference from an international bank, and evidence of tax compliance with both CRA and home-country authorities.
Payment flows from health-care providers and cross-border medical data

Root cause: Payment flows from health-care providers and cross-border medical data

Defense evidence: Supply the executed EMR software license agreements with medical clinics, a privacy impact assessment under PIPEDA and BC PHIPA, a sub-processor list excluding US-based cloud infrastructure if patient data residency is in Canada, and a SOC 2 Type II report or ISO 27001 certification. Demonstrate that payment processing occurs through PCI-DSS-compliant rails and that no patient personal health information is transmitted through the bank's transaction descriptions.
Handling of escrow / capital-deposit accounts

Root cause: Handling of escrow / capital-deposit accounts

Defense evidence: Provide the statutory or contractual basis for the capital escrow (e.g., corporate law capital maintenance or client trust rules for medical-clinic depositories), a written escrow agreement, and a clear ledger of inbound and outbound movements. Confirm that the ULC is not acting as a money services business or taking deposits from third parties; if any clinical payment intermediary function exists, register with FINTRAC as an MSB and provide the MSB registration certificate.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For a BC ULC receiving equity capital from a non-resident parent or shareholder, the compliant wire protocol uses SWIFT MT103 or Fedwire/SWIFT cross-border transfer in CAD or USD to the ULC's CAD or USD clearing account at a Tier-1 Canadian bank. The payment must reference the corporate name and the purpose code 'Equity Injection' (or 'Subscription for Shares'). For tax compliance, provide to the bank a resolution authorizing the issuance of shares, a subscription agreement, and a Canadian-counsel opinion that the inbound equity is treated as paid-up capital without triggering Part XIII withholding tax. For amounts exceeding CAD 10,000, the bank will file an electronic funds transfer report with FINTRAC. If the transfer is structured as a shareholder loan, the purpose code should be 'Intercompany Loan' and the transaction must comply with Section 15(2) and 80.4 of the Income Tax Act to avoid deemed benefit. The ULC should maintain a dedicated Capital Escrow Account to hold the funds until the rights to shares are legally perfected, with clear segregation from operating funds.Trade settlements for a medical-clinic EMR SaaS business are primarily service exports. The compliant outbound rails are standard SWIFT MT103 for vendor payments, licensing fees, dividend distributions, and intercompany charges. For larger transactional service contracts, a Commercial Letter of Credit (LC) is rarely required for SaaS recurring subscriptions, but if the customer insists on a guarantee, a standby LC (SWIFT MT700) can be issued by the Tier-1 bank. All outbound remittances must include the required purpose/payment code under the Government of Canada's Reporting of Currency and Monetary Instruments (RCMI) framework. Dividends to non-residents must use the correct income code and may be subject to 25% Part XIII withholding tax, reduced under an applicable tax treaty. Royalty and software-license payments must be supported by a transfer-pricing document (Form T106) demonstrating arm's-length pricing. The bank may request a copy of the CRA's NR4 remittance statement for dividend payments to confirm that withholding has been remitted. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. Foreign digital payment platforms and Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Payoneer, or Airwallex may offer multi-currency accounts and SWIFT rails to a Canadian ULC, but they must register as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC unless they rely on a Canadian partner-bank arrangement. The ULC itself may maintain an account with a foreign EMI for ancillary disbursements; however, such accounts do not replace a domestic Canadian banking relationship for statutory escrow, payroll, and GST/HST remittance purposes. Legal constraints include (i) prohibited lending or deposit-taking activities under the Bank Act; (ii) FINTRAC MSB registration thresholds (only if the EMI is physically conducting business in Canada); and (iii) absence of Canadian deposit insurance (CDIC). For medical-clinic EMR operations, patient health information must never transit through unencrypted or US-only payment rails, and PIPEDA / BC PHIPA compliance may limit using offshore EMI sub-accounts without contractual data-processing safeguards. A pragmatic structure is to use a Tier-1 domestic bank as the primary liquidity hub and a regulated Canadian MSB (e.g., a payment platform licensed under provincial money-lender regulations) for B2B subscription billing.
