Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) – federal income tax and GST/HST administration; portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html) and GST/HST NETFILE; customs interface via Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for import/export accounts and export declarations.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-resident BC Unlimited Liability Corporation must first assess whether it is carrying on business in Canada for GST/HST purposes. Registration for a federal Business Number (BN), GST/HST account, and, if applicable, payroll and import/export program accounts is completed by filing CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or online through CRA My Business Account. Required supporting evidence includes: certificate of incorporation or continuance of the BC ULC, registered office address in British Columbia, board resolution authorizing the registration representative, proof of identity for directors and controlling officers, and a description of planned Canadian business activities. CRA issues a nine-digit BN with program-specific suffixes (e.g., RT0001 for GST/HST, RM0001 for payroll, and RD0001 for import/export). A ULC without a Canadian permanent establishment must still obtain a BN and GST/HST registration before making taxable supplies in Canada or before claiming input tax credits on importations; registration is mandatory once the entity ceases to be a small supplier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Under subsection 96(2.1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada), a BC Unlimited Liability Corporation all of whose issued shares are owned by non-resident persons may elect to be treated for Canadian income tax purposes as a partnership, enabling flow-through taxation to its members (the 'Canadian check-the-box' election). The election is filed using Form T1001 and is effective from the date specified, allowing ULC members (e.g., U.S. LLC owners) to obtain transparent treatment and avoid Canadian entity-level Part I tax. Without a valid election, the ULC defaults to ordinary corporate taxation under Part I of the Income Tax Act. Additional elections available include the section 216 election for rental income remittance by non-residents and elections under Part IV of the Excise Tax Act relating to GST/HST rebate and recovery rules; late-filed 96(2.1) elections may qualify for relief under the CRA administrative policy.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (small supplier threshold) – GST/HST registration becomes mandatory once the entity's worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies exceeds CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters; BC PST registration is separately triggered at CAD 10,000 of taxable sales for sellers located in or selling into British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, a zero-rated supply includes a supply of freight transportation, courier, or logistics services that is in respect of goods exported from Canada (sections 7 and 20), and services supplied to a non-resident person in respect of goods that are exported. A BC ULC engaged in GDP pharma freight may zero-rate its supply of transportation, cold-chain handling, and storage services where: (i) the goods are physically exported from Canada as part of the service arrangement; (ii) the supply is made to an exporter, a non-resident freight forwarder, or the foreign consignee; and (iii) the ULC retains evidence of export and payment in a foreign currency. Services that are ancillary to the export (e.g., temperature-controlled staging, customs brokerage, perishable-pharma handling) qualify where they are performed in Canada and are directly linked to the subsequent export movement. The ULC must document the pharmaceutical chain of custody and GDP compliance records to demonstrate that the services are performed on goods destined for export, not domestic consumption.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Canada Export Reporting System – CAED / G16 or e-manifest)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or cross-border truck) or equivalent cargo manifest
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank confirmation of foreign-currency payment repatriation within 180 days)
  • Commercial Invoice and detailed packing list evidencing the pharma consignment
  • SWIFT wire or Direct ACH payment confirmation from the foreign freight principal
  • GDP compliance logs (temperature excursion records, chain-of-custody documentation) retained for CRA audit

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or an API-based tax authority pre-approval step for invoice issuance. Federal and BC legislation require GST/HST invoices to contain prescribed information (supplier name and BN, date of invoice, description of goods/services, GST/HST paid or payable, and applicable rate). Electronic invoicing is permissible, and the CRA has endorsed the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Interoperability Framework (based on PEPPOL / OIOUBL BIS 3.0) for voluntary B2B exchange. There is no mandatory national clearinghouse validation; however, all electronic records and invoice data must be retained in readable format for at least six years. For cross-border freight services, invoices must additionally identify the export nature of the supply and reference supporting export documentation to substantiate zero-rating.

Under the Excise Tax Act, a ULC that zero-rates exports without adequate proof of export is liable to reassessment of GST/HST on the full value of the supply, plus arrears interest compounded daily. Penalties for false statements or omissions include a penalty up to 25% of the amount at issue under subsection 285(1), increased to 50% for gross negligence; fraudulent claims may be prosecuted under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, exposing officers to fines and imprisonment. CRA and CBSA jointly audit export zero-rating claims under GST/HST Memorandum 7-3, and the ULC must maintain export evidence for six years. Failure to produce the mandatory customs and FX documentation within the audit window permits CRA to recharacterize the supply as taxable and claw back refunds or input tax credits retroactively for up to four years, with no limitation period in cases of fraud or misrepresentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates under a fully convertible currency framework on both current and capital accounts as an IMF Article VIII member. There are no Canadian foreign-exchange controls restricting inflows or outflows, no permission or licensing thresholds for the conversion of CAD to foreign currency, and no restriction on the repatriation of profits by a British Columbia unlimited liability corporation (BC ULC). The relevant frictions are not FX-based but tax and reporting based: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid by the BC ULC to a non-resident shareholder (statutory 25% unless reduced by an applicable Canada tax treaty); the requirement to maintain arm's-length pricing for cross-border related-party transactions to avoid transfer-pricing adjustments under section 247 of the Income Tax Act; the potential application of thin-capitalisation and withholding rules to interest-bearing shareholder advances; FINTRAC large-transaction reporting for transfers of C$10,000 or more; and Canadian sanctions/export-control due diligence on pharmaceutical and clinical freight. Profit remittances must be supported by board resolutions, financial statements evidencing available earnings, and documentation that the unlimited-liability corporate form is not being used to evade Canadian tax or creditor obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canadian law does not impose any mandatory surrender, conversion deadline, or minimum conversion ratio for export proceeds. A BC ULC may retain freight and logistics export receipts in CAD, USD, EUR, or any other permitted currency in accounts with a Canadian Schedule I bank or an authorized foreign bank. Export proceeds must be supported by commercial invoices, customs declarations, GDP certification logs, bills of lading or airway bills, and settlement instructions sufficient to satisfy a Canadian Revenue Agency audit and FINTRAC record-keeping obligations. The standard documentary retention period is at least six years. There is no statutory timeline within which export receipts must be brought into Canada, but once any inbound or outbound transaction reaches C$10,000 in value, the bank is obliged to file a large-transaction report with FINTRAC, and the bank may internally require the ULC to provide the underlying trade documentation to clear payment screening.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Moderate for ancillary cross-border payment routing; Low for primary treasury settlement. Offshore digital EMIs and payment service providers may be engaged as non-bank payment gateways, but they cannot act as the BC ULC's principal settlement institution in Canada. An offshore EMI that collects or pays out funds on behalf of Canadian residents is a money service business or payment service provider under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and the Retail Payment Activities Act. Such an entity must be registered with FINTRAC and, where applicable, registered with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act before offering payment services to a Canadian ULC. It must maintain settlement accounts through a Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II bank; direct clearing membership in Lynx or direct access to Canadian automated clearing settlement is unavailable to unlicensed offshore EMIs.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections to the BC ULC should be settled by SWIFT MT103 from the foreign parent or investor through its bank to the ULC's receiving account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. The SWIFT MT103 must contain the full legal name of the ULC and its British Columbia registration number in the beneficiary fields (field 57/59), the exact remitting account information, and in field 70 a clear purpose phrase such as 'EQUITY CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' or 'SHARE SUBSCRIPTION FOR CA_ULC'. There is no Canadian government-mandated FX purpose code, but the receiving bank is required to know the true economic purpose and will apply its own internal transaction-code classification. The Canadian bank may also require a copy of the ULC board resolution authorizing the account and the capital injection, plus the underlying share subscription and corporate registry filings, before settling FX. If the contribution is made in USD, the bank will convert at the prevailing benchmark/spot rate or at any locked forward rate in place. Larger transactions will be subject to FINTRAC large-transaction reporting and to bank-level sanctions screening. To avoid adverse tax recharacterisation, the inbound funds should be documented as an equity contribution, not as a shareholder loan, unless a separate formal loan arrangement with commercially reasonable interest and transfer-pricing documentation is maintained.Outbound trade and operating disbursements for the GDP clinical logistics business should be executed through the following clearing rails: (1) domestic Canadian-dollar payments via Direct ACH/Electronic Funds Transfer and the Lynx real-time settlement system for high-value obligations; (2) cross-border supplier and freight payments via SWIFT MT103 messages with exact beneficiary bank details, including SWIFT/BIC, IBAN or account number, and payment reference information containing the commercial invoice number and harmonized-system tariff code; (3) commercial letters of credit, either at sight or usance, issued by the Canadian bank in favour of international pharmaceutical suppliers or freight providers where documentary title and compliance assurance are required; (4) for certain government-domiciled payors, automated clearing through the Canadian Payments Association and correspondent banking arrangements. Every outbound disbursement must be screened against Canadian sanctions, OFAC and international sanctions lists, monitored for high-risk jurisdictions, supported by the relevant invoice, customs declaration, shipping document and GDP record, and, where the payment exceeds C$10,000 or an internal bank threshold, subjected to enhanced transaction-level review. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Moderate for ancillary cross-border payment routing; Low for primary treasury settlement. 23% (combined Federal 15% and British Columbia provincial 8% general corporate rate; BC small business rate 0% for Canadian-controlled private corporations on first CAD 500,000 of active business income)

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 annual aggregate threshold for controlled cross-border transactions requiring complete OECD-compliant Local File/Master File documentation in CRA administrative practice; CbC notification is mandatory for MNE groups with consolidated revenue exceeding approximately CAD 783 million (EUR 750 million). Below CAD 1 million, CRA may still demand contemporaneous records under s.247(4).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain an Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to UAE; instead, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under s.245 and transfer-pricing rules under s.247 impose a functional substance requirement. The BC ULC must demonstrate real commercial activity, including local GDP-validated operations, employees, storage facilities and contractual substance. Absent genuine economic activity, CRA may re-characterize intercompany arrangements as a sham and deny treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Return

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s.239(1): fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; under s.163(2): gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax; compounded daily interest under s.161, plus CRA enforcement including garnishment, liens and asset seizures.

Director personal liability: Directors become jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions, unpaid Part XIII withholding, and GST/HST under s.227.1 of the Income Tax Act; where a director knowingly participates in evasion, CRA may issue a director's liability certificate and the CBSA may impose travel restrictions for outstanding tax debts above CAD 10,000.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke GAAR under s.245 to deny the treaty benefit and re-assess on a non-treaty basis; the company may be deemed a sham under common law, resulting in full tax re-assessment with penalties and interest; provincial registration may be cancelled under BC Business Corporations Act if the filing is materially untrue.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil in cases of sham or fraud; directors can be personally liable for fraudulent conveyances under BC law, including clawback of transferred assets; criminal charges may follow under Criminal Code s.380(1) (fraud affecting public market) with imprisonment up to 14 years.
Transfer Pricing and Intercompany Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA re-assessment at the arm's-length price under s.247(2); statutory transfer-pricing penalty under s.247(3) equal to 10% of the amount of the gross transfer-pricing correction where documentation is incomplete; failure to provide documents after a formal demand carries an additional daily penalty; statutory interest accrues from the original filing-due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false or forged intercompany contracts may be held personally liable for a trust-rule liability; in cases of intention to defeat the Revenue Act, the court can enter judgment against directors jointly and severally for the shortfall, plus penalties, and may issue personal bankruptcy progression or charge criminal fraud.
AML / Money Laundering Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 per contravention against a corporation; criminal laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31 carries up to five years imprisonment and unlimited property forfeiture; CRA may also apply the 50% gross-negligence penalty to unreported proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly direct, authorize or participate in laundering drug freight proceeds are personally liable under the Criminal Code and may suffer criminal forfeiture of personal assets; BC Civil Forfeiture permits non-conviction-based confiscation of personal and corporate assets, and the corporate veil is irrelevant for intentional misconduct.
