Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada) — corporate income tax and GST/HST administration; portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html). BC Ministry of Finance — Consumer Taxation Branch for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) via eTaxBC portal.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned ULC must obtain a federal Business Number (9-digit BN). Standard procedure: (1) complete Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and CRA Program Accounts' online through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or by mail, including a Canadian agent-for-service address if non-resident; (2) CRA automatically creates the corporate income tax account (BN/'RC0001'); (3) register for GST/HST program account (BN/'RT0001') when making taxable supplies in Canada unless the small supplier threshold applies; (4) for BC PST, file Form WPST-1 'Application for Provincial Sales Tax Registration' via eTaxBC or through BRO and obtain a separate PST number. A non-resident registrant may need to post security under ETA s.240. The GST/HST number is the BN followed by 'RT0001'; the corporate income tax account uses 'RC0001'.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible (foreign U.S. check-the-box classification is available; under the Canadian Income Tax Act the BC ULC remains a taxable Canadian corporation taxed at entity level and is not eligible for Canadian domestic pass-through treatment).

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box or entity classification election under the Income Tax Act; a BC ULC is statutorily a corporation for Canadian tax. For U.S. federal tax purposes, a ULC owned by a U.S. person may file IRS Form 8832 to elect classification as a disregarded entity (pass-through) under Treasury Regulation §301.7701-3, creating a hybrid entity outcome when combined with Canadian entity-level taxation. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules, including excessive interest and country-by-country limitation rules, may restrict the resulting benefit.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies over the immediately preceding four calendar quarters (general GST/HST small-supplier threshold). Under the 2021 digital services registration framework, non-resident suppliers of digital products or SaaS to Canadian consumers must register if annual Canadian taxable sales exceed CAD 30,000. BC PST has no general monetary threshold; registration is required as soon as the ULC makes taxable sales in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST/HST zero-rating is available under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI. For goods, the supply is zero-rated where the supplier exports the goods from Canada within 60 days or ships them in direct export. For services supplied to a non-resident, zero-rating applies under Sch. VI s.20 if the service is not performed in Canada, or if performed in Canada exclusively in respect of tangible personal property situated outside Canada at the time of performance. For B2B digital services such as SaaS and e-signature authentication, the supply is generally zero-rated when the recipient is a non-resident and the place of supply is outside Canada; evidence of non-residence, actual use abroad, and absence of Canadian benefit is required. Claims for input tax credits on inputs attributable to zero-rated exports must be fully substantiated.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canadian G49) for tangible goods exports
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill or other contract of carriage
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank transfer advice evidencing foreign currency settlement (SWIFT MT103)
  • Commercial invoice with description, HS/NTRM codes, destination and value
  • Sales contract / purchase order and technical service description
  • Evidence of non-resident status of customer (foreign tax ID, address, incorporation records)

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory government API clearance exists in Canada. CRA does not mandate e-invoicing. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA My Business Account or EDI where taxable sales exceed CAD 1.5 million annually, but all registrants may e-file voluntarily. BC PST returns are filed through eTaxBC. All prescribed books and records must be retained in Canada or made available for CRA inspection; electronic records must be convertible to an auditable accessible format.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated export within the normal four-year reassessment window, with no time limit where misrepresentation, fraud, or willful default is shown. If zero-rating is denied, GST/HST becomes payable at the applicable rate in British Columbia (5%) plus statutory interest and arrears. Excise Tax Act s.280 imposes a penalty of 25% of net tax for false statements or omissions; Income Tax Act s.239(1) can support tax evasion prosecution with fines up to 200% of the amount evaded and imprisonment. Input tax credits claimed on the exports are clawed back and any refunded amounts become recoverable. Similar BC PST reassessment powers, interest, and misreporting penalties apply where a PST exemption is incorrectly claimed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. Canada imposes no exchange-control licensing, no capital-repatriation approval, and no registration requirement for foreign investments into or out of a British Columbia limited liability company (ULC). Profit remittance via dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, or liquidation proceeds is legally unrestricted; the only fiscal friction is Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, which imposes a 25% statutory withholding tax on outbound dividends and passive payments, generally reduced to 5%–15% under an applicable treaty (e.g., 5% for a US corporate parent owning at least 25% of the ULC's shares). No central-bank economic-needs test applies, and the Bank of Canada does not require end-use certificates for cross-border payments.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada operates a free-funds regime: there is no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio (0% surrender requirement), and no requirement that export receipts be held in Canadian-dollar accounts. A BC ULC may retain foreign currency indefinitely in a USD/EUR-denominated account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. However, the ULC must maintain full books and records under the Income Tax Act, must report every cross-border funds transfer equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 to FINTRAC through its financial institution, and must account for foreign-exchange gains or losses in taxable income.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential supervision of financial institutions and foreign-exchange clearing conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money-laundering oversight administered by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility as a supplementary rail, not as the primary banking layer. Offshore or foreign-licensed e-money institutions (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Mercury) cannot lawfully operate as deposit-taking banks in Canada unless authorized by OSFI, and they do not provide direct access to the Canadian high-value clearing system (LYNX, formerly Large Value Transfer System). An unlicensed offshore EMI may hold funds and execute SWIFT/ACH/SEPA transfers, but Canadian-dollar clearing requires an OSFI-regulated institution. Therefore the viable design is: (i) primary CAD operational and capital-escalation accounts at a Schedule I bank; (ii) a foreign EMI used exclusively as a procurement/payout conduit for USD/EUR/SGD corridors; and (iii) Stripe or another card acquirer used solely for retail SaaS billing, not treasury settlement.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity subscriptions and shareholder loans must be remitted by SWIFT MT103 (for single-customer credit transfers) or MT202/MTCOV cover payments, using the full 15-character SWIFT/BIC of the beneficiary BC branch. Canada does not maintain a statutory purpose-code registry, but the bank's compliance narrative must include 'EQUITY CONTRIBUTION' or 'SHARE SUBSCRIPTION'; transfers equal to or above CAD 10,000 are automatically reported to FINTRAC, and enhancements above CAD 100,000 trigger automated source-of-funds and source-of-wealth requests. The recommended rail is a US-parent entity wiring via its US bank into the ULC's USD-denominated Schedule I operating account, followed by an internal spot conversion to CAD; each remitter must provide the corporate legal name, registered office, jurisdiction, and a full beneficial-ownership attestation, and the originator bank must be FATF-compliant.Non-documentary treasury payments to suppliers, licensors, and subcontractors should be executed by SWIFT MT103 with a single beneficiary; MT202/MTCOV cover payments are used for any intermediary-bank settlement. For higher-value, invoice-backed international transactions, a Commercial Letter of Credit (LC) issued by a Canadian Schedule I bank is available, but for LegalTech SaaS the standard operating rail is MT103 combined with ACH/SEPA local payout corridors and card-acquirer settlement via Stripe. Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (Federal 15% general rate under ITA s.123(1) + British Columbia 12% provincial rate under BC Income Tax Act s.20); reduced to 11% combined for the first C$500,000 of active business income only where the ULC qualifies as a Canadian-controlled private corporation — generally unavailable for a non-resident-controlled ULC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII gross withholding tax on 'dividends' paid or credited to non-resident shareholders (ITA s.212(2)); because the BC ULC is a hybrid eligible for US check-the-box, distributions from the US-flow-through perspective may nonetheless remain Canadian-source dividends subject to Part XIII; s.212(2) applies to deemed dividends on winding-up as well.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 must be filed within 6 months after the fiscal year-end (ITA s.150(1)(a)); due date is six months after year-end (e.g., Dec 31 year-end: due June 30). Balance of tax is due on the balance-due day, 3 months after year-end for non-CCPCs (2 months for CCPCs); instalments required where prior-year federal+BC tax exceeds C$3,000.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File duty for Canadian reporting entities with gross revenue above C$50 million AND reportable cross-border controlled transactions above C$5 million (CRA Information Circular IC 94-4R / OECD-aligned three-tier documentation); Form T106 required at C$1 million per non-arm's length non-resident counterparty; Master File required for consolidated MNE group revenue ≥ C$750 million; Country-by-Country Report for Canadian ultimate-parent groups ≥ C$750M revenue. A LegalTech SaaS ULC operating at the C$5–50M tier should nevertheless prepare lightweight contemporaneous documentation if intercompany charges exceed C$5M or carry any pricing risk.

Economic substance scope: Canada is not an ESR jurisdiction; the substantive doctrines governing ULC hybrid structures are ITA s.245 GAAR (statutory purposive abuse-into-tax-avoidance), s.247 transfer pricing recharacterization (CRA can disregard or reclassify artificial transactions), and the Canada-US Treaty LOB provisions (Article XXIX-A) denying treaty benefits to 'conduit' ULCs lacking real business in BC. CRA actively audits cross-border SaaS-evergreen licence arrangements, requiring payroll substance in the ULC (employees, actual software operations, board case-in-point) to defeat abuse-evil-contentions where a Canadian ULC is used as a hybrid pass-through conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Wilful Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: ITA s.239(1): summary conviction — fine 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to 2 years; indictment — fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. CRA Criminal Investigations Program referral. Civil gross-negligence penalty s.163(2) 50% of understated tax; TP gross-negligence penalty s.247(3) 10% of net adjustment without a reasonable-efforts defence.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted Part XIII withholding, payroll withholdings, and GST remittances if the ULC fails; CRA may assess directors for full amounts plus interest and penalties without requiring the corporation to be formally dissolved. Travel bans under the Proceeds of Crime money-laundering statutory regime, director criminal prosecution under s.239 if participating in the evasion, and civil restitution orders under the Ontario/BC analogue of corporate-veil piercing. Under s.160 related-party transfer rules, a ULC distributing assets to US shareholders at undervalue creates joint and several liability for unpaid tax.
Transfer Pricing / Thin Capitalization Audit Adjustments

Statutory sanction: CRA adjustment under s.247(2) adds the full TP shortfall to income; s.247(3) penalty is 10% of the aggregate net adjustment, not reduced by any offsetting adjustment, against every related party. Interest on the deficiency compounds at the prescribed rate from the balance-due date. Recharacterization of a sham under s.245 disallows the transaction in its entirety.

Director personal liability: Directors of the ULC can be sued jointly for TP-correction-derived deficiencies only where the deficiency arises from unremitted withholdings; ordinary TP corporate arrears do not pierce the veil unless the directors personally caused the misreporting — then gross-negligence penalties and s.239 criminal exposure attach personally. CRA may also assert director liability for the GST/HST portion on the intercompany back-charge invoices.
Hybrid Mismatch / Treaty Abuse (Shell ULC)

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment disregarding the ULC or reattributing income to a permanent establishment in Canada; CRA issues notice under treaty Article XXIX-A denying the 5%/0% reduced rates; a deemed dividend under s.212(1)(b) at 25% can be applied; administrative penalty under s.163(2) up to 50% for false statements in the NR302 treaty-application; recomputation of Canadian-source tax under Part I on deemed PE profits.

Director personal liability: Under Canadian conflicts-of-law and CBCA/BCBCA fair-standards, the BC Court may pierce the ULC veil where the corporate form is a 'mere instrumentality' in fraudulent conveyances; directors signing the NR302 treaty-certificate without ensuring the LOB tests are met commit a false certification — CRA can assess a §163.2 'false statement' penalty of up to C$3,000 per false T2/T106 statement (sic) and cause the director to be named in a s.239 prosecution; senior officers may be barred from crossing into Canada at the direction of a tax warrant until restitution.
Part XIII Withholding Default / Non-Resident Distribution

Statutory sanction: s.215(1) makes the ULC absolutely liable for the whole Part XIII amount not withheld; s.227(8) interest at prescribed rate; penalties up to 10% of the unreported withheld amounts for failure to file information returns (NR4, s.162(11)); repeated failure up to 20%. The safe harbour of reasonable care is exiguous.

Director personal liability: s.227.1 director liability is automatic for all Part XIII remittance failures: CRA can issue a director's notice of assessment and demand, enforce against personal bank accounts and the director's BC-registered personal property, and obtain a court order enabling foreign enforcement. Directors also face a minimum C$500 fine under the Bureau de l'assurance? no — under the BC Business Corporations Act for authorizing unlawful dividends where the ULC is insolvent (equitable restitution action from the directors personally) and the shareholder remains liable to repay the illegal dividend.
