Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA-ARC), dedicated portals: 'My Business Account' and 'Business Registration Online' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); provincial retail-sales-tax counterpart in British Columbia is the BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes/sales-taxes/pst).

E-government filing portal

A BC-incorporated ULC obtains a 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and, where its taxable supplies exceed the small-supplier threshold, a GST/HST program account (RT/RZ) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and CRA Program Accounts) with the CRA or, for incorporated companies, through the combined BC Corporate Registry / Business Registration Online workflow; attach the certificate of incorporation issued under the BC Business Corporations Act, registered-office particulars, director/shareholder information, and the planned accounting year-end. CRA issues the BN within 1-10 business days. Foreign (e.g., US) shareholders may be identified without a Canadian SIN; the ULC must retain a registered office and corporate records in BC. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a BC ULC is eligible for US federal check-the-box treatment (IRS Form 8832) as a disregarded entity or partnership, but under Canadian domestic income tax law the ULC is an entity-level corporate taxpayer resident in Canada by incorporation (ITA s. 250(4)(a)); Canada has no domestic check-the-box election, so Canadian taxation stays at the entity level regardless of the US-side pass-through classification.

Available election forms

Canada has no statutory equivalent to check-the-box; the relevant levers are: (i) IRS Form 8832 entity-classification election (US side) to render the ULC transparent for US federal tax purposes; (ii) hybrid-mismatch alignment elections and self-assessments under ITA ss. 18.2, 18.3, 20.2 and 20.3 for hybrid dividend/branch-payment mismatches; (iii) income-tax rollover elections on seeding the ULC with media contracts, intangibles or customer lists under ss. 85(1), 97(2) or 98; (iv) s. 256 amalgamation elections if future provincial reorganizations are done; (v) GST/HST elections including voluntary registration (ETA s. 240(3)), the s. 156 nil-consideration election between closely related registrants, the quick-method election, and annual/semi-annual filing elections; and (vi) s. 216(4) election if the ULC later holds Canadian real property leased by its non-resident owners.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — a person becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant as soon as its worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies, financial services, supplies of goodwill, and certain other items) exceed CAD 30,000 either in one calendar quarter or over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA s. 148(1)); voluntary registration is available below the threshold to claim input tax credits. BC PST: registration is required from the first day a PST liability on taxable sales/leases in BC arises; no minimum PST registration threshold for general service providers, though specific platform thresholds exist for accommodation and short-term rental facilitators.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7, a service supplied to a non-resident person is zero-rated (0% GST) where the supply is not in respect of real property situated in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada when the service is performed, and not supplied to the non-resident while the person is in Canada. Affiliate-media-buying, programmatic advertising, and offshore lead-generation services performed by the BC ULC for non-resident advertisers, affiliate networks, and offshore principals qualify as zero-rated exports (full input-tax-credit recovery follows); services delivered to Canadian-resident advertisers or connected to Canadian-facing campaigns are taxable at 5% GST. Evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (e.g., foreign registration/certificate of residence, offshore billing address, and campaign-territory documentation) must be maintained.

  • Customs Export Declaration — Canadian Form B13A/export-reporting through the Canadian Border Services Agency (CBSA) or CARS self-assessment where goods, devices, promotional collateral or hardware accompany the campaign; for pure digital advertising services no customs declaration is filed, but a detailed commercial invoice and service rendering report must be retained.
  • Bill of Lading / air waybill or equivalent transport document — required for any physical export component; for offshore media-buying services substitute with ad-server traffic logs, platform auction records, insertion orders, and campaign-delivery evidence demonstrating performance to the non-resident account.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank credit advice — settlement proof showing remittance from the non-resident payer and conversion through the ULC's CAD account; this corroborates the ETA Schedule VI Part V s. 7 zero-rating on the output sale and the arm's-length character of cross-border media payments for transfer-pricing (ITA s. 247) purposes.

Canada currently imposes no statutory real-time e-invoicing, fiscal clearance, digital-stamping, or continuous-transaction-control (CTC) mandate on GST/HST. CRA mandates electronic GST/HST filing (GST/HST NETFILE) for registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 50,000 and electronic remittance where average monthly net tax exceeds CAD 10,000. Invoices must contain the registration number and the prescribed invoice-content elements under ETA s. 169 and GST/HST Memorandum 12.1; invoices may be issued on paper or in a structured electronic format (EDI/XML/JSON). No CRA API clearance gateway or mandatory pre-stamping of the ULC's invoices is in force; pan-Canadian e-invoicing interoperability is voluntary and under federal study.

CRA audits zero-rating claims strictly: the registrant bears the burden of proof and must retain export-eligibility records for six years (ETA s. 286, ITA s. 230). If the CRA determines that the supply was actually taxable in Canada — e.g., the recipient was present in Canada when the service was rendered, the service related to Canadian real/tangible property, or documentation was fictitious — it reassesses GST at 5% on full consideration, denies related input tax credits, and applies arrears interest plus penalties: ETA s. 280(1) (25% civil penalty on false/promoted tax-avoidance filings), ETA s. 285 gross-negligence penalty (50% of the net GST sought), and criminal prosecution under ETA s. 273. On the income-tax side, ITA s. 163(2) imposes a 50% gross-negligence penalty on any understated tax, with the s. 247(10) documentation penalty up to CAD 24,000 (CAD 1,000/month) for inadequate transfer-pricing records. Although the ETA imposes no literal 180-day FX-repatriation rule, extended non-repatriation of export proceeds with no commercial rationale invites GAAR (ITA s. 245) and s. 247 transfer-pricing recharacterization; the 180-day benchmark is therefore applied internally to the ULC's receivable-monitoring and foreign-exchange-settlement policy.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains IMF Article VIII status with full current- and capital-account convertibility and no exchange controls. A British Columbia ULC may freely invoice and hold CAD, USD, EUR, or other currencies and may remit dividends, interest, royalties, and loan repayments to non-resident shareholders without FX licensing, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced to 5% for dividends to a 10% U.S. corporate shareholder under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty), adequate corporate records, and FINTRAC monetary reporting thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory repatriation period, and no conversion ratio for export or advertising-service receipts. Affiliate media-buying revenue earned in U.S. dollars remains fully retained at treasury discretion, and all realized foreign-exchange gains or losses are recognized under Income Tax Act subsection 39(1)/(2) and ordinary business accounting principles.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) serves as the central monetary and fiscal agent, but Canada has no standalone foreign-exchange administration. Prudential supervision of banks and authorized FX dealers rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering surveillance sits with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); and sanctions enforcement is administered by Global Affairs Canada under the Special Economic Measures Act (SEMA) and the United Nations Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

High as a supplementary collection and distribution rail; low as the sole treasury venue. Wise Business, operating in Canada as a FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB), enables the BC ULC to hold and convert CAD/USD/EUR balances and to pay global publishers through local clearing; Payoneer and OFX are similarly viable MSB rails. Mercury is not available for Canadian-incorporated entities, although a U.S. disregarded-entity subsidiary could access Mercury ACH for USD publisher payouts. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate for ordinary income: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined rate on eligible active business income within the CAD 500,000 small-business limit, subject to federal and British Columbia eligibility rules. A non-resident-controlled ULC generally does not qualify as a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty or other statutory relief. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying company that owns at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other ordinary cases. Treaty anti-avoidance, limitation-on-benefits, beneficial-ownership, and hybrid-entity rules must be satisfied.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. A Canadian taxpayer must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length prices for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies when reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million in aggregate, subject to statutory rules. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, translated under the applicable Canadian rule. Canada does not impose a separate OECD-style statutory Master File threshold for all taxpayers, but CRA may expect group-level information and supporting documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Substance is nevertheless relevant through the Income Tax Act, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose or anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership analysis, transfer-pricing rules, debt-versus-equity characterization, corporate-residence principles, and the requirement that deductions be incurred to earn income. A media-buying ULC should maintain Canadian decision-making evidence, bank and accounting controls, personnel or genuine outsourced operational capacity, advertising-platform contracts, campaign records, client contracts, risk allocation, pricing support, and evidence of actual services. A ULC with no meaningful Canadian functions that merely receives and remits affiliate funds is vulnerable to denial of deductions, recharacterization, withholding adjustments, treaty denial, permanent-establishment disputes, and GAAR assessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, tax evasion can result in prosecution, fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment of up to five years. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, and denial of deductions may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and controlling persons may face personal criminal prosecution where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. Corporate limited-liability protection does not protect intentional fraud. Restitution, asset seizure, production orders, and prosecution-related travel restrictions may arise; an automatic director travel ban is not a standard tax sanction.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The corporation can be assessed for the unremitted withholding tax, interest, and penalties. Penalties may reach 10% of the amount required to be remitted, or 20% for knowing or grossly negligent failure, with additional penalties for repeated failures. NR4 non-compliance may generate separate penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for corporate source deductions, including Part XIII amounts, under the director-liability rules if the corporation failed to remit and statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under the transfer-pricing rules, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, assess withholding tax, and charge arrears interest. Contemporaneous documentation and reasonable-efforts requirements are critical.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for unpaid source deductions and GST/HST, and may face civil or criminal exposure if the pricing records are knowingly falsified. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, recharacterize income, deny expenses, assess Part XIII withholding, and impose civil penalties and interest. Anti-money-laundering and beneficial-ownership reporting consequences may also arise.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can face regulatory, civil, and criminal consequences for knowingly facilitating fraud, money laundering, or deceptive tax arrangements. Civil restitution and asset-restraint orders are possible; imprisonment depends on prosecution and conviction. Corporate status alone does not create automatic personal liability.
GST/HST Fraud and Improper Input Tax Credits

Statutory sanction: The Excise Tax Act permits reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence or false-statement penalties, and criminal prosecution. Fraudulent conduct can attract fines and imprisonment in addition to civil assessments.

Director personal liability: GST/HST amounts are trust-like liabilities. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted net tax, penalties, and interest where statutory conditions are met and due diligence is unavailable.
Payroll and Source-Deduction Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess unremitted deductions, employer CPP and EI amounts, penalties, interest, and information-return penalties. Repeated or deliberate failures can result in prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted payroll source deductions and related penalties, subject to statutory limitation periods and due-diligence defenses.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds-of-Crime Exposure

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, reporting obligations, criminal money-laundering charges, forfeiture, and sanctions-related penalties may apply. Criminal money laundering can carry substantial fines and imprisonment of up to ten years on indictment, depending on the offence.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may be prosecuted where they participated in or directed the conduct. Assets may be restrained or forfeited, and criminal courts may impose probation, restitution, or imprisonment. Travel restrictions are case-specific and are not automatic.
Corporate Record and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The Business Corporations Act (British Columbia), federal tax statutes, and applicable AML rules may impose compliance orders, administrative penalties, prosecution, and adverse evidentiary consequences. Tax deductions and treaty claims may be denied where records are inadequate.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory offences, fines, civil claims, and personal liability for intentional misconduct or statutory trust amounts. A BC ULC's unlimited shareholder liability does not automatically make directors personally liable for all corporate debts.
Unlimited Liability Corporation Structural Misuse

Statutory sanction: The ULC remains a separate Canadian legal person and is generally taxable as a corporation in Canada. Incorrect classification can cause double taxation, denied treaty benefits, unexpected withholding, reassessment, and creditor claims. Improper distributions can trigger corporate-law remedies.

Director personal liability: Shareholders of a BC ULC may have unlimited liability under applicable corporate-law rules. Directors may be personally liable for statutory remittances, unlawful distributions, oppression-related remedies, insolvency misconduct, and knowing breaches of duty. Imprisonment is reserved for applicable criminal or regulatory offences, not ordinary corporate insolvency.
