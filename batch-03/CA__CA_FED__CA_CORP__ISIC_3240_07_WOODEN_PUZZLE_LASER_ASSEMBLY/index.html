E-government filing portal

Incorporate federally or provincially to obtain a Canadian corporate identity; for existing Canadian corporations, register with CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts'. Non-resident foreign legal entities carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) and, where applicable, a GST/HST registration (program account RT), an import/export account (RM) via CBSA, and a payroll account (RP). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No OECD-style 'check-the-box' election exists in Canada; classification is determined by legal form and residency under the Income Tax Act. A non-resident corporation may elect under subsection 216(1) to file a separate return for treaty/federal rental income, and under subsection 216(1.1) for resource property income. GST/HST elections allow closely related corporations to treat supplies as having been made for nil consideration (section 156 of the Excise Tax Act). No hybrid mismatch check-the-box election is available, but Canada applies specific hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (total taxable supplies made during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; exceeding the small-supplier threshold triggers mandatory registration for residents and non-residents carrying on business in Canada)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available for exports of tangible personal property under Part V of Schedule VI (Excise Tax Act) when the goods are exported directly or as part of a continuous chain of export by the registrant, or by the recipient under approved conditions, or for non-resident customers under specified contracts. Services supplied to a non-resident are generally zero-rated if not for use in Canada and not in respect of Canadian real property or tangible personal property. Exports must be supported by contemporaneous documentary evidence.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Customs, CBSA B13A or ECI electronic equivalent), Bill of Lading, Inward Remittance Certificate / proof of payment from the non-resident purchaser
  • Supporting commercial invoice and sale contract identifying the exact goods/services exported
  • Transport documentation including waybill, harbour/shipping lines, or courier export receipts

No real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital stamping mandate is currently in force at the federal level. Canada relies on self-assessment and CRA post-filing audits. Registered suppliers must issue and retain GST/HST-compliant invoices containing prescribed information (Excise Tax Act, section 169 generally; Input Tax Credit Information Requirements in s.169.1 / Reg. 310), and must file GST/HST returns electronically when required by CRA. Small suppliers and certain non-resident suppliers may invoice without a GST/HST number. CRA encourages voluntary electronic invoicing under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing standard (Peppol/BIS), but it is not obligatory.

CRA may reassess zero-rating claims within four years (unlimited in cases of fraud or misrepresentation), disallow refunds, and impose interest under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act. Penalties apply under section 285 (for false statements, gross negligence) ranging from 5% to 10% of the net tax due, or $250, whichever is greater, and may be escalated for repeat violations. Criminal prosecution under section 327 can lead to fines of up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment not exceeding two years. Retrospective clawbacks extend to ineligible input tax credits and refunds, plus applicable provincial HST portions where the export substantiation is inadequate.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility. Canada has maintained IMF Article VIII status since 1952 and applies the same liberal treatment to current-account and capital-account flows: both are freely convertible. Profit remittance is unrestricted at the exchange-control level; the only fiscal gates are Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends, interest, rents, and royalties (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced in most cases to 15% or 5%), the thin-capitalization and interest-deductibility rules (Income Tax Act ss. 18(4)-(6), 212(3)), and CRS/FATCA reporting. No central-bank pre-approval, permit, or repatriation schedule applies to a Canada Business Corporation or its shareholders.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada repealed the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951 and imposes no statutory surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, no minimum retention period, and no timeline for repatriation of export proceeds. An ISIC 3240 wooden-puzzle exporter may lawfully invoice in USD or EUR, retain foreign-currency receipts in domestic or foreign accounts, and convert at market rates. The only compliance attach is ordinary: FINTRAC large-transaction reporting when thresholds are met, Statistics Canada / Bank of Canada International Transactions Reporting System classification of cross-border payments, and CRA foreign-currency disclosure; none of these restrict free disposal of export earnings.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank, constituted under the Bank of Canada Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. B-2) is the monetary and financial-system authority; prudential supervision of chartered banks is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and AML/ATF supervision to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange administration or licensing agency: the Minister of Finance holds reserve powers, but no statute empowers routine FX approval or control of cross-border payments.

Use SWIFT MT103 (customer credit transfer) into the beneficiary account at the chosen Schedule I bank. Recommended protocol: (i) open the Operational Account and, if funds are not yet fully subscribed, a segregated Capital Escrow Account; (ii) instruct the remitter to include the full corporate legal name exactly as registered under the CBCA, the beneficiary account number, and the narrative 'Foreign direct investment - paid-up capital contribution - ISIC 3240 / NAICS 339930'; (iii) provide the bank a pre-advice with the share subscription agreement, board resolution, and source-of-funds questionnaire; (iv) for amounts at or above CAD 10,000, the bank will classify the inflow under the Bank of Canada / Statistics Canada International Transactions Reporting System direct-investment category and may require an attestation that the funds are non-criminal proceeds. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility for a low-risk ISIC 3240 exporter, but strictly as a secondary collection and facilitation rail, never as the principal treasury or capital-escrow custodian. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, Ebury, Currencycloud) soliciting Canadian clients must be registered as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and, if foreign, must satisfy foreign-MSB registration rules. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual value of reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions (triggering CRA Form T106); CRA contemporaneously audits TP files when intercompany or royalty payments exceed this materiality marker; no separate CAD-only statutory Local/Master File threshold is stated in the Income Tax Act, but large MNEs with consolidated revenue above the OECD/Canada CbC threshold must also file CbC reporting.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a bespoke jurisdictional ESR (Economic Substance Regulations), but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR, s.245) and treaty-based limitation-on-benefits provisions require substantive business purpose, local management, and genuine operational capacity; a letterbox or shell corporation is exposed to CRA annulment, loss of treaty benefits, and penalty reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of tax understated; criminal prosecution under s.239 of the Income Tax Act may lead to fines not exceeding 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to 2 years, and judicial interest on all arrears.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and confiscated intermediary proceeds; when fraud is proven, corporate veil-piercing permits civil restitution, travel bans through enforcement actions, and personal imprisonment.
Shell Company / Application of GAAR

Statutory sanction: CRA can reassess on a substance-over-form basis, disallow the corporate entity or treaty benefits, and levy Part XIII withholding back-up on deemed ‘shadow distributions’, plus interest and administrative penalties up to 25–50% in cases of misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who structure sham corporate relationships may be personally assessed under s.160 for transfers of value; aggravated disregard of corporate formalities can support civil restitution and personal liability beyond the corporate veil.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML/PCMLTFA Violation)

Statutory sanction: FINES: up to CAD 500,000 for corporations and CAD 100,000 for individuals per administrative violation under PCMLTFA; criminal ‘money laundering’ liability under Criminal Code s.462.31 carries fines, confiscation of property, and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors who knowingly aid money-laundering flows face personal prosecution, asset freezes, travel bans, and restitution orders; CRA may also hold directors personally liable for hidden source funds and penalty amounts.
Transfer Pricing / Royalty Rebilling Abuse

Statutory sanction: Additional transfer-pricing penalties of 10% on net upward adjustments and regular tax on reassessed income; CRA can re-characterize transactions, impose Part XIII withholding tax on misclassified royalty streams, and set off restructuring penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who execute abusive intercompany rebilling as a deliberate scheme can be personally liable for unpaid source amounts; in serious fraud cases, personal judgment, freezing orders, travel bans, and custodial sanctions may follow.
