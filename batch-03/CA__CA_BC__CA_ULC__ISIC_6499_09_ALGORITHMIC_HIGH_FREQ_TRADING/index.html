E-government filing portal

A BC ULC (including a foreign-owned ULC) must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA before commencing commercial activity. Registration is available online via Business Registration Online, by phone, or by mail, using the valid incorporation certificate, legal name, jurisdiction of incorporation, directors, registered office, and majority owner details. The BN is the prerequisite TIN for GST/HST, income tax, and payroll accounts; non-resident applicants may appoint a representative and must disclose foreign beneficial ownership.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Available election forms

No Canadian 'check-the-box' election exists; a BC ULC is taxed as a corporation for Canadian purposes. For US tax, the ULC may elect under IRC §301.7701-3 (Form 8832) to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership, without altering Canadian tax classification. For GST/HST, a financial institution may elect its net tax method or use a specified alternative approach; treaty residency can be elected under Article IV(6) of the Canada-US Treaty.

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies; financial services are generally exempt, but a selected listed financial institution (e.g., certain financial intermediaries) is deemed registered for GST/HST regardless of threshold

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods, zero-rating requires export within 60 days (or prescribed time) under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act, with documentary proof of export. For offshore services, zero-rating applies when a financial service is supplied to a non-resident, provided the non-resident is not a specified financial institution and the supply is not a domestic financial service under Schedule VI Part VI section 6. The supplier must retain evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and the export nature of the transaction.

No real-time fiscal clearance, API clearance, or digital stamping mandate in Canada. GST/HST invoices may be issued electronically; mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns applies for registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD $1.5 million. Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully floating exchange-rate regime under the Bank of Canada Act and the Currency Act. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account; no central bank approval, quota, repatriation schedule, or priority allocation applies to cross-border capital movements. Profit remittance (dividends) from a BC ULC is legally unencumbered but attracts Part XIII withholding tax at 25% statutory, generally reduced to 5% to 15% under the Canada-US tax treaty (Articles X and IV(7) for ULC pass-through election). All cross-border transfers are subject only to FINTRAC reporting (e.g., large cash transactions over CAD 10,000, electronic funds transfer reporting thresholds, and specified terrorist property monitoring) and Statistics Canada balance-of-payments survey thresholds (typically CAD 30,000 for financial transactions).

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender, mandatory conversion, or retention-timeline obligation for foreign exchange receipts. Export and trading proceeds may be held in CAD or any foreign currency indefinitely. Only statistical and AML reporting duties apply, including monthly or quarterly BOP reporting for transactions above CAD 30,000 and FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reports for international remittances at or above CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential and clearing oversight coordinated with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), Payments Canada (for LYNX high-value settlement), and the British Columbia Securities Commission (BCSC) for securities market conduct

Wise Fit: High

Low_Feasibility for primary treasury clearing. Offshore digital electronic money institutions (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer, Revolut) may provide convenience accounts for minor disbursements, but they are not CDIC-insured banks, cannot access LYNX or CDSX settlement, cannot provide broker-dealer margin or prime-brokerage custody, and must be registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate for a British Columbia corporation: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may receive the federal small business rate of 9% and the British Columbia small business rate of 2%, producing an 11% combined rate on eligible active business income within applicable limits. Proprietary investment trading income and specified investment business income generally do not qualify for the small business deduction unless the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty or statutory exemption. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the recipient company satisfies the substantial ownership threshold and 15% for other qualifying corporate or individual recipients, but the applicable treaty must be reviewed for each recipient and ownership structure.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within 6 months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due 2 months after year-end; a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may receive a 3-month balance-payment period if the statutory conditions are met. Monthly installments are generally due by the last day of each month. Corporate taxpayers must retain books and records generally for at least 6 years from the end of the last taxation year to which they relate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a universal transaction-value threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is legally unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions, subject to statutory conditions. T106 reporting generally applies when the aggregate amount of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1 million. A Canadian master file is not generally a separate statutory filing requirement under Canada's ordinary transfer-pricing regime. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian ultimate parent or surrogate parent of a multinational enterprise group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, with equivalent reporting obligations for certain Canadian constituent entities.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR statutes. Economic substance remains relevant through the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose and limitation provisions, beneficial-ownership analysis, debt-versus-equity characterization, foreign-affiliate and FAPI rules, thin-capitalization rules, interest-limitation rules, and the factual determination of whether an arrangement is commercially genuine. A proprietary HFT structure should maintain evidence of Canadian decision-making, risk control, personnel, technology, capital, exchange access, execution authority, market risk, operational controls, and actual functions. Contractual allocation alone does not establish that the Canadian entity controls the relevant risks.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction for tax evasion may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to 2 years, or both. Civil reassessments, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, loss denial, and prosecution may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate income tax solely because of their office. Personal exposure may arise for knowing participation, aiding or abetting, conspiracy, obstruction, falsification, proceeds-of-crime conduct, or statutory director liability for source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts. Courts do not routinely pierce the corporate veil merely because a corporation is closely held. A court may order restitution or forfeiture where authorized; a travel ban is not an automatic tax sanction and generally requires a separate judicial or criminal process.
Failure to Remit Payroll or Withholding Taxes

Statutory sanction: The corporation may owe the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, late-remittance penalties, and information-return penalties. Under the Income Tax Act, directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted source deductions where statutory conditions are met, subject to the due-diligence defence and limitation rules.

Director personal liability: Personal assessment may attach to directors who were directors when the failure occurred. Resignation does not automatically eliminate historical exposure. Personal assets may be pursued after statutory collection steps, and criminal liability may arise for deliberate evasion or obstruction.
Transfer-Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under section 247, adjust income, deny or reduce expenses, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, and charge interest. Contemporaneous-documentation failures may eliminate penalty protection and trigger additional reporting penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors generally face corporate reassessment rather than automatic personal tax liability. Personal exposure may arise for intentional false statements, obstruction, fraudulent books, criminal tax conduct, or participation in a scheme. Supporting records should be approved and maintained independently of the foreign related party.
Shell Company, Treaty Abuse, or Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied under beneficial-ownership, limitation, principal-purpose, anti-conduit, or general anti-avoidance rules. The payer may be assessed for the full 25% Part XIII withholding, arrears interest, penalties, and information-reporting failures. Repeated or deliberate false certifications may support civil or criminal enforcement.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure for knowing participation, false declarations, fraud, money laundering, or obstruction. Corporate separateness normally remains respected absent abuse, sham, fraud, or statutory personal-liability provisions.
Unlicensed Dealer, Securities, Derivatives, or Exchange Activity

Statutory sanction: Possible consequences include cease-trade or compliance orders, registration restrictions, administrative monetary penalties, disgorgement, suspension or expulsion from a self-regulatory organization or exchange, and criminal prosecution for prohibited conduct under applicable federal and provincial securities legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be subject to provincial securities orders, administrative penalties, director-and-officer bans, civil claims, and criminal sanctions where they authorized, directed, or knowingly permitted the conduct. Imprisonment depends on the applicable statute and proven criminal offence; it is not automatic for an unregistered proprietary trading operation.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds-of-Crime Violations

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, publication of violations, asset seizure or forfeiture, prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act or Criminal Code, and sanctions-related enforcement. Penalties depend on the specific violation and culpability.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may be personally prosecuted for authorization, direction, consent, acquiescence, or wilful blindness. Criminal convictions can result in fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and court-ordered restrictions. A travel ban is not automatic but may arise from bail, sentencing, or immigration and border proceedings.
Books, Records, and Electronic Trading-Control Failure

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face denied deductions, reassessment on estimated income, record-keeping penalties, compliance orders, adverse evidentiary inferences, administrative penalties, and criminal charges for obstruction or falsification. Records must be retained for the statutory period and remain intelligible and accessible.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may incur personal regulatory or criminal liability where they direct or knowingly permit record destruction, obstruction, or false reporting. Corporate indemnification cannot eliminate statutory criminal liability and may be limited by public policy.
Thin Capitalization, Interest Limitation, and Hybrid ULC Failure

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions may be denied or limited under thin-capitalization and excessive-interest rules; withholding may be reassessed; treaty benefits may be denied; and penalties and interest may apply for inaccurate returns or deficient documentation.

Director personal liability: Ordinary technical errors generally create corporate liability only. Personal exposure may arise if the structure includes deliberate concealment, false reporting, fraudulent documentation, or knowing participation in an abusive arrangement.
GST/HST and Payroll Misclassification

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face reassessment of GST/HST, denied input tax credits, source-deduction assessments, penalties, interest, and information-return failures. Deliberate false claims may result in prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for certain unremitted source deductions and collected GST/HST under statutory rules. Criminal exposure may arise from deliberate false claims, fraud, or obstruction.
