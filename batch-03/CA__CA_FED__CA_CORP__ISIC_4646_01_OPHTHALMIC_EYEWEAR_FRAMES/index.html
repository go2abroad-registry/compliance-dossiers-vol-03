Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) – GST/HST account via My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html)

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities carrying on commercial activity in Canada must obtain a Business Number (BN) and register for GST/HST through Business Registration Online (BRO) or submit Form RC1. Non-resident registrants must complete Form RC391 (Application for Non-Resident Registration) or use the account opening step in My Business Account. Registration is mandatory if taxable supplies exceed the $30,000 small-supplier threshold; voluntary registration may be elected irrespective of threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property are zero-rated where the supplier sells and delivers/makes the goods available outside Canada and retains evidence of physical export. Services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated where the beneficial use is outside Canada and the service is not in connection with Canadian real property, vehicles, or tangible property. IT systems must maintain proof of foreign destination and payment receipt.

  • Customs Export Declaration
  • Bill of Lading
  • Inward Remittance Certificate

No national real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping is currently mandatory. CRA allows electronic invoices if they are accurate, complete, and retainable; GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE). Federal e-invoicing/clearance obligations for B2B are being phased in via the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework.

False zero-rating claims attract a gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated net tax, interest, and possible criminal fines up to CAD 10,000 and/or imprisonment. Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full currency convertibility in accordance with IMF Article VIII obligations. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are fully convertible with no exchange controls, no prior authorization requirements, and no restrictions on profit repatriation. The only material repatriation imposts are the Part XIII non-resident withholding taxes (generally 25% statutory, reduced by applicable tax treaties to 5% or 15% on dividends depending on the shareholder's jurisdiction and shareholding threshold) and any applicable provincial taxes. No exchange-control approval is required for dividend remittances, management fees, or capital distributions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for the surrender of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio into Canadian dollars, and no balance-of-payments repatriation obligation. Exporters may freely retain and disburse foreign currency revenue in CAD, USD, or other currencies. Financial institutions maintain FINTRAC reporting obligations for cross-border transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 (or its equivalent), but such obligations are administrative recordkeeping/reporting matters, not surrender or conversion requirements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); Canada does not maintain a dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration. Oversight of financial institutions is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while anti-money laundering surveillance is administered by FINTRAC. Foreign investment review is separately governed by the Investment Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium — legal and operational constraints apply. Offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) and fintech payment platforms may be used as supplementary payment channels only if they are properly registered with FINTRAC as money services businesses in Canada or hold an approved provincial licence. Effective July 2024, the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) requires payment service providers offering retail payment functions to persons in Canada to be registered with the Bank of Canada. A federal Canadian wholesale distributor must maintain a primary designated settlement account with a Tier 1 Canadian chartered bank; reliance on an offshore EMI as sole treasury rail is not prudently viable or fully compliant. Legal constraints include restrictions on accepting deposits, federal banking requirements, sanctions-screening obligations, and the inability to clear CAD-denominated instruments outside the Canadian payments system.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity capital is routed via SWIFT MT103 payment order from the non-resident shareholder to the Canadian corporate operational or escrow account. The payment instruction must carry an accurate narrative purpose, such as 'Capital contribution – direct investment in Canada', and, where requested by the receiving bank, a Statistics Canada Balance of Payments code covering inward direct investment equity. Verification protocol requires the sending bank to confirm remitter identity, the receiving bank to perform beneficiary name-match screening, and the corporate to produce a certified shareholder resolution, capital contribution agreement, and source-of-funds evidence. There is no Bank of Canada approval, no FX conversion obligation, and no capital-import licensing requirement. Standard correspondent routing applies for USD-denominated injections via the U.S. Fedwire or CHIPS network; CAD-denominated injections settle through Payments Canada's High Value Payment System or ACSS rails. For amounts above the prescribed threshold, the financial institution files a large-value cross-border transaction report with FINTRAC; documentary trail must therefore include the SWIFT gpi tracker or bank-issued confirmation as proof of settlement.Outbound trade settlements are structured through three principal rails. First, SWIFT MT103 (gpi) payment orders for direct international supplier invoices, with clear trade narratives referencing invoice, contract, and any relevant export documentation. Second, commercial letters of credit governed by UCP 600 for larger or contingent purchases from overseas optical-frame manufacturers, typically issued as irrevocable at-sight or usance LC through a Canadian Tier 1 issuing bank and advised through an overseas correspondent. Third, documentary collections under URC 522 for settled supply relationships seeking lower costs. Domestic CAD disbursements clear through Payments Canada ACSS and the High Value Payment System; USD disbursements clear via the bank's U.S. correspondent corresponding through Fedwire or CHIPS. All outbound wires must pass sanctions screening and FINTRAC reporting thresholds. Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Local File and Master File required if gross revenue exceeds CAD 50 million and the aggregate value of cross-border transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 5 million (or CAD 1 million for services); smaller taxpayers must maintain contemporaneous documentation if transactions exceed CAD 1 million (goods) or CAD 500,000 (services)

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) but applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles; transactions must have genuine commercial purpose and economic substance; the CRA may challenge arrangements lacking business rationale, and penalties apply for grossly negligent transfer pricing

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act (s. 239): fine of 50% to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and civil penalties up to 50% of the understated tax

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes (s. 227.1) if they failed to withhold or remit; civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment for willful evasion
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment (s. 245) disallowing tax benefits; penalties up to 25% of the tax benefit; potential criminal charges for fraudulent schemes

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for tax benefits obtained through artificial arrangements; court orders for restitution and potential travel bans
AML (Anti-Money Laundering) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for individuals and CAD 2 million for corporations; criminal penalties up to CAD 500,000 and 5 years imprisonment

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be personally liable for corporate AML violations if they knowingly participated or were negligent; travel bans and asset freezes possible
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty (s. 247(3)): 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (if adjustment exceeds CAD 5 million); interest on reassessed tax; potential criminal charges for gross negligence

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they authorized or acquiesced in the mispricing; civil restitution and potential imprisonment for fraud
