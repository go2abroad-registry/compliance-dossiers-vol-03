Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

      
Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

    

    

      
Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) service and My Business Account portal; the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), including the Canada Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) system, administers customs registration and export reporting. British Columbia provincial sales tax matters are administered by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance through its eTaxBC system.

        
E-government filing portal
A British Columbia unlimited liability corporation must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number (BN), normally through the corporate income tax account registration process using Form RC1 or CRA Business Registration Online, subject to portal eligibility. The corporation must register for a GST/HST account where mandatory registration applies or where voluntary registration is elected. The corporation must provide its legal name, incorporation jurisdiction, registered address, directors, principal business activity, fiscal year-end, authorized representative details, and non-resident information where applicable. The CRA issues a nine-digit BN and program account identifiers, including the corporate income tax account and, where applicable, the GST/HST account in the format BN plus RT0001. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, making taxable supplies in Canada, importing commercial goods, or otherwise meeting Canadian registration requirements must complete the relevant CRA registration. A non-resident supplier may be required to provide security before GST/HST registration or may use the simplified GST/HST registration regime where the statutory conditions apply. Customs export or import activity requires CBSA/CARM onboarding and applicable importer or exporter account setup. British Columbia PST registration is separate from GST/HST registration and is required where the corporation makes taxable sales, leases, or provides taxable software or related services in British Columbia and meets the provincial rules. ULC status does not create a separate federal tax identification number from the corporation's BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation

        
Available election forms
A Canadian ULC is a corporation for Canadian income tax purposes and is generally subject to Canadian corporate income tax on its taxable income; Canada has no general domestic check-the-box election that converts a Canadian corporation into a fiscally transparent entity. For United States federal tax purposes, an eligible Canadian ULC may generally elect under Treasury Regulations to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership, depending on ownership, using IRS Form 8832, subject to U.S. eligibility, classification, reporting, treaty, and foreign tax credit consequences. A ULC election may create hybrid-entity and branch- or entity-mismatch issues under the Canada-U.S. treaty, Canadian foreign affiliate rules, U.S. anti-hybrid rules, and applicable limitation-on-benefits and beneficial-ownership provisions. The ULC's Canadian legal personality, unlimited shareholder liability, Canadian corporate tax obligations, GST/HST obligations, payroll obligations, and provincial tax obligations are not eliminated by a U.S. classification election. No Canadian corporate tax check-the-box election is available merely because the ULC is unlimited liability.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies in the relevant single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, generally applies to a person that is not a small supplier for GST/HST purposes. The threshold and special rules differ for public service bodies, charities, non-resident suppliers, taxi and ride-sharing operators, and certain other categories. A business may register voluntarily below the threshold, subject to the statutory conditions. British Columbia PST has separate registration rules and generally does not use the federal CAD 30,000 GST/HST threshold as its universal registration test. The federal GST rate is 5%; HST rates vary by participating province. British Columbia PST is generally 7% on taxable goods, software, and specified services, subject to statutory exemptions and place-of-supply rules.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
For goods, GST/HST zero-rating generally requires that the goods be exported from Canada under the applicable Excise Tax Act rules, with the supplier retaining evidence that the goods were shipped or delivered outside Canada and that all statutory timing and documentary conditions were satisfied. Goods supplied to a recipient for export may qualify only where the prescribed export conditions are met; a customer's statement of foreign use alone is insufficient. For services, a supply to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the service is supplied to a person that is not resident in Canada and the service is not excluded by the Excise Tax Act. Relevant exclusions and conditions can apply to services related to real property or tangible personal property in Canada, services performed in Canada in respect of persons or property in Canada, personal services, certain transportation services, and services supplied to a Canadian permanent establishment or other Canadian presence. Cloud hosting, software access, database monitoring, API monitoring, and DevOps tools require a fact-specific place-of-supply and characterization analysis; electronic delivery or foreign customer billing does not automatically establish zero-rating. The supplier must verify customer residency, contractual recipient, place of supply, service performance, Canadian permanent establishment involvement, and statutory exclusions. Zero-rated supplies remain taxable supplies for GST/HST registration and input tax credit purposes. A registrant claiming GST/HST refunds or input tax credits must maintain contracts, invoices, customer residency evidence, service-access and usage records, payment records, tax-return workpapers, and evidence linking incurred Canadian GST/HST to commercial activities.

Canada and British Columbia do not generally impose a universal real-time electronic invoice clearance or government digital-stamping mandate for ordinary domestic B2B supplies, including cloud, software, database, and API monitoring services. A GST/HST registrant must issue compliant invoices or other documentary evidence when required by customers, retain records supporting tax collection and input tax credits, and maintain books and records in an accessible electronic or paper form for the statutory retention period. Invoices should identify the supplier, date, consideration, applicable GST/HST rate and amount, registration number where required, and sufficient information for input tax credit substantiation. GST/HST returns and remittances may be filed electronically through CRA systems, and large corporations and certain registrants may be subject to mandatory electronic filing. CRA electronic filing is not transaction-level fiscal clearance. Customs export declarations are submitted electronically through CBSA systems, including CARM where applicable, and must contain accurate tariff, valuation, origin, and shipment data. British Columbia PST invoices and records must satisfy provincial documentation requirements; no general BC PST real-time invoice clearance system applies.

      
Canada has no general foreign-exchange control rule requiring export proceeds to be repatriated to Canada within a prescribed number of days. Export proceeds may generally remain offshore, subject to ordinary tax, transfer-pricing, anti-money-laundering, sanctions, customs, financial reporting, and foreign-affiliate requirements. GST/HST zero-rating may be denied or reassessed where export, customer-residency, place-of-supply, or statutory exclusion conditions are not proven. The supplier may be assessed for the uncollected GST/HST, applicable interest, and civil penalties; penalties can apply for false statements, omissions, failure to file, failure to remit, repeated failure to file, gross negligence, or fraud. Gross-negligence penalties can generally be 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount, while fraud may lead to prosecution and court-imposed fines or imprisonment under applicable legislation. Interest and penalties may apply to reassessed GST/HST and improperly claimed input tax credits or refunds. CRA may reassess outside ordinary limitation periods in cases involving misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, willful blindness, or fraud, or where a waiver or statutory exception applies. Records should normally be retained for at least six years from the end of the relevant tax year, or longer where an objection, appeal, audit, investigation, capital property record, or other statutory requirement remains open.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

      
Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

    

    

      
Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
Canada imposes no general exchange controls and maintains full currency convertibility on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no statutory approvals or quantitative limits for dividend distributions, profit repatriation, or capital withdrawals by a non-resident shareholder of a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC). Profit remittances are subject on Canadian-source earnings to non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (subject to treaty relief), but are otherwise unrestricted. FINTRAC suspicious transaction reporting applies to large or unusual cross-border wire transfers, and any cash transaction exceeding CAD 10,000 requires mandatory record keeping and reporting. The absence of capital controls facilitates efficient treasury consolidation, intercompany funding, and dividend flow-back to foreign owners, provided transfer pricing and thin capitalization rules are observed.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
Statutory surrender and conversion obligations: None. Canada does not impose any mandatory timeline or conversion ratio for export proceeds. Exporters may retain and use foreign currency receipts freely, whether held in domestic foreign-currency accounts or in offshore bank accounts. However, cross-border electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are subject to FINTRAC monitoring and reporting, and cash payments exceeding CAD 10,000 require an MSB compliant declaration. All export and licensing receipts must be supported by commercial documentation (invoices, purchase orders, or executed cloud-services agreements) to evidence the source of funds in the event of an AML/ATF examination.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with foreign exchange monitoring and reporting administration vested in the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Bank of Canada Act and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA)

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails
            Fit: High
          

          
Feasibility rating: Medium. Canada does not grant a statutory 'EMI' passport for foreign electronic-money institutions. Any non-bank entity performing money transfer, distribution, issuing or redeeming money orders, or holding custodial client funds must be registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and, in British Columbia, may require a provincial Money Lending Act or Payment Services Act-related authorization depending on business scope. Offshore EMIs (e.g., FCA-authorised UK EMIs or EU/EEA EMIs) are not automatically recognized in Canada; they must either obtain a Canadian MSB registration or operate through a registered Canadian MSB as an agent. In practice, Schedule I Canadian banks may apply de-risking policies and decline treasury accounts for foreign unlicensed EMIs, making bank rails the primary corporate clearing mechanism for the ULC.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

      
      

        Non-resident ownership opacity: a BC ULC directly or indirectly owned by a foreign corporate umbrella or trust with layered shareholdings.
        
Root cause: Non-resident ownership opacity: a BC ULC directly or indirectly owned by a foreign corporate umbrella or trust with layered shareholdings.

        
Defense evidence: Complete BC Registry certified statement of registration and good standing; ULC articles and share register; a notarized organizational chart identifying all 25%+ beneficial holders; certified passports of natural persons; underlying trust deed or parent corporate registry extract; board resolution adopting the Canadian AML/ATF compliance policy; signed declarations of source of wealth; and, if available, a Canadian-legal opinion on the unlimited liability structure and tax classification.

      

      
      

        High-velocity cross-border SaaS subscription payments through Stripe and Mercury ACH, creating red flags for volume volatility or foreign counterparty patterns.
        
Root cause: High-velocity cross-border SaaS subscription payments through Stripe and Mercury ACH, creating red flags for volume volatility or foreign counterparty patterns.

        
Defense evidence: Business-model memorandum mapping the ISIC 6201 developer infrastructure and API monitoring commercial purpose; standard customer subscription or license agreements; Stripe and Mercury settlement statements for 12 months; a list of targeted markets; transfer-pricing documentation for intercompany payments; evidence of provisioning, support, and monitoring services delivered from Canada; board-approved AML risk rating and transaction monitoring summary.

      

      
      

        Significant inbound equity or loan capital from a jurisdiction listed on the FATF grey list, or any unexplained round-tripping of Treasury funds.
        
Root cause: Significant inbound equity or loan capital from a jurisdiction listed on the FATF grey list, or any unexplained round-tripping of Treasury funds.

        
Defense evidence: Fully executed shareholder loan or equity subscription agreement; board minutes approving the financing and specifying the economic substance of the ULC; invoice trail or capital call statements; solvency/asset statement from the foreign investor; sanctioned-party and PEP screening results on the remitter; regulatory-compliance certificate from the foreign financial institution; Canadian counsel legal opinion confirming the transaction is consistent with the Ontario/British Columbia ULC pass-through eligibility and does not trigger prohibited foreign influence.

      

      
    

    

      
Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        Compliant capital injection into the ULC operating account should use SWIFT MT103 wires from the foreign parent or investor into the bank account held at a Schedule I Canadian bank. Remittance information must separately identify the economic nature as: 'Equity Contribution – Foreign Direct Investment' or 'Intercompany Shareholder Loan – Funding of Canadian Capital'. Under ISO 20022 cross-border payments the purpose codes 'CAPI' (Capital transfer enterprises) or 'FDI' are acceptable. For sums above CAD 250,000, the receiving bank will typically require a board resolution, share subscription agreement or intercompany loan agreement, and confirmation of the ULC's BC registered office. The treasury dossier should include: source-of-funds explanation, ultimate beneficial owner diagram, anti-money laundering screening reports, and copies of FINTRAC large-value transaction reports when applicable.Operational trade disbursements are cleared through SWIFT MT103 (with Global Payments Innovation (gpi) tracking) for licensing, cloud infrastructure, subcontractor fees, and transaction settlements with non-Canadian vendors. For substantive infrastructure procurement (e.g., hardware co-location or enterprise storage), a Commercial Letter of Credit under UCP600 provides the required documentary guarantee. Domestic US-dollar and CAD payments are processed via Mercury ACH or Mercury wire transfers for payroll, tax, and subscription payments. All outgoing payments must include the ULC legal name, BC registered address, and a proper purpose description; payee-account validation is required to mitigate wire fraud. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 annual aggregate of cross-border related-party transactions triggering mandatory contemporaneous Local File/Master File documentation; below this threshold documentation is still required if CRA requests it and penalty relief requires reasonable efforts to determine arm's length prices

      
Economic substance scope: Canada applies GAAR (General Anti-Avoidance Rule) and statutory transfer pricing rules requiring economic substance in intercompany transactions; a BC ULC must demonstrate real operational substance, maintain a physical presence, employ personnel performing DevTools infrastructure functions, and avoid treaty-shopping or shell arrangements. The CRA may disregard transactions lacking commercial rationale under section 247 of the Income Tax Act.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion and Willful Blindness
        
Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax; criminal prosecution with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years under the Income Tax Act; CRA may also assess interest and penalties on reassessment

        
Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in tax evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid taxes, penalties, and interest; the corporate veil can be pierced for fraud, resulting in personal monetary restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment

      

      
      

        Shell Company and Sham Transactions
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR to deny treaty benefits, reassess the company as resident, impose full 25% withholding on distributions, and assess penalties up to 50% of the tax benefit; potential criminal charges for fraud or misrepresentation

        
Director personal liability: Directors may be held liable for taxes and penalties where the corporate form is a sham; courts may impose personal liability for unjust enrichment, restitution, and criminal fines; travel restrictions and imprisonment possible in serious cases

      

      
      

        Transfer Pricing Manipulation
        
Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing reassessment, add-back of income, and a 10% penalty (subsection 247(3)) on the adjustment amount where documentation requirements are not met or arm's length prices not reasonably determined; interest accrues from the original filing due date

        
Director personal liability: Directors who approved or signed false transfer pricing records may be personally liable under the doctrine of culpable negligence; criminal and civil penalties for fraudulent transfer pricing can be enforced against individuals; travel bans and imprisonment apply where fraud is proven

      

      
      

        AML and Economic Sanctions Violations
        
Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act of up to C$1,000,000 for corporations; criminal penalties for violation of Canadian sanctions laws (Global Affairs Canada) with fines up to C$25,000 per violation and imprisonment up to 2 years; seizure of assets

        
Director personal liability: Directors or officers who knowingly cause the corporation to violate AML or sanctions laws are personally liable to criminal prosecution, imprisonment up to 10 years, fines, and travel restrictions; civil liability for losses and disgorgement of profits is possible

      

      
      

        Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Tax
        
Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the full unwithheld tax, plus interest, penalties (5% + 1% per month up to 12 months), and gross negligence penalties of 50% if intentional; CRA can audit and reassess retroactively

        
Director personal liability: Directors are statutorily liable under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for the corporation's failure to withhold and remit non-resident tax if they knew or ought to have known; personal liability includes civil collection, asset freeze, travel ban, and imprisonment for knowing omission
