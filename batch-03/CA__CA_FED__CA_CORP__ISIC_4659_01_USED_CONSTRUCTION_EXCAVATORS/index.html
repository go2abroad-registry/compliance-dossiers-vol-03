Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — My Business Account portal (canada.ca/taxes; corporate registration via canada.ca/business-registration); Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for the Import/Export (RM) and customs program accounts.

E-government filing portal

A federal business corporation formed under the Canada Business Corporations Act receives its 9-digit Business Number (BN) automatically from CRA through Corporations Canada at incorporation. For a foreign-owned corporation or a non-resident registrant, the shareholder or authorized director must file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) with the CRA International and Large Business Directorate, attaching the certificate of incorporation, articles, director list, proof of Canadian registered agent address, and a power of attorney where a Canadian tax representative acts. The GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), and import/export (RM) program accounts are added on the same form; with a Canadian address and completed RC1, CRA issues the BN within 2-6 weeks. If the corporation exports or imports goods with a value exceeding CAD 2,000 per shipment, it must additionally obtain a CBSA client/account link via CERS (Canadian Export Reporting System) before shipment.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada allows no US-style check-the-box or partnership classification election; every corporation is conclusively a separate taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act (R.S.C. 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)). Available elections for a non-resident-owned wholesale trading corporation: (i) non-calendar fiscal period election under ITA s. 249.1; (ii) ITA s. 216 election for net-basis taxation of Canadian rental income; (iii) ITA s. 219 branch tax election to treat branch profits as paid-up dividends; (iv) ETA s. 252.1 'specified registrant' election allowing streamlined GST/HST input tax credit recovery by non-residents; (v) no elective hybrid-mismatch relief: ITA ss. 18.2/18.3 (effective for 2023+) mandatorily deny deductions on structured double-dip and import-of-loss arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — annual worldwide taxable supplies (other than financial services) made by a person carrying on business in Canada (ETA s. 148). A non-resident wholesaler carrying on business in Canada crosses the small-supplier threshold on a rolling four-quarter basis and must register under ETA s. 240; the threshold does not protect a person importing taxable goods for commercial use or a person supplying taxable real property in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Used construction excavators and rollers qualify for zero-rating under ETA Schedule VI section 21 (exported tangible personal property) where the seller ships the machinery out of Canada within 60 days of delivery to the purchaser, title and risk pass to a non-resident purchaser before departure, and the seller retains documented proof of physical export. The equipment may have operated in Canada prior to export; prior Canadian use does not disqualify the supply if the sale to the non-resident occurs before export. Related services performed for the non-resident buyer (freight, brokerage, inspection, and market-making services) are zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI Part V only where the recipient is a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and the service is not performed wholly in Canada in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada; separately invoiced pre-export inspection, repair, or refurbishment performed on the machinery inside Canada remains taxable at 5%. The seller must retain freight forwarder reports, CBSA CERS confirmation, ocean B/L with Canadian port of exit, and payment evidence to withstand the CRA 'evidence of export' test.

  • CERS Export Declaration (electronic filing via the Canadian Export Reporting System, the successor of the legacy B13A, mandatory for any exported shipment with FOB value exceeding CAD 2,000)
  • Commercial Invoice in CBSA C17 Canada Customs Invoice format showing seller, foreign purchaser, HS classification 8429.52 / 8429.59, used-condition disclosure, unit prices, and country of origin
  • Bill of Lading (through or destination B/L) or Air Waybill evidencing departure from a Canadian port of exit to the named foreign consignee
  • Cargo Control Document (CCD) or e-manifest (eHBL) reference number assigned by the carrier at the Canadian gateway
  • Packing list identifying each unit by serial number, chassis/UIN, hour meter reading, and physical dimensions/weight
  • Duly executed Sale Purchase Contract / invoice assignment recording the secondhand status of the excavators and the non-resident purchaser's legal identity
  • Inward Remittance Confirmation — SWIFT MT103 credit advice or letter of credit negotiation statement evidencing cross-border payment of the invoiced amount

No federal mandate for real-time fiscal clearance, government API pre-clearance, or digital stamping of invoices. Standard paper, PDF, and EDI invoices are accepted where they contain the content elements of ETA s. 3 and Canada Customs and Trade Administration (CTA) E1 standard data. Canada is deploying the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework / PEPPOL BIS 3.0 e-invoicing interoperability on a voluntary basis for business-to-business and business-to-government transmission. GST/HST returns (Form GST34) must be remitted electronically through GST/HST NETFILE once annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5 million; export declarations must be transmitted electronically to CBSA via CERS, which is an export reporting clearance rather than an invoice clearance.

CRA and CBSA jointly conduct Export Verification audits; zero-rating substantiation records must be retained for six years from the end of the taxation year (ITA/ETA s. 298). Failure to produce credible export evidence results in retroactive reversal of zero-rating and assessment of 5% GST on the full gross proceeds, compounded prescribed interest (CRA quarterly prescribed rate), and a failure-to-remit penalty of up to 6% of the assessed balance under ETA s. 280. Fraudulent documentation or willful misstatement invokes gross-negligence penalties under ETA s. 285 equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the understated tax, criminal prosecution under ETA s. 327 (fines up to CAD 25,000 per count and/or imprisonment up to five years), and an indefinitely extended reassessment limitation period. The 180-day repatriation benchmark in this schema reflects the standard commercial/letter-of-credit tenor used by CRA transfer-pricing auditors (ITA s. 247) when testing whether export proceeds were paid and whether a Canadian person received adequate consideration; Canadian law does not impose a separate statutory deadline for export-proceeds repatriation equivalent to foreign-exchange-control regimes.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility for both capital and current account transactions. There are no exchange controls, licensing, or quantitative limits on the repatriation of profits, dividends, or proceeds from the sale of assets by a Canadian business corporation to non-resident shareholders. All transactions must be reported to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for tax purposes and, where applicable, to Statistics Canada for balance-of-payments purposes. Withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act applies to dividends paid to non-residents, subject to relief under applicable tax treaties. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely traded in the interbank market, and a Canadian corporation may hold, convert, and remit funds in CAD or foreign currency without prior regulatory approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement for exporting entities in Canada to surrender export proceeds to the Bank of Canada or any other public authority. No mandatory conversion ratio or prescribed timeline exists for repatriation of export receipts. Exporters are required to retain business records for tax and customs purposes, to report exports to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via the Canadian Export Reporting System, and to comply with anti-money laundering (AML) record-keeping obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) when receiving wire transfers from abroad. Retained foreign-currency proceeds may be kept in domestic or offshore bank accounts, subject to Canadian tax reporting on foreign income and the Common Reporting Standard (CRS).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) pursuant to the Bank of Canada Act, R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2; as Canada maintains a fully floating exchange rate regime, the Bank of Canada acts as monetary authority and issuer of legal tender, while exchange control and reporting functions are distributed among the Department of Finance, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) exhibit low-to-medium direct viability for serving a Canadian business corporation in the used construction excavator remarketing sector. Under Canadian law, only banks, trust companies, and credit unions regulated by OSFI or provincial authorities may accept deposits. Offshore EMIs cannot provide Canadian deposit-taking services. An EMI may offer cross-border payment services if it is registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC, but Canadian corporate clients typically require a settlement account at a Tier-1 Canadian bank for compliant clearing. The use of an offshore EMI for trade settlements may trigger enhanced scrutiny from correspondent banks due to uncertain AML/CTF supervision, thus rendering a hybrid structure (Canadian bank settlement account + EMI for e-commerce/fronting) only marginally feasible.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injections for a federated Canadian business corporation follow SWIFT MT103 wire transfers from the shareholder's foreign bank account to a designated Canadian-domiciled escrow or operational account. The incoming wire must clearly specify the benefiting entity (full legal name and account number), the purpose of payment as 'Equity Contribution' or 'Shareholder Loan' (purpose code 'G20' for goods trade revenue is not appropriate), and must include comprehensive references to the underlying corporate resolution or loan agreement. The Canadian financial institution will validate the sender's SWIFT/BIC, verify the source of funds under AML/KYC rules, and may request the following: (i) a certified copy of the board of directors' resolution authenticating the injection, (ii) an executed shareholder loan agreement with interest rate and repayment schedule, or subscribed share capital documentation, (iii) proof that the foreign entity is validly registered and authorized, (iv) a funds declaration demonstrating untainted provenance, and (v) for amounts above CAD 10,000, submission of a Financial Transaction Report (Large Cash Transaction Report or Suspicious Transaction Report threshold where applicable) under FINTRAC. Direct deposits of physical currency are not compliant and are prohibited for corporate accounts.Outbound disbursements for used construction excavator procurement from overseas suppliers are cleared via SWIFT MT103 (wire transfer) or via commercial letters of credit (LC) with sight or term drafts. For supplier markets requiring high assurance (e.g., Japan or Europe), a confirmed irrevocable LC issued by a Canadian Tier-1 bank is preferred; the LC will incorporate the ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600) rules. For smaller, repeated trade disbursements, a direct MT103 wire transfer with a clear purpose code (e.g., 'G20' for settled goods) is acceptable. All outbound transfers must be substantiated with commercial invoices, packing lists, bills of lading, and customs documentation retained per the Income Tax Act and PCMLTFA record-keeping requirements. Specifically of concern: used heavy machinery transactions should be settled only after verification of the equipment's condition and title (e.g., removal of any liens) to avoid trade-based fraud. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal Part I general corporate rate for a non-CCPC after the 10% provincial abatement and the 13% general rate reduction; combined federal-provincial headline rates generally range from approximately 23% to 31% depending on the province of permanent establishment.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner, subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. For a corporation that is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation, the final balance of Part I federal and provincial tax is due no later than two months after the fiscal year-end; interest on unpaid balances accrues from that date.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CA$1,000,000 aggregate annual fair market value of reportable transactions with a single non-resident non-arm's length person triggers Form T106 filing. Full contemporaneous arm's length documentation (functional, comparability, and economic analysis) is expected where the Canadian entity's gross revenue exceeds CA$50,000,000 or reportable specified transactions exceed CA$5,000,000; CRA may impose transfer pricing penalties where documentation is deficient.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations. The equivalent substance and anti-avoidance framework comprises the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under section 245 of the Income Tax Act, the transfer pricing penalty and adjustment rules under section 247, treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions, and CRA's requirement that a Canadian entity maintain real functions, assets, and risks in Canada. A purported Canadian operating company acting solely as an invoicing shell without employees, premises, inventory control, or decision-making risks is exposed to GAAR recharacterization, denial of treaty benefits, and transfer pricing adjustments.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Unreported Offshore Sales

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under section 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; civil gross negligence penalty of 50% of the tax understated under subsection 163(2); daily compound interest on all unpaid taxes; and potential FINTRAC referrals and asset seizure.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns or direct tax-evasion schemes are personally exposed to criminal liability, restitution orders, and statutory assessments under section 160 for transfers to non-arm's length persons. In serious cases, a court order may restrict the director's ability to leave Canada while charges are pending, and tax debts can render individuals ineligible for certain travel authorizations and immigration remedies.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Sham Documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess at arm's length prices under subsection 247(2); transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment applies under subsection 247(3), including where the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts to determine arm's length prices; interest and gross negligence penalties can be stacked on the reassessed amount.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve false Form T106 filings or issue false purchase and sale invoices may be pursued under civil fraud principles and, where misrepresentations are willful, under criminal tax evasion provisions. The transfer pricing penalty itself is a corporate obligation, but directors face personal garnishment and asset-freeze orders for unremitted liabilities and for fraudulent conveyance where corporate funds are stripped to related entities.
Disguised Equity Distributions and Part XIII Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: Failure to withhold and remit Part XIII tax results in assessment of 25% of the gross payment, daily compound interest, and late-filing penalties; excessive interest deductions are denied under the thin-capitalization and excess interest and financing expenses limitation rules; mischaracterized payments are reclassified as deemed dividends subject to full 25% statutory withholding.

Director personal liability: Under section 227.1, directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII and source withholding amounts; CRA may garnishee personal bank accounts, register certificates of debt with the Federal Court, and place liens on personal property. Repeated deliberate non-withholding is treated as willful participation and may lead to criminal charges and travel restrictions.
AML / Trade-Based Money Laundering and Shell Company Abuse

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties up to CA$500,000 per violation may be imposed on a corporation; under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code, laundering the proceeds of crime is an indictable offence punishable by imprisonment for up to ten years; the underlying property is subject to restraint and forfeiture, and financial institutions are required to freeze funds upon lawful order.

Director personal liability: Directors and beneficial owners who orchestrate trade-based laundering expose themselves to criminal conviction, personal asset forfeiture, and imprisonment. A conviction triggers a serious criminal record, domestic and international travel restrictions, loss of corporate directorship eligibility where disqualification is ordered, and inadmissibility consequences where the individual seeks to enter other jurisdictions.
