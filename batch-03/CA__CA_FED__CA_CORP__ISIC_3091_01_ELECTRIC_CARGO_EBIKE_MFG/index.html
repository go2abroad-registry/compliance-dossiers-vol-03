Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — Business Registration Online (BRO) portal at canada.ca; GST/HST NETFILE and My Business Account (MyBA) digital services.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated entity carrying on business in Canada obtains a statutory 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts (e.g., GST/HST account RC0001, Corporate Income Tax RC0001) by registering via Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) with the CRA International Tax Services Office when the entity has no Canadian premises. Where the foreign investor incorporates a federal/provincial Business Corporation, Corporations Canada (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada) issues articles of incorporation and a corporate number; the entity then files Form RC1 to create the BN and the GST/HST account. Non-resident registration requires evidence that the entity carries on business in Canada or enters Canada to perform a taxable supply; mandatory registration arises once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD $30,000 in the preceding four calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold).

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation is taxed at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act (federal and provincial rates combined); no pass-through check-the-box election exists. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations are taxed on Canadian-source business income plus a 25% Part XIV branch tax on after-tax Canadian business earnings (treaty-reduced where applicable). GST/HST applies separately at 5% federal GST, with provincial HST/PST/QST layered in each province. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation is taxed at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act (federal and provincial rates combined); no pass-through check-the-box election exists. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations are taxed on Canadian-source business income plus a 25% Part XIV branch tax on after-tax Canadian business earnings (treaty-reduced where applicable). GST/HST applies separately at 5% federal GST, with provincial HST/PST/QST layered in each province. No federal tax-exempt zone applies to the manufacturing of electric cargo bicycles; standard manufacturing activity is fully taxable.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box classification exists; tax characterization is statutory under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act. Available elections include: s. 216(1) election for non-residents with rental or timber royalty income; s. 217 election for non-residents with Canadian employment income; s. 219 (Part XIV) branch tax filing election; s. 256 associated-corporation election/determination; GST/HST s. 150 election for closely related corporations to treat supplies as deemed consideration; and GST/HST s. 156 election for related registrants to waive tax on intercompany supplies. Hybrid mismatch measures are imposed rather than elective, including the Excess Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL) under Income Tax Act ss. 18.2 and 18.21.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 — the small-supplier threshold measured on total worldwide taxable supplies in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and the current quarter; a supplier exceeding the threshold in the course of a commercial activity in Canada must register for GST/HST, including non-resident suppliers soliciting or fulfilling orders in Canada, performing services in Canada, or supplying real property in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

An electric cargo bicycle supply qualifies for 0% GST/HST zero-rating under Part V, Schedule VI (s. 1) of the Excise Tax Act where: (i) the supplier sells the goods in the ordinary course of business for consideration in money, title transfers at a place outside Canada, and the goods are exported as soon as is reasonably possible without further processing in Canada; or (ii) the supplier delivers the goods to a common carrier, consignee, or warehouse for direct export outside Canada. Offshore services supplied in respect of exported goods (e.g., freight handling, logistics, and certain warranty services) may be zero-rated under Schedule VI ss. 5.1, 6, or 7. The exporter must retain contemporaneous documentary proof of export to support the zero-rating claim and thereby recover all input tax credits (ITCs) embedded in inputs, yielding an effective refund of Canadian GST/HST.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (CAED / Form B13A or electronic ACI export reporting) for goods valued at CAD $2,000 or more
  • Commercial invoice showing exporter/importer identity, HS classification (e.g., HS 8711.60 / 8711.9x), quantity, unit value, currency, and Incoterms
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical movement of goods from Canada to a foreign destination
  • Carrier certificate, forwarder's receipt, or customs broking proof of export with date of shipment within the reporting period
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank transfer records evidencing receipt of export proceeds in the exporter's Canadian financial account

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory e-invoicing clearance, or digital stamping regime exists at the federal level; e-invoicing (PEPPOL, EDI, XML) is voluntary in the commercial B2B environment. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD $1,000,000 must file returns electronically (GST/HST NETFILE) and remit via electronic payment; prescribed return contents include the BN, reporting period, net tax, and revenue amounts. Customs export documentation is processed electronically through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) platform and the Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) system, but individual sales invoices are not subject to CRA pre-clearance.

The CRA may reassess within the standard four-year reassessment window (Excise Tax Act s. 298) to deny zero-rating where export evidence is insufficient, thereby charging uncollected GST/HST plus compound arrears interest. Civil penalties include: s. 280(1) failure to file — 5% of the tax plus 1% per month (maximum 12 months); s. 285.1 false statements or omissions — the greater of CAD $250 and 25% of the net tax understatement; gross negligence penalty under s. 285 — CAD $100 plus 25% of tax that would have been payable. Directors may be jointly and severally liable under s. 323 for amounts not remitted. Criminal tax evasion under Income Tax Act ss. 238/239 carries fines of 50% to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to five years; CRA International and Large Business Audit Division and the CBSA routinely conduct export-verification audits and reciprocal customs data matching to detect fictitious export and circular transaction schemes, with retrospective clawbacks extending to interest and penalty reassessments beyond the normal limitation period where misrepresentation is found.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility of the Canadian dollar (CAD) on both current and capital accounts and imposes no exchange-control approval regime. Profit remittance in the form of dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, and capital repatriation is unrestricted but subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%, frequently treaty-reduced to 5%–15% depending on income class and residence) and to transfer-pricing scrutiny under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, including annual Form T106 disclosure for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Inbound equity injections require no Bank of Canada or ISED approval unless a transaction triggers the net-benefit review threshold under the Investment Canada Act (enterprise-value thresholds updated annually); ordinary capital funding of a start-up manufacturing subsidiary is outside review.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory remittance deadline on export receipts. Exporters may lawfully hold, convert, and deploy export proceeds in any currency indefinitely. The only compliance obligations are FINTRAC large-transaction reporting at the C$10,000 threshold, FINTRAC money-service-business reporting where applicable, and CBSA cross-border currency or bearer-instrument reporting for movements of C$10,000 or more on physical crossing of the border.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); banking prudential supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF regulation by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); foreign-investment review by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) under the Investment Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-to-Medium as a primary operational bank; viable only as a secondary collections and payouts channel. Any offshore digital EMI providing payment services to a Canadian resident must register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA, maintain a Canadian AML/ATF compliance program, and appoint a qualified compliance officer. Tier-1 Canadian banks generally decline to sponsor unregistered or lightly regulated offshore EMIs due to correspondent, settlement, and sanctions risk. A FINTRAC-registered EMI, including a licensed EU/UK EMI operating into Canada under a registration passport, may open a limited corporate payment account for e-bike customer deposits and small supplier payouts, but it cannot replace a Schedule I commercial bank clearing account. The forthcoming Retail Payment Activities Act will add a further federal registration, risk-management, and safeguarding mandate for all non-bank payment service providers.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory de minimis threshold exists for the contemporaneous documentation obligation under ITA section 247(3), which applies to all non-arm's length cross-border transactions. However, the separate T106 information return filing is triggered where transactions in any category exceed CAD 1,000,000. The OECD Country-by-Country reporting regime applies to groups with consolidated revenues of CAD 1 billion or more and carries corresponding Master File and Local File expectations in CRA risk-assessment manuals

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations code, but the federal Income Tax Act applies a statutory general anti-avoidance rule under section 245, supplemented by common-law economic substance and commercial purpose doctrines. A federal corporation without employees, premises, business operations, or board-level decision-making in Canada may be re-characterized under the central management and control test, treated as a shell for treaty or GAAR purposes, and denied treaty benefits under limitation-on-benefits clauses

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Filing

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty of 50% of the understated tax for gross negligence under ITA section 163.2; upon indictment, a fine not exceeding 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years; summary conviction carries a fine up to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment for up to 2 years less a day

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, or senior managers who authorized or acquiesced in the evasion may be prosecuted personally; upon conviction, the court may impose criminal restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment, and CRA may pierce the corporate veil for fraudulent transfers
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure / Mis-Pricing

Statutory sanction: CRA is entitled to re-assess taxable income using an arm's length price; a penalty of 10% of the transfer-pricing adjustment applies when the taxpayer cannot provide adequate documentation; gross negligence penalties and interest may also be imposed

Director personal liability: Directors generally are not personally liable for transfer-pricing reassessments if the corporation maintained adequate records; however, if a director signs or directs fabricated transfer-pricing analyses, they may face fines, civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment for documents-fraud or tax evasion
Part XIII Withholding Tax Defaults

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the payor corporation for the unwithheld amount, statutory interest, a 10% failure-to-withhold penalty if the amount was paid after compliance demand, and a 20% penalty for repeated failures; withheld amounts are held in a deemed trust in favor of the Crown

Director personal liability: Every director who failed to cause the corporation to remit source deductions and Part XIII withholding is jointly and severally liable under ITA section 227.1; CRA may pursue director assets, and a criminal conviction can trigger civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping / Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke the GAAR to deny the tax benefit, reallocate income to the true economic owner, and impose the gross-negligence penalty of 50%; treaty benefits may be denied under limitation-on-benefits provisions; the corporation may also be dissolved under the Canada Business Corporations Act for repeated non-compliance

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for false declarations in incorporation filings and for fraudulent conveyance if the shell launders funds; courts may order civil disgorgement, restitution, travel bans under the criminal process, and imprisonment for up to 5 years where proceeds of crime are involved
