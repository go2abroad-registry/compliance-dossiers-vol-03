Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online and My Business Account; British Columbia Ministry of Finance, including eTaxBC, administers British Columbia provincial sales tax and related provincial tax accounts.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian ULC incorporated in British Columbia generally obtains a nine-digit CRA Business Number through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1, Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts. The corporation then registers the applicable CRA program accounts, including RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll deductions, and RM for import-export activities. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada may apply for a Business Number and the relevant GST/HST, corporate income tax, payroll, and import-export accounts through Business Registration Online, Form RC1, or the CRA Non-Resident Registration Web Form. The applicant must provide legal name, jurisdiction and date of incorporation, registered office, Canadian business address, directors, authorized representative, activities, expected revenue, fiscal period, and banking or customs information where requested. The GST/HST account is effective from the required registration date or an earlier voluntary registration date accepted by the CRA. A business making taxable supplies in British Columbia may separately register for a provincial PST number with the British Columbia Ministry of Finance through eTaxBC or the applicable provincial registration process. A CRA Business Number is not itself a GST/HST registration; the RT program account identifies the GST/HST account.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada generally treats a British Columbia ULC as a corporation subject to corporate income tax; Canada has no United States-style check-the-box election permitting a Canadian ULC to elect disregarded-entity or partnership status for Canadian tax purposes. For United States federal tax purposes, an eligible Canadian ULC may generally elect its classification under United States Treasury Regulations section 301.7701-3 by filing IRS Form 8832, subject to United States ownership and eligibility rules. The United States default classification depends on the number and status of owners. A United States election does not change the ULC's Canadian legal form or Canadian entity-level tax status. Canadian hybrid-mismatch rules, treaty limitations, foreign tax credit rules, and anti-avoidance provisions must be reviewed where the United States classification differs from the Canadian classification. Canadian corporations may make other specific statutory elections, such as elections under section 85 of the Income Tax Act for qualifying transfers, but these are not entity-classification elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST registration is generally required when a person is no longer a small supplier, ordinarily when worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or exceed CAD 30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters. Associated persons and specified supplies may affect the calculation. Voluntary registration is generally available before the threshold is exceeded, subject to CRA conditions. There is no single equivalent CAD 30,000 threshold for all British Columbia PST obligations; PST registration depends on the nature and location of taxable sales, leasing, use, or provision of taxable goods and services. A restaurant or prepared-food operator must analyze the specific provincial PST treatment of prepared food, delivery charges, equipment, software, and other inputs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were exported, including evidence of delivery or shipment outside Canada and compliance with applicable documentary requirements. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated only where the specific exported-service provisions apply. The analysis depends on the recipient's residency, the place and manner of performance, the nature of the service, and exclusions for services relating to Canadian real property, tangible personal property situated in Canada, personal services physically performed in Canada, and other excluded categories. Digital, marketing, platform, and administrative services require separate classification. Food preparation and delivery performed for Canadian customers in British Columbia are ordinarily domestic taxable supplies and are not converted into zero-rated exports merely because a customer uses a foreign-owned delivery platform or an offshore payment account. GST registrants may claim input tax credits for eligible GST incurred in commercial activities, subject to documentary and timing requirements. A non-resident person may qualify for limited rebates only under specific statutory programs; there is no general export refund solely because the supplier is non-resident.

  • Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) export declaration or other applicable Canada Border Services Agency export reporting record, unless an exemption applies
  • Commercial invoice identifying exporter, consignee, description, quantity, value, currency, and delivery terms
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier manifest, or equivalent transport documentation
  • Proof of export or delivery outside Canada, such as carrier records, customs exit evidence, delivery confirmation, or foreign import documentation
  • Purchase order, contract, customer invoice, and correspondence establishing the non-resident recipient and the nature of the exported supply

  • Inward remittance or banking evidence where relevant to commercial substantiation, audit support, or a foreign exchange control requirement; Canada does not generally require an Inward Remittance Certificate as a condition for ordinary GST zero-rating

Canada has no general nationwide real-time electronic invoicing or pre-clearance mandate for ordinary private-sector GST/HST transactions. A registrant must issue GST/HST documentation containing the information required by the Excise Tax Act and its regulations, retain reliable books and records, and provide prescribed invoices or receipts when requested by customers or the CRA. Electronic invoices and records are generally acceptable if they preserve integrity, accessibility, readability, and auditability. CRA digital services include Business Registration Online and My Business Account; they do not constitute universal real-time invoice clearance. British Columbia does not generally require universal invoice-by-invoice fiscal clearance for private businesses. Separate electronic customs filing requirements may apply through the Canada Border Services Agency, including the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), and platform, payment processor, consumer-protection, and record-retention requirements may apply independently.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days, and there is no general foreign-exchange-control rule imposing a mandatory 180-day remittance deadline on Canadian exporters. Export zero-rating is subject to CRA audit, reassessment, documentary verification, and recovery of improperly uncollected GST or improperly claimed input tax credits or rebates. The CRA may assess the tax, deny input tax credits or rebates, charge arrears interest, and impose penalties for failure to collect, remit, file, maintain records, or provide adequate evidence. A false statement or omission attributable to fraud or gross negligence may attract a penalty generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit or rebate, subject to the applicable statutory calculation. Repeated or intentional non-compliance may result in gross-negligence or criminal penalties, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under the Excise Tax Act. False customs declarations may result in Canada Border Services Agency Administrative Monetary Penalties, seizure, forfeiture, reassessment of duties or taxes, and prosecution. British Columbia PST assessed as unpaid or improperly exempted may also be reassessed with interest and provincial penalties. Records supporting zero-rating should generally be retained for the statutory record-retention period, commonly six years from the end of the relevant period, unless a longer period is required by law, objection, appeal, audit, investigation, or litigation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada applies a free-floating CAD with full current- and capital-account convertibility; no exchange-control approval is required for inbound equity, inbound loans, trade settlement, or profit repatriation. Profit remittance from a British Columbia ULC is restricted only by Canadian withholding-tax compliance: Part XIII tax (default 25% on dividends) and possible treaty recharacterization benefits or penalties under the applicable Canada income-tax convention, not by FX rationing.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, mandatory conversion, or deadline applies to export proceeds in Canada. Exporters may retain receipts in CAD or foreign currency; FINTRAC cross-border reporting applies to movements of funds over CAD 10,000; corporate-tax and GST/HST reporting still require the underlying receipts to be recognized and documented.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-moderate for a standalone offshore EMI; it cannot substitute for a Schedule I bank account because taking deposits or clearing CAD requires Canadian banking authorization. An offshore digital EMI may be acceptable only as a secondary payout or collection rail if it is registered with FINTRAC as an MSB and operates through a Canadian partner bank; the primary treasury relationship should remain at a tier-1 domestic bank to support DoorDash and UberEats payouts and FINTRAC-compliant reconciliation.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value per reportable transaction category with non-arm's-length non-residents in the taxation year triggers the mandatory T106 information return and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation; Country-by-Country Report filing is required only for MNE groups with consolidated group revenue exceeding CAD 900,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada and British Columbia have no statutory Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to UAE ESR, but substance is enforced through: (i) the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245) which denies benefits of avoidance transactions lacking economic substance; (ii) the common law sham doctrine and 'central management and control' residence rules; (iii) treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions (Canada-US Treaty Art. XXIX-A) requiring genuine business purpose; and (iv) provincial licensing and payroll records creating a rebuttable presumption of a permanent establishment in BC. A ULC that contracts for delivery sales but has no employees, no leased kitchen premises, no equipment and no operational management in BC is fatally deficient.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.239(1), tax evasion is punishable by a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 2 years on summary conviction; CRA may also reassess beyond the normal 4-year limitation period and impose a 50% gross negligence penalty under s.163(2); GST/HST evasion carries additional penalties under the Excise Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct, acquiesce in or benefit from the evasion are prosecuted as parties under Criminal Code s.21; civil restitution orders, freezing injunctions, departure bans as bail conditions, and personal liability under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted payroll source deductions and GST/HST apply; fraudulent conveyance actions against shareholders under BC law are available to CRA.
Shell Company, Sham and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA will disregard the entity under the sham doctrine, reassess the foreign parent as having a permanent establishment in Canada, disallow treaty benefits under the LOB clause, apply GAAR under s.245 to recharacterize the arrangement, and assess the full Canadian tax plus interest, penalties of up to 50% of the avoided tax, and potential Part XIII withholding on deemed dividends.

Director personal liability: Under the BC Business Corporations Act, shareholders of an unlimited liability company are jointly and severally liable on the corporation's dissolution for corporate debts; directors face personal liability for unpaid wages under the BC Employment Standards Act and for unremitted source deductions under ITA s.227.1; courts may issue travel bans pending criminal prosecution and civil asset forfeiture orders.
AML, Money Laundering and FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation may be imposed on corporations; criminal money laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31 carries imprisonment up to 10 years on indictment and unlimited fines; BC's Civil Forfeiture Act permits in rem forfeiture of the kitchen assets and bank balances.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized, directed or acquiesced in the laundering are personally criminally liable; restraint and freezing orders under Criminal Code s.462.33, Canadian departure bans, large personal fines and restitution orders are routinely sought; directorial disqualification and personal civil liability follow a conviction.
