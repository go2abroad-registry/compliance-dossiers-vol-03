Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a federally incorporated business corporation is a resident of Canada under ITA s. 250(4) and is subject to Part I federal corporate income tax at the general rate of 15% (2024), plus provincial corporate tax (e.g., Ontario 11.5%-12.2%) where permanent establishment exists. There is no pass-through; the corporation files a T2 Corporate Income Tax Return. Export sales of scrap are taxable income but zero-rated for GST/HST.

Available election forms

Canada has no 'check-the-box' entity classification election. Relevant elections: (i) ETA s. 156 election for nil-consideration supplies between closely related corporations; (ii) ETA s. 271 election appointing a resident agent for non-resident registrants; (iii) ETA s. 177 quick method not available; (iv) ITA s. 85(1) rollover election for property transfers on incorporation; (v) ITA s. 216 election for non-residents renting Canadian property; (vi) ETA s. 191 election to treat a registrant as non-registrant not available; (vii) provincial Ontario scrap-metal dealer licensing election does not affect tax classification. Use Form RC461 (ETA s. 156) and Form T2057 (ITA s. 85).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supplier must obtain and retain documentary proof of export (CRA Memorandum 17-10-1). The scrap must be physically shipped from Canada to a foreign consignee; sale cannot be 'sold but left in Canada'. GST/HST registrant status is required to issue zero-rated invoices and claim full input tax credits.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully floating exchange-rate regime with no statutory foreign exchange controls. Both capital and current account transactions are freely convertible into and out of Canadian dollars (CAD). Profit remittance by way of dividends, management fees, or loan repayments is unrestricted, subject only to standard withholding tax rates under the Canada–Income Tax Act and applicable tax treaties. There are no restrictions on repatriation of invested capital, no capital controls, and no requirement for central bank approval for cross-border transfers. However, financial institutions and intermediaries must comply with the Currency Transactions Reporting and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and associated FINTRAC report filing thresholds, which are unrelated to convertibility but may impose documentary and reporting frictions on large cross-border wire movements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender or mandatory conversion requirements exist for export receipts in Canada. Exporters are free to retain proceeds in CAD or any permitted foreign currency. For exported recycled copper wire and scrap metal, receipts may be settled through normal banking channels without any requirement to sell foreign currency to the Bank of Canada or a licensed dealer. Reporting obligations are limited to anti-money laundering record-keeping and transaction report thresholds under FINTRAC (e.g., large cash transaction reports and electronic funds transfer reports over CAD 10,000) and to the applicable Canada Border Services Agency export declarations and Statistics Canada trade reporting, which do not restrict currency retention.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Foreign fintech and electronic money institutions (EMIs) face significant legal restrictions in Canada. Operating as an unlicensed cross-border payment service provider is not permissible unless the entity is registered as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and, where applicable, holds a payment service provider licence under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). The RPAA, fully phased in after 2024, requires entities performing electronic payment functions for Canadian users to be incorporated or maintain a physical presence in Canada and obtain regulatory approval from the Bank of Canada. Offshore digital EMIs with no Canadian presence are therefore considered low-to-medium viability for the core treasury function. They may be used as ancillary collection or disbursement rails in limited amounts only, provided they become FINTRAC-registered and comply with surety bonds or other safeguarding requirements. For robust compliance and audit readiness, a tier-1 domestic commercial bank remains the preferred primary channel.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injections from shareholders into the Canadian operating entity must be routed via SWIFT wire transfer using the corporate account held with a tier-1 domestic bank. The designated purpose code should reflect the nature of the injection: for share capital contributions under a federal incorporation, the Statistical Office of Canada's Balance of Payments classification 'Direct investment – equity capital' is used, commonly encoded in SWIFT MT103 field 70 or the bank’s e-banking transfer notes. All inbound remittances above CAD 10,000 are subject to FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting. The bank will require the remitter to provide clear source-of-funds documentation, the corporate resolution authorizing the borrowing or capital increase, and the final ultimate beneficial owner declaration. Where the injection is structured as a shareholder loan, the purpose code should be 'Inter-company debt – direct investment' and an executed loan agreement with an arm’s-length interest rate and repayment schedule must be maintained; otherwise CRA recharacterization risk arises.Outbound disbursements for purchasing recycled copper scrap from overseas suppliers should be executed through two principal clearing rails: (i) SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfers for immediate cash payments against proforma invoices or open-account trade terms, and (ii) documentary letters of credit (LC) governed by ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP600) for large, high-value shipments where the seller requires bank-assured payment. MT103 transfers must contain exact invoice and contract references, an accurate purpose code describing the physical import/export of non-ferrous scrap metal, and must be sent only to banks deemed compliant with Canadian AML/KYC and sanctions requirements. For the documentary LC rail, the issuing bank will require a completed application, underlying sales contract, delegation of authority, and confirmation whether the LC is at sight or with usance tenor. Both rails must align with Canada Border Services Agency customs valuation and the supporting export/import documentation package. Cross-border outbound transfers exceeding CAD 10,000 automatically trigger FINTRAC EFT reporting to the receiver.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate rate after provincial abatement and general rate reduction; 9% for eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation business income; combined federal/provincial statutory rates generally range from approximately 26% to 31% depending on province and entity type.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII Canadian withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, absent treaty relief.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Form T2 is due no later than six months after the taxation year end. Lesser balance-due remittance dates apply: generally two months after year end for most corporations, and three months after year end for certain eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of reportable non-arm’s-length cross-border transactions in the fiscal year triggers Form T106 and contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation obligations. Country-by-country reporting applies to MNE groups meeting the CAD 750,000,000 or equivalent consolidated revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply an offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations code, but the CRA enforces substantive economic activity through the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR), beneficial ownership tests, treaty anti-avoidance rules, and PE-profit attribution standards. Commercial transactions structured without Canadian decision-making, risk assumption, operational substance, or valid business purpose are exposed to GAAR reassessments and treaty-relief denial.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the net upward adjustment, deny deduction of the amount to the extent not arm’s-length, and reassess beyond the normal three-year limitation period in cases of misrepresentation or failure to file. Interest accrues on all assessed deficiencies.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for corporate amounts withheld from employees or remitted under tax legislation, and directors who knowingly participate in creating or maintaining false transfer-pricing records can face gross-negligence penalties and criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act, including fines and imprisonment.
Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act, conviction for tax evasion carries a fine of at least 50% and up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years on indictment. Summary conviction penalties are also available, and CRA may seize records and freeze funds during investigation.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who direct, consent to, or participate in the evasion can be prosecuted as controlling minds, subjected to civil restitution, banned from leaving Canada or from holding board positions, and imprisoned. The corporate veil is pierced where the corporation is used as an instrument of deceit.
Shell Company / Artificial Substance Abusing Treaty Benefits

Statutory sanction: GAAR under section 245 will deny the tax benefit; the treaty rate can be reclassified to the full 25% Part XIII withholding; the arrangement may be ignored for tax purposes and the income re-attributed to the non-resident shareholder with interest and penalties. Administrative monetary penalties may be added.

Director personal liability: Nominee or shadow directors who sign financial statements or filings for a shell without oversight may be jointly liable for misrepresentations and fraudulent conveyances; CRA can issue director liability assessments and seek orders restricting re-entry, travel, and transfers of personal assets.
Anti-Money Laundering / Suspicious Payment Flows

Statutory sanction: Financial institutions and intermediaries are required to file Suspicious Transaction Reports with FINTRAC; failure to have AML compliance systems or making false statements can lead to administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 in aggravated cases and criminal sanctions for money laundering under the Criminal Code, including imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be charged where they knowingly benefit from criminally derived goods or funds laundered through the corporate entity, including personal financial restitution, forfeiture orders, asset seizure, travel bans, and imprisonment; enhanced due diligence and source-of-wealth evidence is essential for directors of scrap-metal trading companies.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII

Statutory sanction: The payer remains liable for the uncollected withholding tax plus interest and penalties, and may be assessed a 10% penalty or higher for failure to deduct, remit, or report; Part XIII amounts are deemed to be held in trust for the Crown and carry priority in bankruptcy.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII amounts where the corporation fails to deduct at source; CRA may issue a director’s liability certificate and pursue personal bank accounts, property, equity interests, and travel restrictions until the debt is satisfied.
