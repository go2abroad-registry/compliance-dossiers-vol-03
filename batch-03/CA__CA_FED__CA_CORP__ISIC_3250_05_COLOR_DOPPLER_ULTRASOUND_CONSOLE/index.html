Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, or otherwise required to file, must first obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number (BN) via the CRA's online 'Business Registration Online' service, Form RC1, or by written application. For GST/HST purposes a separate GST/HST account number (the BN followed by 'RT') is required once registration is mandatory or voluntarily elected. Registration is effected by completing the CRA Form RC1 for the BN and GST/HST account, providing legal entity name, proof of incorporation, directors, place of business, and the expected annual taxable supplies.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A federally incorporated Canadian Business Corporation is a statutory person taxable in Canada on worldwide income unless a territorial or exempt nexus applies; no Canadian analogue to the U.S. pass-through check-the-box regime exists. The corporation's Canadian-controlled status and permanent establishment placement determine treaty benefits and provincial attribution only; the federal entity is always a separate taxpayer.

Available election forms

No check-the-box or entity classification election is available under the Income Tax Act for a Canadian federal corporation; the entity is per se taxable. Relevant elections include: (i) hybrid mismatch and thin capitalization elections under Sections 18.2, 18.4, and Part XII.3 for excessive debt; (ii) GST/HST elections under section 156 for closely-related corporations, section 169 input tax credit allocations, and the streamlined REC rules for financial institutions; and (iii) elections under subsection 216 of the Income Tax Act allowing an eligible non-resident insurer or trust to fulfil tax obligations on Canadian-source business income. A foreign-owned Canadian corporation may also make a 'deemed non-resident' election under subsection 250(5.1) only if the treaty applies and stringent conditions are met; for U.S. tax purposes, a Canadian corporation is a per se corporation and cannot be treated as a partnership or disregarded entity.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total consideration for worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated exports) made in any single calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration applies. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada is also required to register if its Canadian taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 annually. A person less than the threshold may voluntarily register to claim input tax credits, subject to holding a valid GST/HST account.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export treatment under section 6(a) of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act applies to tangible personal property exported from Canada by the supplier, or to goods delivered outside Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident outside Canada may be zero-rated under section 4 of Part V of Schedule VI provided the recipient is not registered or resident in Canada and the service is not performed substantially in Canada in relation to real or tangible property. The exporter must demonstrate that title and risk of loss passed outside Canada and that the goods were exported on a continuous journey or removed within a prescribed time. A manufacturer of medical diagnostic scanners must hold an export sales invoice, proof of shipment, customs documentation, and payment records evidencing supply to a foreign customer.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (final export declaration through Canadian Export Reporting System, CERS, for goods subject to export controls or valued over CAD 2,000 unless a permit or exception applies)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from Canada and destination outside Canada
  • Commercial invoices, packing list, and documentary evidence of the foreign purchaser's identity and non-resident status
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank transfer/letter-of-credit records proving payment in foreign currency and repatriation to Canada

No federal real-time fiscal clearance, API-enabled digital stamping, or mandatory continuous transaction control (CTC) regime currently applies in Canada solely for GST/HST. The CRA requires a prescribed invoice containing the supplier's name and business number, a description of goods/services, tax status, and GST/HST charged, and input tax credit entitlement depends on contemporaneous documentation. Voluntary electronic data interchange, PEPPOL e-invoicing, and business-to-government e-invoicing are accepted, but no CRA pre-clearance or real-time reporting of individual export invoices is required.

Improper or fraudulent zero-rating claims may trigger CRA audits, denial of input tax credit recovery, reassessment of GST/HST on the supply, interest, and penalties. Under the Excise Tax Act, knowingly making false statements can result in penalties up to 25%-40% of the gross tax and parliamentary fines; under the Income Tax Act, false deductions or omissions attract penalty up to 50% of tax avoided plus interest from the original due date. The CRA may also assess a 6% export-reporting penalty under the Export and Import Permits Act if a final export declaration is incomplete, and may pursue criminal fraud remedies where invoices, bills of lading, and remittance certificates are falsified to substantiate zero-rated treatment.

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely convertible G10 currency. Both current account and capital account transactions are fully convertible under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act, with no prior central-bank approval, reinvestment quota, or phasing requirement for trade settlement, intercompany debt, equity subscription, or profit remittance. The principal legal hurdle affecting repatriation is Part XIII non-resident withholding tax of the Income Tax Act: dividends paid by a Canadian federal corporation to non-resident shareholders are generally subject to 25% statutory withholding, reduced to 15% under most income tax treaties and to 5% where the recipient is a treaty-qualifying direct shareholder holding substantial equity interest; branch profits of a Canadian permanent establishment are similarly subject to a 25% branch tax (treaty-reducible). Additional compliance obligations include FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reports (EFTR) for any international EFT of CAD 10,000 or more, sanctions screening under the Special Economic Measures Act and UN regulations, and conservatively hedged treatment of foreign-currency receivable balances under IFRS/ASPE standards.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed timeline for repatriation. Export receipts in USD or other foreign currencies may be retained in a bank-held foreign-currency account or converted at the corporate treasury's election. The statutory compliance perimeter is: (i) customs reporting of exported goods above CAD 2,000 via a B13A Export Declaration under the Customs Act and the Reporting of Exported Goods Regulations, with retention of commercial invoice, packing list, and transport documents; (ii) tax recognition of export revenue in Canadian dollars using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate on the income-recognition date; (iii) maintenance of transfer-pricing documentation for intercompany offshore purchases or sales under the OECD-aligned Canadian transfer-pricing rules; and (iv) FINTRAC reporting for cross-border EFTs at or above CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central monetary authority); prudential banking supervision delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF and cross-border financial intelligence administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); Canada maintains no separate Foreign Exchange Administration and imposes no exchange-control licensing regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Conditionally viable, rated Medium for supplementary disbursement use and Low as a primary treasury rail. Offshore digital EMI providers such as Wise Business, Revolut Business, and Currencycloud/GlobalPay can execute small- and mid-value operational payments for CA_CORP, but they are not chartered banks under the Bank Act, do not hold Canadian deposit balances, and cannot directly access Payments Canada Lynx settlement. Legal constraints under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act include: FINTRAC registration as a money service business (MSB) for any entity that remits EFTs on Canadian client instructions; mandatory EFTR filing for cross-border EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more; sanctions screening obligations identical to those of a bank; and Bank Act prohibitions on an unlicenced entity offering deposit-taking services. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Legal constraints under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act include: FINTRAC registration as a money service business (MSB) for any entity that remits EFTs on Canadian client instructions; mandatory EFTR filing for cross-border EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more; sanctions screening obligations identical to those of a bank; and Bank Act prohibitions on an unlicenced entity offering deposit-taking services. Because export trade finance and documentary credits require an institutional clearing bank, the EMI rail should be restricted to vendor disbursements under CAD-equivalent 50,000 per transaction and never used to receive medical-device export proceeds or capital subscriptions.
