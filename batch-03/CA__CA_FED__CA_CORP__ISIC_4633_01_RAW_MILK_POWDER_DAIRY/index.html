Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - Business Registration Online (BRO) / My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html)

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities, including non-resident business corporations, must obtain a Business Number (BN) and, where taxable supplies are made in Canada, a GST/HST account. Standard procedure: submit Form RC1 or use CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO). Non-residents not yet carrying on business in Canada may be required to register by correspondence with the CRA Non-Resident Registry, providing corporate articles, proof of directors, and a designated Canadian representative for service of documents where applicable. Registration is mandatory once the entity makes taxable supplies in Canada and exceeds the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold or otherwise carries on a commercial activity in Canada. For GST/HST purposes, the entity will receive a separate GST/HST account number under the same BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not employ a US-style check-the-box regime. The federal Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act provide limited express elections, including: (i) the s. 156 ETA election for zero-rating of taxable supplies between closely related corporations; (ii) the s. 91 ETA group registration election; (iii) the s. 216 ITA election for computing Canadian income tax on certain non-resident property income; and (iv) miscellaneous partnership and rollover elections under ss. 97 and 85 ITA. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are addressed by statutory rules rather than elective regimes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (or on a calendar-quarter basis for registrants who are not resident and do not carry on a permanent establishment in Canada, subject to mandatory registration criteria for non-residents carrying on commercial activity).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate supplies of whole and skimmed milk powder where the goods are exported by the supplier or delivered to a common carrier for export outside Canada, in accordance with section 39 of the Excise Tax Act and CRA policy. The exporter must maintain evidence of exportation acceptable to the CRA, including the export declaration, bill of lading/air waybill, and proof of payment or remittance from the foreign buyer. Zero-rating also applies to supplies made to a non-resident for the purpose of commercial export if the goods are delivered by the supplier outside Canada. For dairy commodity trading, typical commercial terms are FOB or FCA, with risk and title passing in Canada; the supplier must nevertheless demonstrate that the goods were physically removed from Canada within the prescribed timeframe.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canadian Export Reporting System / CERS or Form B13A)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing export from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT/wire transfer evidence of payment received from the non-resident buyer

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping regime exists at the federal level. GST/HST registrants must provide a valid statutory invoice containing prescribed CRA information, but e-invoicing is voluntary. CRA accepts electronic records and supports PEPPOL-based B2B e-invoice exchange; no mandatory national API clearance platform applies.

CRA may disallow zero-rating where the required export evidence is incomplete, missing, or untimely. The resulting assessment applies full GST/HST at the applicable rate, together with interest from the date of the supply. For gross negligence or wilful participation in a false statement or omission, the Excise Tax Act imposes civil penalties up to 25% of the tax evaded, in addition to fines and possible imprisonment for fraud. False export claims also trigger extended reassessment periods (beyond the normal four-year limitation period) and potential director liability. The exporter bears the burden of proving physical export and must retain all documentation for six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility maintained under IMF Article VIII. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is free-floating with unrestricted current- and capital-account transactions; no central-bank approval is required for profit remittance, dividend distribution, capital repatriation, or trade settlement. The only material frictions are the Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends (25% statutory; treaty-reduced, e.g., 5% for a 10%+ U.S. corporate shareholder) and the ICA start-up notification requirement for a non-Canadian investor establishing a new Canadian business. Paid-up capital is immediately deployable for trade disbursements; no minimum statutory capital applies to a federal Business Corporation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory repatriation timeline. Milk-powder exporters may retain proceeds in CAD, in foreign-currency accounts at Schedule I banks, or offshore, subject solely to ordinary income attribution, arm's-length transfer-pricing rules, and the 1.5:1 thin-capitalisation limit under Income Tax Act section 18(4). Export of dairy products remains subject to Canadian Dairy Commission export-authorisation and supply-management compliance, which are trade measures, not FX controls.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — central monetary authority; prudential supervision of banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT and EFT report administration by FINTRAC; foreign-investment screening under the Investment Canada Act (ICA) by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED). Canada maintains no dedicated FX-control ministry or exchange-approval agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low for primary liquidity; Medium for ancillary FX conversion. Canada does not recognise EU/UK EMI passports; offshore digital EMIs must register with FINTRAC as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) and, where performing retail payment functions, must authorise under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Foreign EMIs cannot issue documentary Letters of Credit or clear CAD through Lynx, both of which are chartered-bank functions under the Bank Act. Restrict EMIs to sub-CAD 100,000 supplier disbursements initiated only after the primary bank rail is operational.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a 9% federal small-business rate on up to CAD 500,000 of qualifying active business income, subject to the business limit and associated-corporation rules. Provincial or territorial corporate tax is additional and depends on the permanent establishment jurisdiction.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Under the Canada-United States tax treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a company owning at least 10% of the voting power of the Canadian payer, and 15% in other qualifying cases. Treaty eligibility, limitation-on-benefits requirements, beneficial ownership, and anti-conduit principles must be satisfied.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within 6 months after the end of every taxation year. Any balance of tax is generally due 2 months after year-end, or 3 months after year-end for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting the statutory conditions. Instalments are due during the year under the applicable monthly or quarterly schedule.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is optional. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million. Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated annual revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. A Canadian master file is generally relevant where the Canadian entity is part of an MNE group subject to the applicable OECD-style reporting framework; Canada does not impose a universal standalone master-file threshold for all taxpayers.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the general anti-avoidance rule, the transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse rules, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and common-law sham and agency doctrines. A dairy trading corporation should maintain real commercial functions, decision-making, inventory and logistics evidence, financing rationale, personnel or outsourced operational capability, contractual control, and risk-bearing consistent with reported profits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction can result in fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years under the Income Tax Act. Civil reassessments, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, repeated-failure penalties, and collection actions may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution where they participated in, authorized, acquiesced in, or directed the conduct. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses. Criminal imprisonment is personal; civil veil piercing is not automatic and generally requires a statutory basis, fraud, sham, or other exceptional circumstances. Travel restrictions may arise from criminal process, bail, judgment enforcement, or passport-related measures, but are not an automatic tax penalty.
Failure to remit payroll deductions or Part XIII withholding

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, interest, late-remittance penalties generally ranging from 10% to 20% depending on the failure, and possible director liability. Wilful failure to deduct or remit can support criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions and GST/HST if the corporation failed to remit and the director did not exercise due diligence. Liability may survive dissolution subject to statutory limitation rules.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, adjust income, deny deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, and charge interest. Failure to file or inaccurate Form T106 may generate additional penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing reassessment, but personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in false records, tax evasion, fraud, or unremitted statutory amounts.
Customs, import, food-safety, and dairy-regulatory non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Goods may be detained, refused entry, seized, recalled, or forfeited. Administrative monetary penalties, duties, GST, interest, license or permit suspension, and prosecution may apply under customs, food, and agricultural legislation.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for directors or officers who authorize statutory offences, submit false customs information, or obstruct inspectors. Imprisonment exposure depends on the specific federal statute and offence.
Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime risk

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may require reporting, recordkeeping, client identification, and suspicious-transaction controls through reporting entities. Criminal money-laundering offences can carry imprisonment of up to 10 years for indictable offences; proceeds and property may be restrained or forfeited.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, conspiracy, counselling, or wilful blindness. Corporate status does not protect individuals from criminal prosecution or asset forfeiture.
Sanctions and export-control violations

Statutory sanction: Assets may be frozen and transactions prohibited. Administrative or criminal penalties, substantial fines, and imprisonment may apply under Canadian sanctions and export-control legislation, depending on the regime and conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for authorization, consent, participation, or failure to prevent an offence where the governing statute permits. Civil and criminal enforcement can extend beyond the corporation.
Shell-company, beneficial-ownership, and corporate-record abuse

Statutory sanction: Corporate registry sanctions, tax reassessment, denial of treaty benefits, beneficial-ownership disclosure orders, fines, forfeiture, and criminal fraud or tax-evasion prosecution may apply. A corporation may be struck from a registry or lose good standing for non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil, regulatory, and criminal liability for false corporate records, fraud, obstruction, or knowing participation. The corporate veil may be disregarded in exceptional cases involving fraud, sham arrangements, or misuse of the corporation.
Thin capitalization and excessive related-party financing

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions may be denied under thin-capitalization and excessive-interest rules, deemed dividends may be subject to Part XIII withholding, and reassessment interest and penalties may apply. The debt-to-equity ratio and related-party ownership must be tested annually.

Director personal liability: No automatic personal liability arises from a corporate deduction denial, but personal exposure can arise from fraudulent records, wilful tax evasion, or unremitted withholding amounts.
Failure to maintain records and obstructing CRA

Statutory sanction: CRA may impose recordkeeping and information-request penalties, draw adverse inferences, reassess using indirect methods, and seek compliance orders. Obstruction or destruction of records can support criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct destruction, concealment, or obstruction may be personally prosecuted. A company cannot shield an individual from criminal liability for the individual's own conduct.
