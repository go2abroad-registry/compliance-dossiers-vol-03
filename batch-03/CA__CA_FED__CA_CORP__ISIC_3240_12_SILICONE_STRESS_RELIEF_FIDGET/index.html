Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; Business Registration Online (BRO) portal for Business Number and GST/HST account creation; My Business Account portal for ongoing corporate income tax and GST/HST compliance.

E-government filing portal

Canada operates a unified Business Number (BN) system rather than a separate VAT ID. A foreign-owned federally incorporated corporation obtains its statutory BN through one of two streams: (1) upon incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act, the corporation applies through the Newly Incorporated Business Registration Online (NIBRO) service, which simultaneously issues the nine-digit BN, the GST/HST program account (RC0001), and the payroll program account (RC0001); or (2) a pre-existing non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada or otherwise meeting Canadian GST/HST nexus rules files Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) including the non-resident GST/HST registration annex, submitted directly to the CRA Sudbury Tax Centre or through an authorized representative using Form RC59. The application must designate a Canadian registered office or address for service in Canada, identify all directors or trustees, declare the date of commencement of business in Canada, and describe the Canadian business activities. CRA generally issues the BN and activates the GST/HST account within 10 to 15 business days. The BN is entity-specific and non-transferable between legal persons.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a federal CBCA business corporation is a resident corporation taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act (Canada) on worldwide income; no pass-through regime is available to federal corporations. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access the small business deduction on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income and associated preferential rates, but the corporation itself remains the sole taxpaying entity; income, losses and credits do not flow through to shareholders.

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a US-style check-the-box election or a statutory hybrid mismatch classification election; entity classification is governed by the central management and control residence test and the CBCA. Statutory elections available to this entity include: (i) the subsection 85(1) ITA rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of eligible property to the corporation; (ii) the subsection 256(2) ITA associated-corporation allocation election on T2 Schedule 23 affecting the small business limit; (iii) GST/HST elections, including the section 177 Excise Tax Act corporate reorganization election, the annual filing election under subsection 234(4) ETA for small suppliers, and the Quick Method election for registrants with worldwide taxable supplies not exceeding CAD 400,000; (iv) the designation of dividends as taxable dividends to align Part IV tax treatment under subsection 186(1) ITA; and (v) for non-resident corporations, the branch tax regime under section 219(1) ITA and treaty-based permanent establishment determinations, which govern whether the foreign parent is assessed on a branch basis or remains outside Canadian taxing jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. A supplier, including a foreign supplier making taxable supplies in Canada, classified as a small supplier is not required to register for GST/HST unless its worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the aggregate of the previous four consecutive calendar quarters. Once exceeded on a forward-looking basis, GST/HST registration is mandatory effective the first day of the following month; a single taxable supply made in Canada with a value greater than CAD 30,000 immediately obligates the supplier to register.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of molded silicone tactile sensory fidget toys are zero-rated for GST/HST where the registrant supplier sells and exports the goods to a non-resident customer and the goods are physically delivered outside Canada. Under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, the supply qualifies if the supplier ships the goods directly from Canada to the non-resident, or delivers the goods to a non-resident for export within 60 days after the physical delivery to the non-resident, and retains documentary evidence of export. Sales through online marketplaces to foreign end-consumers are zero-rated only if the goods are exported before domestic consumption in Canada; goods sold and fulfilled from a Canadian warehouse to Canadian end-users remain taxable at 5% GST or the applicable HST. Services supplied to a non-resident for use exclusively outside Canada (e.g., product design or engineering services accompanying exported goods) may likewise be zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI, subject to carve-outs where the service is performed for persons present in Canada or relates to Canadian-situated property.

  • CBSA Form B13A Export Declaration or the electronic CADEX / Canadian Export Reporting System data submission evidencing the outward export transaction, required for commercial goods with an export value of CAD 2,000 or more unless a statutory exemption applies; low-value shipments still require commercial invoice evidence and outward manifest data.
  • Commercial invoice and weighted packing list identifying the foreign buyer, the HS 2022 classification for silicone fidget toys (Chapter 95 or Chapter 39), the Incoterm, the country of destination and the declared export value.
  • Transport document proving physical export: negotiable ocean Bill of Lading with vessel name and foreign discharge port, Master Air Waybill or courier airway bill showing the Canadian departure airport, or highway bill of lading for US-bound shipments referencing the border crossing port.
  • Proof of consideration and FX settlement: bank SWIFT MT103/MT202 remittance credits, the export sales contract or purchase order referencing the foreign currency invoice, and the foreign-exchange realization record corresponding to the 180-day repatriation deadline.

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance regime, no digital stamping, and no mandatory pre-clearance e-invoicing approval. Every invoice for a taxable supply must state the supplier's BN and GST/HST program account number, the invoice date, the recipient identification, the consideration, and either the GST/HST charged or a zero-rating statement for exported supplies. Export evidence, including CBSA outward manifest data and B13A declaration records, must be preserved. Registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1.5 million must file GST/HST returns electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or approved service providers. The CRA participates in the emerging Pan-Canadian e-invoicing interoperability framework on an optional basis; no PEPPOL or B2B e-invoicing mandate is yet in force. Books and records must be located in Canada or accessible electronically from Canada for six years from the end of the relevant reporting period.

The CRA applies documentary-heavy audit scrutiny to export zero-rating claims, requiring the supplier to demonstrate physical movement of goods out of Canada, a genuine foreign counterparty, and the absence of re-importation into the Canadian market. Where the evidence bundle is deficient, the CRA reassesses the supply as taxable at 5% GST or the applicable HST, denies the related input tax credits, and charges arrears interest under section 280 ETA. Penalties escalate as follows: a 5% to 10% penalty for knowingly false statements under section 285 ETA; a 25% gross-negligence penalty for misrepresentation; and criminal prosecution for tax evasion under section 327 ETA, with fines of up to 250% of the tax sought to be evaded and potential imprisonment for directors, officers, and promoters. The CRA may also invoke the section 274 ETA general anti-avoidance rule to recharacterize artificial or circular export chains. The standard four-year reassessment window runs from the mailing of the original notice of assessment, but the limitation period is suspended indefinitely where misrepresentation, fraud, or a knowingly false statement is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction and has maintained full current- and capital-account convertibility without exchange-control licensing since the abolition of the Foreign Exchange Control Board in 1951. There are no statutory restrictions on conversion of CAD to USD, EUR, CNY, or other trade currencies, no currency allocation quotas, and no capital-repatriation permits. Profit remittance is subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends (25% statutory, reduced to 5-15% under relevant income tax treaties, e.g., Canada-U.S. Convention typically 5% for a 10%+ corporate shareholder) and to normal banking diligence; capital injections and loan repayments are free of exchange-control approval. FINTRAC requires financial intermediaries to report electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more in a single transaction, and CBSA requires physical cross-border currency declarations above CAD 10,000, but neither obligation constrains convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no foreign-exchange surrender rule in Canada; the Export and Import Permits Act and the Currency Act impose no mandatory conversion ratio, no statutory repatriation timeline, and no holding-period restriction on export proceeds. A Canadian exporter of silicone fidget toys may retain USD or other foreign receipts in a foreign-currency bank account or convert them at market rates. Standard documentary evidence (bill of lading, commercial invoice, CBSA B3 export declaration, and USMCA/CUSMA certificate where applicable) must be retained for tax and FINTRAC audit purposes, but no exchange-control filing is triggered by receipt of export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the statutory monetary and banking-system authority under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2); day-to-day regulatory administration of financial institutions is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46); AML/ATF enforcement for cross-border funds movement is exercised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17); cross-border currency declaration is administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility for use as an adjunct payment rail: Medium-High. Feasibility as a primary operating bank: Low-Medium. Canadian-domiciled fintech EMI-type providers (e.g., Wise Canada Ltd., Payoneer Canada ULC) operate as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses under the PCMLTFA and, where they hold payment balances, must comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (S.C. 2021, c. 23), administered by the Bank of Canada, which requires registration and operational risk-management standards for payment service providers. Offshore digital EMIs (EU e-money institutions or UK EMIs) cannot lawfully accept deposits from, or open contemporary current-account equivalents for, a Canadian corporate entity without a Schedule I or II bank sponsor; their utility is limited to card-issuing, virtual-account aggregation, and cross-border receivable collection, layered on top of an underlying chartered-bank settlement account. For a low-risk ISIC 3240 manufacturer, the recommended structure is a Schedule I operational account plus one FINTRAC-registered cross-border fintech rail for e-commerce platform payouts and small-value supplier disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Structure the inbound contribution as an SWIFT MT103 corporate payment from the parent/shareholder's bank to the Canadian entity's account at a Schedule I institution (e.g., RBC ROYCCAT2, TD TDOMCATTTOR, Scotiabank NOSCCATT, BMO BOFCAMTT, CIBC CIBCCATT). Field 57A must name the Canadian bank and branch, field 59 must carry the beneficiary with the 3-digit institution number, 5-digit transit, and account number (Canada has no IBAN; the Canadian bank will provide a Beneficiary Identifier for SWIFT). Remittance narrative should include an ISO 20022 external purpose code, preferably 'CCAP' (capital contribution) or 'CAPH' (capital increase), plus 'ISIC 3240'; use of 'GIFT' or 'TOYS' descriptions should be avoided. The receiving bank will segregate the funds in a Capital Escrow Account until it matches the share-subscription agreement and board resolution, after which the funds are released to the Operational Account for working capital. Any single wire at or above CAD 10,000 is automatically reported to FINTRAC as an EFT; large shareholder loans are additionally visible to the CRA for thin-capitalization analysis. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (per non-arm's length non-resident, triggering mandatory Form T106 disclosure); contemporaneous s.247 documentation is nevertheless required for all covered transactions regardless of size; country-by-country reporting is required for MNE groups with consolidated revenue exceeding EUR 750 million

Economic substance scope: Canada has no stand-alone Economic Substance Regulations comparable to offshore codes, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA), the sham-document doctrine, and the functional analysis embedded in s.247(2) empower the CRA to disregard transactions lacking genuine commercial substance; a Canadian subsidiary operated as a shell without employees, premises, or meaningful functions and risks will be reassessed, and treaty benefits may be denied under the Principal Purpose Test.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: s.239 ITA: fine of between 50% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to five years, plus the 50% gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2); the CRA may issue a jeopardy order to seize corporate assets and may publicize the conviction.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct, authorize, participate in, or acquiesce to the evasion may be prosecuted personally; s.227.1 ITA makes directors jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII withholding; courts may lift the corporate veil, order civil restitution, and impose travel restrictions or prohibition orders pending prosecution.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing reassessment under s.247(2) plus penalty of 10% of the net adjustment under s.247(3); GAAR nullification of the tax benefit; denial of treaty benefits under the Principal Purpose Test; Part XIII reassessment of deemed dividends paid to the foreign parent.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false T106 disclosures or incorrect NR4 filings face the third-party civil penalty regime; where the corporation is deemed a sham, the CRA may disregard corporate personality and pursue directors personally; failure to remit assessed Part XIII amounts triggers joint and several liability under s.227.1.
AML / Proceeds of Crime and FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2,000,000 for a corporation and CAD 1,000,000 for an individual per violation; criminal money laundering under s.462.31 of the Criminal Code is punishable by a fine of up to CAD 1,000,000 and/or imprisonment for up to ten years; proceeds of crime are subject to forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly or through criminal negligence participate in laundering proceeds face indictment as principals or parties; personal assets traced to the proceeds may be confiscated; a court may impose a travel ban or prohibition order preventing departure from Canada while proceedings are pending.
