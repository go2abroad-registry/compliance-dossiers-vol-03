Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); online portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-individuals-businesses/business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada or required to register for GST/HST must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The foreign entity can register online through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) service or by mailing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts). The BN is a nine-digit identifier, with program accounts such as GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), and import/export (RM) added as suffixes. For a BC ULC with non-resident ownership, the entity must also consider whether the CRA will require a director/resident representative; the ULC must obtain a BN before registering for GST/HST, customs, or excise programs. Once the BN is issued, the entity should register for the GST/HST program account, and if importing or exporting goods, for the CRA import/export (RM) account and customs surety requirements under the Customs Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) is generally treated as a taxable Canadian corporation for Canadian income tax purposes, meaning it is subject to entity-level federal and provincial corporate income tax; however, because a ULC is a distinct legal entity, it cannot itself make a check-the-box election under Canadian domestic law. The ULC's U.S. shareholders may make a 'check-the-box' election under U.S. Treasury Regulations §301.7701-3 to treat the ULC as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. tax purposes if the ULC has a single member or multiple members, respectively. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules (section 18.4 of the Income Tax Act) may recharacterize certain payments or deny deductions if there is a hybrid mismatch arising from the ULC's differing tax treatment in Canada and the U.S.; a ULC may also consider filing an election under subsection 216(1) regarding non-resident withholding taxes on certain income, and if the ULC has a U.S. shareholder, the Canada-U.S. Treaty may permit the shareholder to elect to be treated as a resident of Canada for treaty purposes, thereby becoming subject to Canadian tax on worldwide income under the so-called 'ULC election.' No general check-the-box election exists under Canadian law for the ULC itself to be treated as a flow-through entity; rather, Canada taxes the ULC as a corporation, while the U.S. may respect the foreign entity election for U.S. purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies in Canada over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. For a non-resident that does not carry on business in Canada but imports goods or services, a separate GST/HST registration may be required to self-assess GST on imports under the reverse-charge rules for registrants; a non-resident that does not meet the small-supplier threshold may nevertheless elect to register voluntarily.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act, exports of tangible personal property from Canada are generally zero-rated for GST/HST purposes if the goods are exported 'as soon as reasonably possible' after the sale and certain documentary evidence is retained. Services are zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident who is not a GST/HST registrant and the service is performed wholly or almost wholly outside Canada; also zero-rated are services supplied to a non-resident that relate to real property situated outside Canada, goods exported from Canada, or services relating to moveable property situated outside Canada when the risk of loss passes outside Canada. For an AEO supply-chain audit service (ISIC 7020), zero-rating may apply if the service is performed for a non-resident client and the service is delivered entirely outside Canada; computer-based remote consulting to a non-resident can qualify as zero-rated under subsection 7(a) of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. The ULC must obtain and maintain evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and the overseas performance/delivery of the service.

  • Canadian Export Declaration (CERS/CAED) with proof of filing and CRA/CBSA reference number
  • Commercial invoice or sales contract showing the zero-rated supply and the purchaser's name and address
  • Bill of Lading / air waybill / other transport document evidencing export from Canada within the required timeline
  • Customs export declaration or proof of release for goods exported under the CBSA Export Reporting Program
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank wire confirmation evidencing payment in foreign currency from the non-resident buyer
  • Any written statement from the recipient confirming non-resident status and the supply is for consumption outside Canada
  • AEO security audit report and supply-chain documentation demonstrating that the commercial activity is a qualifying export service
  • Incoterms clause in the contract indicating risk of loss passes outside Canada for goods-related services

Canada does not currently mandate a real-time electronic invoicing or fiscal clearance system comparable to Latin American models. Taxpayers must keep electronic records and issue invoices that satisfy the GST/HST information requirements under the Excise Tax Act and Regulations (e.g., vendor name, BN, date, description, amount, GST rate, and amount of tax). For customs, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) requires electronic filing of export declarations through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or the older Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) system, with API/integration options through CBSA's electronic commerce platforms. There is no digital stamping requirement. Importers must use the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal for customs accounting; GST on commercial imports is generally collected at the border by CBSA on behalf of the CRA.

The CRA may deny zero-rating when documentary evidence is insufficient or when the goods do not leave Canada within the prescribed time, resulting in assessment of GST/HST, interest from the date the tax would otherwise have been payable, and penalties under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act (generally the greater of CAD 250 and 10% of the net tax understated, subject to higher wilful-participation penalties up to 25% or 40%). For knowingly making false statements or omissions in an export declaration, the Customs Act imposes fines up to CAD 25,000 for a corporation on summary conviction and up to CAD 100,000 on indictment, plus potential forfeiture of the goods. Additionally, under subsection 327(1) of the Excise Tax Act, making false statements to obtain a rebate or credit can result in penalties equal to 50% of the claimed amount. Retrospective clawbacks are available for up to four years in normal assessments; the CRA may go back beyond four years in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, and there is no limitation period for fraud. Exporters should maintain full audit trails for at least six years.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current and capital account. Canada has accepted Article VIII obligations of the IMF Articles of Agreement, meaning no restrictions on payments for current international transactions and no discriminatory currency arrangements. The Canadian dollar (CAD) trades freely in spot, forward, swap, and options markets; CAD/USD is one of the most liquid currency pairs globally. Non-resident shareholders may freely subscribe for shares in a British Columbia Unlimited Liability Company (BC ULC), and profits may be repatriated without Bank of Canada authorization. The only structural friction is the Income Tax Act (Canada) Part XIII withholding tax of 25% on dividends and distributions paid or credited to non-resident shareholders, subject to reduction under the Canada–US Tax Treaty (typically 5% on dividends from a subsidiary if the beneficial owner is a U.S. company holding at least 10% of the voting stock; 15% otherwise). A BC ULC that is disregarded as a flow-through for U.S. federal tax purposes must nevertheless be analyzed under Canadian domestic law for Part XIII purposes: distributions in excess of paid-up capital can be recharacterized as deemed dividends. No capital-gains block exists for outbound repatriation provided the conventional share sale relief under the Canada–US treaty applies. Additional tax reporting, but not currency control, applies to dispositions of taxable Canadian property.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. A BC ULC earning fees from cross-border AEO certification and supply-chain security audit services may retain proceeds in foreign currency (USD, EUR, GBP) indefinitely in Canadian or offshore bank accounts without surrender or conversion obligation. However, any physical cross-border movement of currency or monetary instruments exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) under the PCMLTFA cross-border currency reporting rules; bank wire movements are automatically captured by FINTRAC large-transaction reporting (CAD 10,000 or more, or equivalent). Foreign-currency account balances do not require ongoing central-bank reporting, but the Canadian bank may require annual attestation of source of funds under its AML program.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) issues the Canadian dollar and acts as lender of last resort; day-to-day prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46); anti-money laundering supervision is vested in the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada has no active foreign-exchange-control authority: the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951 and the Foreign Exchange Control Board was dissolved. Exchange-rate policy is a free float administered by the Bank of Canada, with no convertibility licensing, no currency declaration regime, and no approval requirement for cross-border capital movements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

High feasibility for Wise (Wise Canada, Inc.), which is registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and operates under an OSFI-conditional technology-services framework with funds held at Schedule I Canadian banks (e.g., held in segregated accounts). Wise is a permissible supplementary payment rail for outbound disbursements in 40+ currencies and for receiving inbound USD/GBP/EUR payments, but it is not a Canadian Schedule I bank: it cannot offer settlement accounts, overdrafts, commercial letters of credit, or CDIC-insured deposits. Statutory constraints include: (i) all Wise balances are uninsured and are not 'deposits' under the Bank Act; (ii) onboarding requires the BC ULC to provide registry extract, beneficial-ownership declaration, and source-of-funds evidence; (iii) the MSB is subject to FINTRAC registration, entity-level compliance, and transaction-reporting thresholds; and (iv) high-value or trade-finance transactions should be routed through a Tier 1 bank to avoid currency-conversion spread stacking. Revolut Business is rated Low viability for Canada: Revolut is not licensed as a bank in Canada and offers limited CAD incoming wire capabilities; its use as a primary treasury rail is not recommended.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: High feasibility for Wise (Wise Canada, Inc.), which is registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and operates under an OSFI-conditional technology-services framework with funds held at Schedule I Canadian banks (e.g., held in segregated accounts). Wise is a permissible supplementary payment rail for outbound disbursements in 40+ currencies and for receiving inbound USD/GBP/EUR payments, but it is not a Canadian Schedule I bank: it cannot offer settlement accounts, overdrafts, commercial letters of credit, or CDIC-insured deposits. Statutory constraints include: (i) all Wise balances are uninsured and are not 'deposits' under the Bank Act; (ii) onboarding requires the BC ULC to provide registry extract, beneficial-ownership declaration, and source-of-funds evidence; (iii) the MSB is subject to FINTRAC registration, entity-level compliance, and transaction-reporting thresholds; and (iv) high-value or trade-finance transactions should be routed through a Tier 1 bank to avoid currency-conversion spread stacking. Revolut Business is rated Low viability for Canada: Revolut is not licensed as a bank in Canada and offers limited CAD incoming wire capabilities; its use as a primary treasury rail is not recommended.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CRA imposes no single statutory dollar threshold for preparing transfer pricing documentation, but the mandatory information return T106 (ITA s.233.1) must be filed for non-arm's length cross-border transactions involving amounts exceeding CAD 25,000 during the year. A full contemporaneous Local File should be prepared where annual controlled transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000, and a Master File is recommended for multinational groups with Canadian gross revenue exceeding CAD 50,000,000. The transfer pricing penalty attaches where the net arm's length adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue and the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts to establish attributable transfer prices.

Economic substance scope: Statutory Economic Substance Regulations as enacted in Gulf Cooperation Council jurisdictions have no direct Canadian counterpart. Canada instead applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), the foreign accrual property income (FAPI) regime under ITA s.91, the common-law sham doctrine, and CRA's transfer pricing recharacterization power under ITA s.247. A BC ULC maintaining physical record-keeping, substantive Canadian employees, board minutes, a segregated bank account and genuine AEO audit workflows is respected as a Canadian taxpayer. A hollow ULC interposed solely to defer or eliminate tax is susceptible to reassessment, deemed shareholder benefit, and GAAR penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Criminal penalties under ITA s.239(1): a fine between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years; civil gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the additional tax assessment under ITA s.163(2); CRA may issue a garnishment, freeze bank accounts, and register a statutory security interest against the ULC's property.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in or authorize the misstatements face criminal conviction, restitution orders, and incarceration; CRA may assess directors personally for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII taxes under ITA s.227.1 without first exhausting corporate assets.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization or sham finding: CRA can attribute the ULC's income to the beneficial U.S. owner; deemed shareholder benefit or dividend under ITA s.15(1) and s.84 triggers 25% Part XIII withholding on unrepatriated funds; the Post-2023 GAAR penalty is increased to 25% of the tax avoided, and FAPI inclusions apply to the U.S. shareholder for 5 years.

Director personal liability: As a ULC, unlimited shareholder liability allows creditors and CRA to pierce the corporate veil and pursue personal assets; directors who act oppressively or knowingly strip assets are jointly liable and may face fraudulent conveyance proceedings and travel bans pending CRA collection.
Cross-Border Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may make an upward adjustment under ITA s.247(2); a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the adjustment applies where documentation is inadequate and the adjustment exceeds the CAD 5,000,000 / 10% gross revenue threshold; gross negligence adds a further 50%; interest compounds daily from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign a false T106 or participate in creating artificial intercompany terms may be assessed the penalty alongside the corporation; criminal liability under ITA s.239 can arise where the TP misstatement is knowingly filed; unlimited liability of ULC shareholders permits direct seizure of personal assets for the corporate debt.
AML / Customs Compliance and AEO Certification Fraud

Statutory sanction: Customs Act s.132.1 penalties up to CAD 1,000 per false statement or omission plus seizure of goods; FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 501,000 for unregistered Money Services Businesses; AEO accreditation revocation, denial of reduced examination rates, and potential suspension from Customs Self-Assessment programs.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers can be issued cross-border travel bans under customs enforcement procedures, be prosecuted for criminal fraud, and face personal liability for customs debts of the corporation where they directed the false AEO representations; the ULC's unlimited liability removes the protective shield otherwise available to shareholders.
