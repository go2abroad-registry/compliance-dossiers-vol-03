Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. There are no exchange control restrictions on profit remittance, dividend distributions, or capital repatriation for a British Columbia unlimited liability corporation (BC ULC). Non-resident shareholders may freely repatriate funds after satisfying Canadian withholding tax requirements (e.g., Part XIII tax on dividends, branch tax where applicable) under the Income Tax Act and relevant tax treaties. The Investment Canada Act may impose notification or review obligations when non-residents acquire 'control' of a Canadian business, but this does not constrain convertibility. In practice, a ULC can hold foreign currency accounts with domestic banks and convert at a free-market rate without central bank approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law does not mandate the surrender, conversion, or repatriation of export proceeds within a statutory timeline. An employer of record (EOR) and staffing entity may retain foreign currency receipts in offshore bank accounts or convert them to Canadian dollars at a freely negotiated rate. Transactions above CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC as large cash or wire transfers, and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and Statistics Canada may collect trade data for statistical purposes. No mandatory conversion ratio or holding period applies to export proceeds or cross-border service income.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulation of banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money laundering supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Moderate-to-High viability as a complementary payment rail, but not as a substitute for a Schedule I bank account. Offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Payoneer, and Currencycloud must be registered as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and hold the requisite provincial regulatory status (e.g., British Columbia's Money Lenders Act). The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) imposes mandatory registration and operational risk management requirements. For a BC ULC engaged in global EOR payroll, licensed fintech EMIs can process low-value cross-border contractor payments efficiently, but the primary payroll, client trust balances, and capital escrow accounts must remain with a federally regulated Canadian bank to ensure legal protection under provincial employment standards and creditor claims in insolvency.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity or debt injections into the BC ULC must be routed via SWIFT MT103 or SWIFT MT202 according to the sending and intermediary bank requirements. The remitter field (50K) must disclose the actual shareholder or lender, the beneficiary field (59) must show the ULC's trust or operational account at a Canadian Schedule I bank, and the remittance information field (70) must contain a clear purpose code, such as 'EQUITY CONTRIBUTION TO CANADA ULC' or 'SHAREHOLDER LOAN TO CANADA ULC'. The ULC must provide the bank with a certified copy of the board resolution authorizing the capital injection and a completed source of funds declaration for the beneficial owner. All inbound wires exceeding CAD 10,000 are subject to FINTRAC large transaction reporting; the bank will verify the transaction against the Investment Canada Act thresholds and, if control is acquired, the non-resident shareholder may need to file a notification with Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada.The primary clearing rail for disbursing contractor wages and trade settlements is SWIFT MT103, sent directly from the ULC's Canadian corporate banking portal to beneficiary bank accounts worldwide. For large trade payables (e.g., subcontracting or licencing fees), a documentary letter of credit (Commercial LC) can be used through the ULC's bank, although open account transfers are the prevailing industry practice. For high-volume, low-value contractor payroll, the ULC may integrate a fintech EMI such as Wise using batch-transfer APIs, provided the EMI is FINTRAC-registered and the transfers are mirrored in the ULC's payroll ledger. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: C$1,000,000 aggregate non-arm's-length cross-border transactions; Master File: C$10,000,000 aggregate threshold; Country-by-Country Report: consolidated group revenue of C$900,000,000 or more

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain an offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) code, but incorporates economic-substance analysis through the GAAR in s. 245 ITA, the treaty abuse rule in the MLI (Article 29(9) applying to the Canada–US treaty), and PE attribution rules in s. 253 ITA / Article V. The BC ULC must demonstrate genuine employment contracts, payroll processing capabilities, local staffing personnel, and strategic control over its EOR activities. A ULC functioning as a mere invoicing conduit without real personnel or payroll infrastructure will fail treaty relief, lose the Small Business Deduction, and be reassessed as a shell with a PE of the foreign parent.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements (s. 163(2), s. 238 and s. 239 ITA)

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty of 50% of understated tax plus prescribed interest; on criminal conviction, a fine equal to 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to 5 years (summary conviction) or up to 14 years (indictment)

Director personal liability: Directors who direct or acquiesce to false filings are personally chargeable under s. 239(1); the CRA may impose travel restrictions and passport surrender pending prosecution; the court can order restitution of evaded taxes; under BC Business Corporations Act the shareholders of a ULC remain personally liable for corporate civil tax debts, effectively piercing the corporate veil
Failure to Withhold and Remit Source Deductions / Part XIII WHT

Statutory sanction: Unremitted amounts become immediately due; CRA discretion to apply seal or quarterly penalties; 10% failure-to-remit penalty on amounts not remitted within prescribed time; interest at the CRA prescribed rate compounded daily

Director personal liability: Under s. 227.1 ITA, directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII amounts regardless of delegation; liability survives corporate insolvency and dissolution; CRA may file a certificate and obtain a provincial court enforcement order, trigger banking garnishments, and freeze director travel pending criminal examination
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping / Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment under s. 245 ITA removes the treaty or Small Business Deduction benefit; s. 163.2 misrepresentation penalty applies up to C$100,000 and 50% of the tax benefit for gross negligence; the ULC profits are recharacterized as a PE profit of the foreign parent and assessed under Part XIV branch tax at 25%; treaty relief is denied retroactively with interest

Director personal liability: Directors approving a sham structure are exposed to breach of fiduciary duty claims and civil actions by the CRA for corporate strip-downs; where assets were upstreamed to shareholders before reassessment, courts have disregarded the ULC limitation structure and imposed restitution obligations on senior officers; travel bans and asset freezes may be ordered pending fraud or GAAR litigation
Unlicensed EOR / Employment Agency in BC

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines of up to C$5,000 per contravention; director orders to stop operating; adjudication orders for unpaid wages and statutory entitlements; retroactive CPP/EI/income-tax reassessments for misclassified independent contractors within the previous three years

Director personal liability: BC directors and officers may be personally fined for contraventions where the corporation is ordered to pay wages to EOR employees; failure to pay accrued wages constitutes criminal fraud under s. 380(1) Criminal Code; because the entity is a ULC, shareholders bear unlimited personal liability for wage claims and employment standards payments
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Compliance (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalty up to C$1,200,000 for an individual and C$10,000,000 for an entity for each violation; FINTRAC may suspend or terminate the business relationship; criminal prosecution under the PCMLTFA for knowingly attempting to evade registration

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly fail to prevent money-laundering practices face criminal liability with fines up to C$10,000,000 or imprisonment up to 5 years; the BC Prosecution Service may impose restrictions on leaving Canada upon charging; corporate registration and reputation loss multiplies personal civil exposure to affected EOR clients and employees
