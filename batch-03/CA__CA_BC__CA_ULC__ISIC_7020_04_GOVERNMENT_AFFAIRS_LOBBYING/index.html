Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a BC or extraprovincial ULC is a 'taxable Canadian corporation' under ITA s.89(1); however, if a joint election under ITA s.96(1) and Regulations 105(d) is filed, the ULC is deemed a partnership for Canadian income tax purposes. For U.S. federal tax purposes (with U.S. ownership), a Canadian ULC is a per se disregarded entity under Treas. Reg. §301.7701-2(b)(8)(ii), enabling U.S. flow-through treatment.

Available election forms

1) ITA s.96(1) / Reg. 105(d) ULC partnership election (Form RC4640) - retroactively treats the ULC and its members as a Canadian partnership; 2) U.S. check-the-box election under Treas. Reg. §301.7701-3 is NOT available for a Canadian ULC because it is statutorily classified as a disregarded entity; 3) GST/HST group or closely related corporation election under ETA s.156 (binding election to treat qualifying corporations as a single person) to simplify GST administration; 4) ITA s.150(2)(b) non-resident elections for branch filings, if the ULC has foreign members.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (ETA s.148(1) small supplier threshold measured on worldwide taxable supplies over the current and immediately preceding four calendar quarters; a ULC whose only services are zero-rated advisory exports must still register once the threshold is exceeded; voluntary registration is available).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under ETA Sch. VI, Part V, s.9 to a supply of a service made to a non-resident who is not registered under Division V of the ETA, where the service is not for consumption in Canada and does not fall within s.9(2) exceptions (services in respect of real or tangible personal property situated in Canada, or physical performance in Canada). Cross-border trade policy analysis, research reports, and foreign stakeholder lobbying advisory services supplied to foreign principals qualify when delivered remotely and paid from abroad; however, advocacy directed at influencing Canadian government decisions is a Canadian-consumed service and cannot be zero-rated, so lobbying fees must be carefully separated contractually.

  • Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) electronic export declaration - required only for goods, not services
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill - only where tangible deliverables (reports, hard-copy materials) are shipped
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank wire/SWIFT confirmation evidencing settlement of the receivable
  • Signed service agreement identifying the non-resident recipient, place of supply, and scope of advisory services
  • GST/HST compliant tax invoice per ETA s.169(4) and CRA Guide RC4022 showing 'zero-rated'
  • Certificate of Residence or foreign tax registration evidence validating non-resident status

None for B2B: Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal invoice clearance, no digital stamping, and no CRA API pre-authorization of invoices. The CRA mandates electronic filing of GST/HST returns for registrants with annual electronic taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1,000,000 (GST/HST EDI or GST/HST NETFILE). The Pan-Canadian e-Invoicing standard (PEPPOL - Canada's Digital Interoperability Framework) applies to business-to-government invoicing on a phased, voluntary basis for B2B. Invoices must satisfy ETA s.169(4) content requirements (supplier name, BN, date, description of supply, GST paid/payable or zero-rated indication).

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year reassessment period (ETA s.298) and claw back zero-rating, charging 5% GST plus interest, if the non-resident status, place of supply, or supporting documentation is deficient. Fraudulent zero-rating claims attract a 25% gross-negligence penalty under ETA s.285.1(2); directors are liable under ETA s.323; criminal prosecution under Criminal Code s.380 applies for false or misleading statements under ETA s.327(1). Books and records must be retained for six years. Canada imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; however, for cross-border related-party receivables outstanding beyond 180 days, CRA may recharacterize the balance as a deemed dividend (ITA s.15(1)), deemed benefit (ITA s.56(2)), or apply transfer-pricing adjustments (ITA s.247(2)) creating Part XIII withholding exposure.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for ordinary commercial and financial transactions. Canada generally has no exchange-control approval requirement, capital-account quota, mandatory repatriation rule, or general restriction on acquiring foreign currency. A British Columbia ULC may generally receive foreign investment, remit dividends, repay documented shareholder loans, pay service fees, and transfer legitimate business funds cross-border, subject to corporate-law solvency requirements, withholding tax, transfer-pricing rules, treaty eligibility, sanctions, AML controls, beneficial-ownership verification, and bank transaction monitoring. Dividend, interest, royalty, management-fee, and other non-resident payments may require Canadian withholding tax and applicable information reporting. A ULC does not automatically obtain pass-through treatment for Canadian tax purposes; its Canadian corporate status, any foreign tax classification, treaty position, and related-party payment profile must be analyzed separately.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or prescribed deadline for converting export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may generally remain in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts and may be repatriated or applied offshore, subject to contractual banking terms, sanctions, AML review, tax reporting, customs documentation where applicable, and accurate characterization of the underlying transaction. Exporters should retain invoices, contracts, shipping or service-delivery evidence, payment records, and foreign-exchange confirmations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; foreign-exchange administration is not conducted through a separate Canadian foreign-exchange control authority. Anti-money-laundering supervision for reporting entities is principally conducted by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), while prudential supervision of federally regulated banks is conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. A foreign electronic-money institution or digital payment provider may be used for lawful corporate collections, payments, and foreign-currency balances if it accepts Canadian corporations, supports Canada-related sanctions and AML controls, and provides adequate transaction records. It should not be treated as a substitute for a Canadian deposit-taking bank where deposit protection, local clearing, cash management, payroll, trust segregation, or institutional counterparty requirements are important. Payment-service activities conducted in Canada may fall within the federal Retail Payment Activities Act and require Bank of Canada registration, operational-risk controls, safeguarding arrangements, incident reporting, and compliance with applicable requirements. The provider's own licence, permitted jurisdictions, safeguarding model, account ownership, insolvency treatment, transaction limits, and termination rights must be verified. Canadian tax, withholding, sanctions, privacy, and books-and-records obligations remain applicable.

Route equity through a Canadian commercial-bank operational or designated capital account using a SWIFT wire or other bank-supported international transfer. For shareholder loans, document interest, maturity, repayment priority, currency, transfer-pricing basis, and withholding-tax treatment. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium. A foreign electronic-money institution or digital payment provider may be used for lawful corporate collections, payments, and foreign-currency balances if it accepts Canadian corporations, supports Canada-related sanctions and AML controls, and provides adequate transaction records. It should not be treated as a substitute for a Canadian deposit-taking bank where deposit protection, local clearing, cash management, payroll, trust segregation, or institutional counterparty requirements are important. Payment-service activities conducted in Canada may fall within the federal Retail Payment Activities Act and require Bank of Canada registration, operational-risk controls, safeguarding arrangements, incident reporting, and compliance with applicable requirements. The provider's own licence, permitted jurisdictions, safeguarding model, account ownership, insolvency treatment, transaction limits, and termination rights must be verified. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general federal and British Columbia corporate income tax (15% federal + 12% BC); 11% combined rate for Canadian-controlled private corporation income eligible for the small business deduction (9% federal + 2% BC)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty relief.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1 million aggregate non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents triggers T106 reporting; contemporaneous Local File transfer pricing records are expected at or above C$5 million aggregate controlled transaction value. Master File and Country-by-Country Reporting generally apply to multinational groups meeting C$750 million consolidated revenue.

Economic substance scope: No standalone Economic Substance Regulations exist in Canada. CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule under ITA s. 245, the sham doctrine, and permanent establishment allocation principles. A government-affairs ULC must maintain a BC office, active management, employees, signed retainers, time records, board minutes, and local bank accounts; a mailbox or agent-only arrangement will be challenged and treaty benefits denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 239: on summary conviction, fine of 50%-200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, fine of 50%-200% and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil gross negligence penalty under s. 163(2): 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who counsel, approve, or sign false returns can be individually prosecuted as parties. CRA may assert director liability for unremitted amounts under s. 227.1; court orders may include asset freezing, departure restrictions, and travel bans pending resolution.
Unlicensed Lobbying and Shell Veil Abuse

Statutory sanction: Unregistered lobbying under British Columbia's Lobbyists Registration Act exposes the corporation to monetary penalties, public disclosure, and potential criminal liability. Tax consequences include denial of deductions, GAAR reassessment, and unlimited shareholder liability under BC ULC statutes for corporate obligations.

Director personal liability: Shareholders of a BC ULC are statutorily unlimited liable for corporate debts. Directors and controlling shareholders who hollow out the ULC can be personally liable for civil restitution and disgorgement; courts may impose asset freezes and travel bans.
Transfer Pricing Misreporting / Thin Capitalization

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust to the arm's-length amount under s. 247(3), disallow excessive interest deductions, and impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty under s. 247(1) where documentation is absent and the adjustment threshold is met.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve false transfer pricing schedules may be liable for gross negligence penalties. CRA may disregard the corporation as a sham and pursue shareholders and directors for resulting tax debts, with travel bans available during collection enforcement.
Unremitted Part XIII Withholding / Repatriation Fraud

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s. 227(8), failure to withhold or remit imposes penalties and interest on the unremitted amount; CRA may assess the Canadian payer and commence criminal proceedings for repeated non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII amounts under s. 227.1 regardless of express misconduct. CRA may register certificates against personal assets and obtain court orders restricting departure where assets are being removed from Canada.
