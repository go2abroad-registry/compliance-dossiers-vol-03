E-government filing portal

A foreign entity obtains a Canadian Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account by filing the Non-Resident Registration form (RC118) with the CRA, or through the Business Registration Online service. A BC PST account is obtained via eTaxBC if the non-resident has a BC taxable presence, marketplace obligation, or otherwise makes taxable sales in British Columbia. The BN becomes the TIN; DUNS or ISO 17442 LEI may be required for financial-sector licensing and AML compliance.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general 'check-the-box' election; a BC ULC is a separate taxable Canadian corporation under the Income Tax Act. However, for U.S. federal tax purposes, a BC ULC (not being a per se corporation) may file IRS Form 8832 to elect disregarded entity or partnership status. Within Canada, the CRA requires all corporations to file T2 returns and report income at entity level, subject to a corporate/partnership hybrid mismatch limitation under the OECD Multilateral Instrument. GST/HST related-corporation section 156 election is available for closely related corporations; BC PST aggregation rules may also apply.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies triggers the GST/HST small-supplier registration threshold; non-residents making Canadian taxable supplies above the threshold must register. BC PST registration is generally required on the first taxable sale in BC once nexus or marketplace-facilitator status exists; no general PST small-supplier threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Tangible personal property exported from Canada by the supplier is zero-rated under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. A cross-border payment gateway's financial services supplied to a non-resident are generally zero-rated if the supply is made to a non-resident and is not for use in Canada, provided the services constitute a 'financial service' for GST/HST purposes; where the supply is exempt rather than zero-rated, input tax credits are unavailable. Claiming 0% requires the supplier to retain documentary proof of export and non-resident status.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A or equivalent ACI/CCVD e-manifest)
  • Commercial invoice and proof of export contract
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / rail or truck carrier manifest
  • Non-resident inquiry evidence on counterparty status
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/PSP settlement notice proving cross-border payment receipts
  • Service agreement identifying the recipient as non-resident and place of supply

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal electronic clearance or digital stamping. GST/HST returns are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or the CRA My Business Account; PST returns are filed through eTaxBC. Taxpayers must keep electronic records and provide an electronic invoice on request. Large registrants are subject to mandatory electronic filing/remittance under CRA administrative policy and GST/HST Regulations.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year reassessment window (extended to ten years for misrepresentation or fraud) to claw back zero-rating and refunds. False or negligent GST/HST refund claims attract a 50% penalty on the overstated refund under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act, plus daily interest and compound interest; fraudulent claims may lead to 100% penalties, director liability, criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and Criminal Code, and cancellation of registration. BC PST audits may similarly impose a penalty of 25% of unreported PST, a 7% annual interest, and enforcement through the Consumer Protection Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has an open capital account and full current-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII (accepted in 1952). The Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act do not impose exchange licences, conversion permits, or prior approval for profit remittance. The only material remittance friction for a BC ULC is Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, s.212 (statutory 25%; treaty-reduced to 5% on dividends paid to a US corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of voting shares under the Canada-US Tax Convention). Fee-based cross-border payment-gateway revenue received from foreign merchants is freely convertible and remittable. No capital-account registration, BC-specific exchange certification, or non-resident dividend clearance mechanism exists.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no surrender ratio, statutory repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion quota on export receipts or cross-border service income. The Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951. The PSP may retain USD, EUR, and other settlement currencies in offshore accounts or in Canadian foreign-currency bank accounts indefinitely without conversion obligations. The only residual compliance requirement is statistical reporting to Statistics Canada and Bank of Canada Balance of Payments surveys when aggregate international transaction volumes exceed prescribed thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting as retail payment service provider supervisor under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA, SC 2021, c. 23); prudential supervision of clearing banks is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering supervision is vested in FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada maintains no standalone foreign-exchange control agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: LOW-MEDIUM (conditional). An offshore digital EMI (UK FCA-authorised EMI, EU-licensed e-money institution, or US state money transmitter) cannot serve as the primary clearing bank for the BC ULC. Direct participation in Lynx (high-value CAD) and ACSS (retail CAD) is restricted to Payments Canada members, which are exclusively chartered banks; RPAA safeguarding rules require end-user funds to be segregated with a Canadian financial institution, covered by a surety bond, or otherwise protected in Canada. The workable architecture is hybrid: the ULC holds its master settlement account and safeguarded RPAA trust account at a Schedule I bank, while the EMI is layered only as a secondary payout or distribution rail for sub-merchant liquidity (e.g., Wise Platform or Stripe Connect). FINTRAC money-services-business registration remains mandatory for the ULC regardless of any offshore EMI licensing.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia provincial tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit: 9% federal plus 2% British Columbia.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to a reduced rate under an applicable tax treaty. The Canada-United States treaty commonly reduces the rate to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder that beneficially owns at least 10% of the voting power and to 15% in other treaty cases. Other treaties commonly provide rates of 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and treaty conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of every taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may obtain a three-month balance-due period if the statutory conditions are met, including the applicable taxable-income limitation. Tax instalments are due monthly unless a valid quarterly-installment rule applies. A corporation with a permanent establishment in British Columbia files the federal T2 with the applicable British Columbia schedules rather than a separate ordinary provincial corporate income tax return.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The filing and payment deadline depends on the assigned reporting period and registrant type. Annual filers generally file and remit within three months after the fiscal year-end for corporations, subject to statutory exceptions. Monthly and quarterly returns are generally due one month after the reporting period. A payment gateway must classify merchant-processing, platform, settlement, agency, financial-service, and cross-border supplies correctly.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is optional for a non-arm's-length cross-border transaction. Contemporaneous documentation must support arm's-length pricing and generally be prepared by the filing deadline and provided to CRA within three months of a written request. Form T106 reporting generally applies where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and filing rules. Canada does not impose a separate general Master File or Local File threshold in the same format used by some jurisdictions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to the United Arab Emirates or certain other ESR regimes. CRA may nevertheless challenge arrangements under the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty-purpose analysis, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, interest deductibility and excessive-interest rules, and sham or misuse doctrines. A Canadian ULC should maintain real Canadian governance and operational evidence where it claims Canadian residence or performs gateway functions, including directors' decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational control, risk management, technology control, compliance oversight, banking authority, contractual authority, and records of merchant and settlement activities.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion, false statements, and deliberate tax suppression

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, tax recovery, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally up to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and penalties for repeated or late filing. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act; GST/HST offences carry separate fines and imprisonment provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily liable merely because a corporation owes corporate income tax. They may face personal exposure for knowing participation in offences, conspiracy, obstruction, false statements, or tax-evasion conduct. Directors can also face derivative liability for unremitted payroll source deductions and GST/HST where statutory due-diligence defenses fail. Criminal conviction may result in probation, restitution, asset restraints, and passport or travel restrictions ordered by a court; a routine tax debt does not automatically create a travel ban.
Failure to remit Part XIII withholding tax

Statutory sanction: The payer remains liable for the unwithheld amount, plus interest and applicable failure-to-remit penalties. Directors and officers may be assessed personally in appropriate circumstances where statutory liability provisions apply or where they knowingly participated in the failure.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic for ordinary corporate withholding errors, but directors can be assessed under applicable derivative-liability rules and may face criminal exposure for deliberate evasion, false records, or obstruction. Civil recovery may include amounts that should have been withheld, interest, and penalties.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and insufficient documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment, deny deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory thresholds and conditions are met, assess interest, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse principles.

Director personal liability: Directors generally face corporate rather than automatic personal tax liability. Personal exposure may arise from knowing false statements, obstruction, fraudulent records, conspiracy, or derivative liability for specified source deductions and indirect taxes. Independent tax advice and documented approval do not cure a deliberately artificial arrangement.
Unlicensed payment services and regulatory non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The Bank of Canada may impose administrative monetary penalties under the Retail Payment Activities Act and may issue compliance directions or pursue enforcement. Unauthorized deposit-taking or other regulated financial activity may trigger federal or provincial offences, injunctions, licence denial, operational prohibition, and monetary penalties. Exact exposure depends on the activity, statutory commencement status, entity role, and enforcement provision.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted where they authorize, consent to, or participate in contraventions. Orders may include compliance directions, production obligations, restitution where legally available, fines, and in serious cases imprisonment under the applicable statute. Travel restrictions are not an automatic consequence and require an applicable court order.
Anti-money laundering and terrorist-financing violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can be substantial and are determined by violation and compliance history. Criminal money-laundering and terrorist-financing offences can produce major fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment for up to ten years or more for specified offences. Proceeds may be restrained, seized, or forfeited.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, compliance officers, and employees may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, wilful blindness, counselling, or obstruction. A corporate veil does not protect intentional AML misconduct. Court-ordered asset restraint, forfeiture, restitution, probation, and travel restrictions may arise in criminal proceedings.
Sanctions, fraud, and payment-network abuse

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include asset freezes, transaction prohibitions, criminal prosecution, forfeiture, regulatory enforcement, network termination, correspondent-bank de-risking, civil damages, and loss of payment licences or registrations. Sanctions penalties and imprisonment depend on the applicable Canadian sanctions regime and offence.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize or knowingly facilitate prohibited transactions may face fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, director-and-officer disqualification consequences, and court-imposed travel restrictions. Corporate indemnification may be unavailable for criminal fines or deliberate misconduct.
Corporate insolvency, trust-property, and merchant-fund misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential remedies include tracing, constructive trust, preference or transfer-at-undervalue claims, oppression remedies, receivership, bankruptcy proceedings, civil damages, director-liability claims, and criminal fraud charges where dishonesty is established.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for unlawful dividends, unpaid wages or source deductions, oppressive conduct, fraudulent conveyances, insolvent trading-related claims where applicable, and knowing breach of trust. Civil restitution and asset recovery are possible; imprisonment requires a separate criminal offence and conviction.
Beneficial-ownership concealment and shell-company conduct

Statutory sanction: Possible consequences include corporate-register or AML penalties, tax reassessment, denial of treaty benefits, GAAR application, confiscation or forfeiture, civil tracing, licence action, and criminal prosecution for fraud, laundering, sanctions evasion, or false records.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal civil restitution, forfeiture, criminal fines, imprisonment, and court-ordered restrictions where they knowingly participated in concealment. A ULC's unlimited liability does not automatically make shareholders or directors personally liable, but it may increase insolvency recovery exposure against the corporation.
