Stage 03

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — national tax administration portal: 'My Business Account' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html); business registration via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO). The British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST) is administered separately by the BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch (portal: eTaxBC, https://etax.gov.bc.ca); customs and export documentation is administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

E-government filing portal

For a BC-incorporated ULC: (1) incorporate under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act (SBC 2002, c.57) via BC Registry Services to obtain the BC Incorporation Number and corporate registry profile; (2) obtain the federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) using Form RC1/RC1A, which creates program accounts: corporate income tax account (RC0001), GST/HST account (RT0001), and payroll deductions account (RP0001) if the company employs individuals; BN issuance typically occurs within 1–3 business days and may be immediate through the online connector; (3) open a BC provincial PST account via eTaxBC (the Small Seller Exemption applies to vendors with no place of business in BC and annual BC taxable sales not exceeding CAD 10,000); (4) register a CBSA export/import program account (RM0001) only if physical network hardware or goods cross the border — pure VPN/SaaS digital supplies require no customs program account; (5) for a foreign (non-resident) entity supplying VPN services into Canada, submit CRA Form RC1 using a foreign address to obtain a non-resident BN and GST/HST account (RT program) under the 2021 e-commerce framework; the BN must be quoted on all GST/HST invoices and used in the simplified GST/HST NETFILE return; (6) maintain BC Registry annual filings and, where non-resident shareholders hold 25% or more of the ULC, comply with transparency/beneficial-ownership reporting under BC corporate registry rules.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — a BC ULC is a domestic 'corporation' under Income Tax Act (RSC 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.)) s.248(1) and a 'taxable Canadian corporation' under s.89(1); it is deemed resident in Canada under ITA s.250(4)(a) by virtue of incorporation in Canada after April 26, 1965, unless a tax treaty tie-breaker relieves residence. The ULC files Form T2 corporate income tax returns at the federal general rate of 15% plus the BC provincial general corporate rate of 12% (combined approximately 27% for a non-CCPC or for income not eligible for the small business deduction). If the ULC is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) — i.e., not controlled directly or indirectly by non-residents — it qualifies for the federal small business rate of 9% and the BC small business rate of 2% on qualified active business income up to CAD 500,000 (combined approximately 11% on that tranche). For GST/HST purposes, the ULC is a 'person' and 'corporation' under Excise Tax Act s.123 and, once registered, is a regular GST/HST registrant. The ULC is 'Pass_Through_Eligible' solely under foreign asymmetric classification rules (US check-the-box under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3), which may treat the Canadian ULC as a partnership or a disregarded entity for US federal tax while it remains a fully taxable corporation under Canadian domestic law; no territorial or tax-exempt zone treatment applies. Canada taxes the worldwide income of resident corporations. — For a BC-incorporated ULC: (1) incorporate under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act (SBC 2002, c.57) via BC Registry Services to obtain the BC Incorporation Number and corporate registry profile; (2) obtain the federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) using Form RC1/RC1A, which creates program accounts: corporate income tax account (RC0001), GST/HST account (RT0001), and payroll deductions account (RP0001) if the company employs individuals; BN issuance typically occurs within 1–3 business days and may be immediate through the online connector; (3) open a BC provincial PST account via eTaxBC (the Small Seller Exemption applies to vendors with no place of business in BC and annual BC taxable sales not exceeding CAD 10,000); (4) register a CBSA export/import program account (RM0001) only if physical network hardware or goods cross the border — pure VPN/SaaS digital supplies require no customs program account; (5) for a foreign (non-resident) entity supplying VPN services into Canada, submit CRA Form RC1 using a foreign address to obtain a non-resident BN and GST/HST account (RT program) under the 2021 e-commerce framework; the BN must be quoted on all GST/HST invoices and used in the simplified GST/HST NETFILE return; (6) maintain BC Registry annual filings and, where non-resident shareholders hold 25% or more of the ULC, comply with transparency/beneficial-ownership reporting under BC corporate registry rules.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a BC ULC is a domestic 'corporation' under Income Tax Act (RSC 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.)) s.248(1) and a 'taxable Canadian corporation' under s.89(1); it is deemed resident in Canada under ITA s.250(4)(a) by virtue of incorporation in Canada after April 26, 1965, unless a tax treaty tie-breaker relieves residence. The ULC files Form T2 corporate income tax returns at the federal general rate of 15% plus the BC provincial general corporate rate of 12% (combined approximately 27% for a non-CCPC or for income not eligible for the small business deduction). If the ULC is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) — i.e., not controlled directly or indirectly by non-residents — it qualifies for the federal small business rate of 9% and the BC small business rate of 2% on qualified active business income up to CAD 500,000 (combined approximately 11% on that tranche). For GST/HST purposes, the ULC is a 'person' and 'corporation' under Excise Tax Act s.123 and, once registered, is a regular GST/HST registrant. The ULC is 'Pass_Through_Eligible' solely under foreign asymmetric classification rules (US check-the-box under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3), which may treat the Canadian ULC as a partnership or a disregarded entity for US federal tax while it remains a fully taxable corporation under Canadian domestic law; no territorial or tax-exempt zone treatment applies. Canada taxes the worldwide income of resident corporations.

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box regime. Available statutory elections for the BC ULC / VPN SaaS structure include: (i) Income Tax Act s.261 functional currency election — a Canadian-resident corporation whose books are kept in US dollars may elect to compute Canadian tax in USD, which is relevant for USD-denominated VPN subscription revenue (election filed within 3 months of the tax year-end; revocation requires CRA consent); (ii) Excise Tax Act s.156 joint election — closely related corporations (90% common control) may elect to treat intercompany taxable supplies as made for nil consideration, neutralizing GST/HST within the corporate group; (iii) hybrid/ULC asymmetry — the US shareholder may file IRS Form 8832 to elect US classification as a partnership or disregarded entity while the entity remains a taxable Canadian corporation, enabling the 'ULC capped-loss' structure under the Canada–US Income Tax Convention (1980, as amended), including Article IV residency analysis and Article XXIX-B hybrid rules; (iv) Excise Tax Act s.240 election — a non-resident registrant may elect to remit GST/HST on a cash basis rather than an accrual basis; (v) Income Tax Act s.216/s.216.1 elections apply only where the non-resident holds Canadian rental or timber property and are not relevant to the VPN business.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies over four consecutive calendar quarters (Excise Tax Act s.148, small supplier threshold). A non-resident supplier of VPN/digital services must register for GST/HST once its Canadian e-commerce taxable supplies exceed this CAD 30,000 threshold; the 2021 e-commerce GST/HST framework (effective July 1, 2021) imposes registration and collection obligations on non-resident vendors and marketplace platforms, with no separate threshold. Once registered, GST at 5% applies to VPN services supplied to Canadian residents; exports are zero-rated. BC PST: registration is required for vendors with a place of business in BC; remote sellers with no BC physical presence qualify for the Small Seller Exemption while annual BC taxable sales do not exceed CAD 10,000, and in all cases digital products delivered electronically are outside the PST base.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of VPN network privacy application services (software licences, SaaS, digital encryption services) to a non-resident recipient are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: s.7 zero-rates services supplied to a non-resident where the service is not in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada; s.10 zero-rates supplies of intangible personal property (including digital software and encryption technology) to a non-resident where the intangible is not used in Canada and does not relate to real estate in Canada. The BC ULC must collect and retain contemporaneous documentary evidence establishing: (a) the non-resident status of the customer (legal name, foreign business address, foreign tax identification number); and (b) delivery of the VPN service outside Canada (server geolocation, IP-based delivery logs, or electronic transmission records to non-resident infrastructure). Supplies made to Canadian residents remain subject to 5% GST and are not zero-rated. If the company also exports physical network hardware, s.10 of Schedule VI and the CRA Exporters' Guide (RC4091) require additional proof-of-export through CBSA.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or CADEX electronic declaration) — mandatory for physical goods; for pure digital VPN/SaaS exports, no B13A is issued and the supplier retains server logs and electronic delivery records demonstrating offshore supply
  • Bill of Lading — applicable to physical hardware shipments; for digital services, electronic delivery receipts, IP-geolocation records, and transfer logs serve as the delivery evidence
  • Inward Remittance Certificate — CRA-corroborating bank confirmation (or SWIFT MT103, Stripe/Paymentwall settlement reports) evidencing payment received from the non-resident recipient in convertible currency
  • GST/HST zero-rated invoice (s.226 Excise Tax Act) specifying the vendor BN, purchaser name and foreign address, and stating 'Zero-rated supply to non-resident' with reference to Schedule VI, Part V
  • Written contract or service agreement with the non-resident customer, including the recipient's foreign tax ID or business registration certificate, to substantiate the offshore nature of the VPN supply

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory API transmission, digital stamping, or government-clearance gateway requirement operates in Canada. Invoices under Excise Tax Act s.226(1) must contain: the supplier's legal name (BC ULC) and Business Number; the GST/HST registration number (RT account); invoice date; purchaser name; description of the VPN/digital supply; consideration; and the tax amount or a 'zero-rated export' notation. Simplified invoices (s.226(2)) are permitted for taxable supplies of CAD 150 or less. Electronic invoicing in PDF, XML, EDI, or Peppol BIS 3.0 format is fully sanctioned by the CRA's Electronic Books and Records guidance (IC 05-1), provided records remain accessible for audit. Participation in the Pan-Canadian Peppol interoperability framework for business-to-government e-invoicing is voluntary; there is no statutory mandate for clearance via API or fiscal stamp.

CRA conducts targeted audit reviews of export zero-rating claims by digital-service exporters, including VPN/SaaS businesses, using third-party payment data from Stripe, Paymentwall, and other rails. The standard reassessment window is 4 years under Excise Tax Act s.298(2); the limitation period is eliminated where the taxpayer has made a misrepresentation or a non-resident registrant is involved. Consequences of failed substantiation or fraud: (i) retrospective clawback of zero-rating, reassessment at 5% GST on the full export revenue, disallowance of input tax credits, and repayment of any refunds claimed with CRA prescribed arrears interest compounded daily; (ii) civil false-statement penalty under ETA s.285.1 — CAD 100 plus 25% of the net tax understated or refund overstated; (iii) criminal evasion under ETA s.280 — on indictment, a fine of up to CAD 200,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years; (iv) late-filing/failure-to-remit penalty under ETA s.280.1 — 5% of unpaid net tax plus 1% per full month of delinquency for up to 24 months; (v) director liability for unremitted GST/HST under ETA s.323 and, uniquely for a BC ULC, personal liability of shareholders because unlimited liability permits creditors and the CRA to pursue the shareholders directly where the ULC cannot satisfy the tax debt — a distinctive and material audit clawback risk.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility under IMF Article VIII. The Canadian dollar trades freely with no general exchange controls, no advance approval for inward or outward transfers, and no separate hurdle for profit remittance. Remittance of dividends, interest, royalties, or management fees is subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding tax and documentary proof of beneficial ownership, but not to FX conversion approval. Legal and compliance hurdles are limited to anti-money-laundering verification, sanctions screening, and the receiving bank's export/import documentation requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no holding-period obligation for export receipts under Canadian federal or British Columbia legislation. Exporters may retain foreign currency in authorized foreign currency bank accounts and deploy proceeds freely. Cross-border wires equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 remain reportable to FINTRAC, and supporting commercial documents such as invoices or contracts must be available on request.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential banking regulation supervised by OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions); cross-border transaction reporting monitored by FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs (electronic money institutions) are not recognized as banks in Canada and cannot become direct members of Canadian clearing systems. A non-resident EMI may operate as a third-party payment facilitator only if it is registered as a money services business (MSB) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and reports to FINTRAC; provincial registration may also apply. Feasibility rating: Medium for supporting the target payment rails (Stripe, Paymentwall) and Low for core treasury clearing, because Canadian-dollar settlement and regulatory reliability require a Schedule I or II bank account and payment rails, not an unwired offshore EMI.
