Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Business Registration Online (BRO) / My Business Account portal; British Columbia Ministry of Finance — Consumer Taxation Branch for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) registration through BCeID.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned BC unlimited liability corporation (ULC) must first complete BC corporate registration, then obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through CRA's Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1. The ULC must open a GST/HST registrant program account (RT) if it makes taxable supplies in Canada, a corporate income tax program account (RC) for federal/provincial filings, and a payroll account (RP) for Canadian crew or office employees. For British Columbia PST, registration is effected separately with the BC Consumer Taxation Branch and is mandatory for any person making taxable sales into British Columbia, including non-resident vendors; the BC registration uses BCeID and assigns a PST number linked to the underlying corporate account. Non-resident ULCs must ensure service of process is legally permitted in Canada and may be required to designate an agent or Canadian address if requested by CRA. The operational statutory tax identifier is the nine-digit Business Number, optionally with two-letter program account suffixes for GST/HST, payroll, and corporate income tax.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box classification election; a BC ULC is fiscally opaque for Canadian federal/provincial income tax purposes and is taxed at the entity level regardless of unlimited shareholder liability. The 'Pass_Through_Eligible' status in the commercial entity framework refers solely to the ULC's eligibility for a U.S. federal check-the-box disregarded-entity or partnership election; that U.S. election has no Canadian tax effect. Available statutory elections relevant to the crewing agency include: the GST/HST election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act for closely related corporations to eliminate GST/HST on intercompany supplies; the annual or quarterly GST/HST filing election for qualifying registrants; and, where applicable, the election to have certain non-resident supplier registrations accepted for GST/HST self-assessment. No PST consolidated filing election exists in British Columbia; each taxable seller must register and remit separately unless a limited common ownership exemption applies.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For GST/HST, the small-supplier threshold is CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada in a single calendar quarter or in four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, mandatory registration is required. For BC PST, there is no monetary registration threshold; registration is required for every person, resident or non-resident, making taxable sales, leases, or imports in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods, zero-rating is available under Schedule VI, Part VI of the Excise Tax Act when goods are exported under a customs export declaration. For crew recruitment and vessel crew management services supplied to non-resident ship owners, operators, or maritime employers, the service is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 where the supply is made to a non-resident person and the service is not performed wholly or partly in Canada for the benefit of persons present in Canada, and is not in respect of real or tangible personal property situated in Canada. Supplies of international maritime transportation services and ancillary crew management may also qualify as zero-rated supplies if the ship is used principally in international water-borne transportation. The agency must obtain and preserve contemporaneous written evidence that the recipient is a non-resident, that the service location is outside Canada, and that the consideration is attributable to a service performed for a foreign vessel operator.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form A1 or equivalent electronic export reporting record) for any physical goods, equipment, or ship stores exported
  • Bill of Lading or signed sea waybill evidencing export movement
  • Inward Remittance Certificate/SWIFT MT103 or bank remittance evidence of foreign currency receipt
  • Executed crewing or manning agreement identifying the non-resident shipowner/operator as principal
  • Tax invoice stating the GST/HST zero-rated status and the supplier's Business Number
  • Vessel registry certificate or foreign-flag documentation confirming non-resident ownership or charter
  • Crew engagement forms, relief reports, or movement manifests proving the service was delivered in respect of an international voyage

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing APIs, or digital stamping. GST/HST invoices may be issued electronically (EDIFACT, PDF, or other IRP-compatible formats) without CRA pre-clearance. Every GST/HST invoice must contain the supplier's Business Number, the GST/HST registration number, invoice date, invoice number, recipient identification, description of the supply, tax rate, and total tax charged. BC PST invoices must separately indicate the amount of PST collected; the PST rate for services is 7%, with certain exemptions available. CRA requires full electronic record retention and production in tax audits; there is no transaction-level government clearance server. A crew recruitment agency using cross-border payment rails should maintain the underlying payment messages (e.g., SWIFT MT103) as part of the invoice and export substantiation file.

CRA audit teams scrutinize zero-rating claims on export services for absence of non-resident evidence, missing contemporaneous export documentation, artificial invoicing, or delayed/non-repatriated foreign currency. Where a zero-rating claim is unsupported, CRA may reassess the supply at the full 5% GST or 12% combined rate, add interest, impose civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions, and extend the normal reassessment period to 7 years or indefinitely where gross negligence or misrepresentation has occurred. Exporters who knowingly sign false certificates, use forged bills of lading, or conceal unrepatriated proceeds face penalties, criminal prosecution, and potential liability of principals for remitted but uncollected tax amounts. Failure to repatriate consideration within approximately 180 days is treated as a factual indicator that the supply may have been sold on domestic terms or that the required export may not have occurred; the taxpayer must provide commercial justification and contractual payment terms explaining any deviation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility of the Canadian dollar (CAD) on both current and capital accounts; the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951 and no exchange-control approvals exist. Dividends, repatriated capital, and service-related profit remittances by a British Columbia ULC to non-resident shareholders are unrestricted by FX regulation, subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (Income Tax Act) and FINTRAC large-transaction reporting for cash or EFT transfers exceeding CAD 10,000.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory repatriation period, and no minimum conversion ratio for export receipts or cross-border service revenue (including international crew recruitment fees). Receipts may be retained in foreign-currency accounts at Canadian financial institutions without compulsory conversion; amounts must be translated to CAD at the relevant CRA published exchange rate for income tax purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential regulation by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and AML/CTF supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); Canada has no dedicated Foreign Exchange Control Board.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium with legal guardrails. Canada does not recognize the European EMI passport; a foreign digital EMI (e.g., Wise Business, Revolut) must register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and, once the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) mandate is fully effective, register as a payment service provider with the Bank of Canada. Such providers are viable only for ancillary multi-currency crew payroll disbursements and low-value supplier payments; they are not permissible for statutory escrow accounts, high-value treasury settlement, or SWIFT-based capital injection rails at an acceptable counterparty risk level.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (15% Federal Part I corporate rate under the Income Tax Act plus 12% British Columbia general corporate income tax rate under the Provincial Income Tax Act)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. Under the Canada-US DTA, the rate is reduced to 5% for a US corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the voting shares of the BC ULC, and to 15% for other portfolio shareholders.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada's s.247 transfer-pricing rules do not impose a formal Local File / Master File filing threshold; CRA requires contemporaneous documentation for all controlled transactions involving non-residents. A documented penalty threshold applies where the net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds C$5,000,000 in a taxation year or where the transaction value triggers CRA review. Country-by-Country Report (CBCR) filing obligation under Form T106 and BEPS Action 13 applies where the multinational group has consolidated revenue of at least C$1,000,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada imposes functional economic-substance scrutiny through s.247 transfer-pricing rules and the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) in s.245 of the Income Tax Act. The BC ULC must demonstrate real decision-making, personnel, contracts, invoicing, bank accounts, and risk exposure in BC; a shell entity that merely routes crew-management fees to an offshore principal will be re-characterized, and a s.245 GAAR adjustment may apply disgorging the tax benefit. The CRA also applies Form T106 and Schedule 214 reporting to control the substance of intercompany arrangements.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): on indictment, a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment of up to five years; on summary conviction, a fine of up to C$50,000 and/or imprisonment of up to two years. Civil assessment includes the full understated tax, gross negligence penalty of 50% under s.163(2), and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or knowingly participate in false filings are jointly and severally liable for unremitted amounts and may be criminally prosecuted. Courts may impose travel bans, asset freezes, surrender of passports, and extradition orders as bail conditions. Nominee directors do not escape liability where the evidence shows actual control and direction.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under s.247(3), a penalty of 10% of the amount by which the transfer price exceeds or falls below the arm’s-length amount, applied only where the adjustment exceeds C$5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue (whichever is less), plus ordinary reassessment interest. Failure to file Form T106 or Schedule 214 exposes additional penalties and an extended normal reassessment period for three years beyond the usual period.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign inaccurate T2 returns or false intercompany agreements may be subject to civil penalties and criminal tax-evasion charges if the deliberate intent is proven. Directors of the ULC may face personal collection actions for s.227.1 source-deductions, and where a foreign parent operates a sham Canadian entity, courts will pierce the corporate veil to reach the directing minds and assets.
AML / Shell Company / Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties up to C$2,000,000 for corporations and C$1,000,000 for individuals per violation; criminal money-laundering under s.462.31 of the Criminal Code carries imprisonment of up to 10 years. CRA may disregard the ULC as a sham under s.245 or recharacterize all flows to the beneficial owner.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for money laundering and fined or imprisoned. Non-resident directors risk travel bans, inadmissibility to Canada, seizure of revenues under civil forfeiture statutes, and personal liability for corporate AML failures where they are the sole directing mind. Voluntary corporate status does not shield the actual beneficial owner.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Source Deductions and Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s.227(2) and s.227(8), unpaid deductions plus interest are recoverable as Crown debt; criminal conviction under s.227(2.1) may result in imprisonment of up to two years and a fine of up to C$25,000. Excise Tax Act penalties for unremitted GST/HST include interest and a civil penalty of 10% of the amount in addition to the tax.

Director personal liability: Directors are deemed trustees of source deductions under s.227 and may be personally liable under s.227.1 for the entire unremitted amount, interest, and penalties. The director liability is several and joint, and a director cannot avoid liability through resignation after the amounts became payable, indemnification waivers, or shareholder approval.
