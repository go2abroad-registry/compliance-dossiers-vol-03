Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada); online portal: 'My Business Account' (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses.html) and Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/business-registration-online.html).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must obtain a Business Number (BN) from CRA. Apply through the Business Registration Online portal or by completing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) and submitting it to the designated CRA tax centre. Registration is mandatory if the non-resident carries on business in Canada, has a permanent establishment, or makes taxable supplies in Canada requiring GST/HST collection. For voluntary GST/HST registration, use Form GST1. The applicant must provide legal name, corporate status documentation, foreign registration number, representative address in Canada, and the date Canadian business activity commenced.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (federal and provincial corporate income tax); however, a BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) is Pass_Through_Eligible for U.S. federal tax purposes under IRC § 301.7701-3 and may be treated as fiscally transparent under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty.

Available election forms

No domestic 'check-the-box' election exists under Canadian tax law; classification is determined under the Income Tax Act (ITA). Hybrid mismatch rules are codified in ITA ss. 18.2–18.5, addressing deductible hybrid dividends and branch mismatches. U.S. owners of a BC ULC may file IRS Form 8832 to elect disregarded entity status for U.S. purposes; this election does not alter Canadian legal or tax treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in gross worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) made in any four consecutive calendar quarters; exceeding this threshold triggers mandatory GST/HST registration. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada with a permanent establishment may be required to register regardless of the threshold if taxable Canadian supplies exceed the small supplier limit.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST/HST zero-rating applies to (i) supplies of goods that are exported from Canada within 60 days of delivery (Schedule VI, Part I); and (ii) supplies of services rendered to a non-resident (Schedule VI, Part V) where the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property located in Canada at the time the service is performed, not a personal service to an individual in Canada, and not supplied to a registered non-resident for consumption, use, or supply in Canada. Substantiation must include evidence of export for goods or evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient for services.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) where applicable for goods
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill / Transportation Documentation
  • Commercial Invoice and original sales contract
  • Service Agreement (for software/cloud service supplies)
  • Proof of non-resident status of the recipient (foreign tax ID, business registration certificate)
  • Bank records / Inward Remittance Certificate evidencing receipt of export proceeds

Canada has no mandatory real-time electronic invoicing or digital fiscal clearance regime at the federal or provincial level. CRA accepts electronic invoices provided records are retained in accessible and legible format. The Pan-Canadian e-Invoice Standard (PEPPOL BIS 3.0) is voluntary and has been announced for adoption. No API pre-clearance or digital stamping is required. GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1 million may be required to file returns electronically.

CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess GST/HST plus statutory interest if export documentation is deficient or the export is not substantiated. Gross negligence penalties under Excise Tax Act s. 285.1 can reach 25% of the amount involved or CAD 5,000, whichever is greater, where false statements are made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims may also lead to criminal prosecution under the ETA and Income Tax Act, with fines and imprisonment. CRA audit reassessment periods generally span three years, extended where misrepresentation or negligence is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully floating exchange rate under the Currency Act with no exchange control regime. Both current-account items (royalties, license fees, SaaS subscription payments) and capital-account items (equity injections, intercompany loans, dividends, and share redemptions) are freely convertible into CAD and foreign currency without Bank of Canada approval or surrender obligation. The only frictions are regulatory rather than exchange-control based: (i) FINTRAC reporting of incoming and outgoing international electronic funds transfers exceeding CAD 10,000; (ii) Part XIII withholding tax on payments to non-resident shareholders (statutory 25%, typically treaty-reduced, e.g., to 0-15% under the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty, with special treatment for a Canadian ULC whose distributions may be characterized as interest for U.S. treaty purposes); and (iii) ISED notification obligations where an acquisition of control of a Canadian business exceeds the applicable monetary threshold. Profit remittance is unrestricted provided that the entity is in good standing under the B.C. Business Corporations Act, the dividend is paid out of retained earnings or contributed surplus, and the applicable NR4 withholding certificate is filed. Prudent treasury practice is to maintain a Canadian-dollar operational buffer of at least three months of disbursements to avoid forced conversion during volatile windows. A BC ULC is similarly unrestricted from holding multi-currency operating accounts; no shadow capital controls, prior-approval percentages, or repatriation quotas exist at either the federal or provincial level.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender rule or mandatory conversion ratio in Canada. Export and cross-border service receipts (including ERP/CRM cloud license fees and implementation services classified under ISIC 6201 / NAICS 511210) may be retained in the invoiced foreign currency indefinitely and may be offset against foreign-currency payables in a multi-currency treasury structure. FINTRAC's cross-border electronic funds transfer (EFT) reporting threshold of CAD 10,000 applies to both inbound and outbound wire transfers, requiring the reporting financial institution to capture originator and beneficiary details; there are no timing requirements for converting receipts. Where the ULC invoices US customers in USD and holds USD at a Canadian Schedule I bank, internal netting against USD vendor payments (e.g., cloud infrastructure providers) reduces bridge FX conversion costs. Any repatriation exceeding CAD 10,000 by wire remains subject to standard EFT record-keeping; if physical cash or bearer monetary instruments cross the border, a Cross-Border Currency or Monetary Instruments Report (G8/E667) is required. No surrender certificate, exporter registration, or central-bank approval is required for software exports.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential bank supervision and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF and cross-border funds reporting; foreign investment review is administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) under the Investment Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM - LEGALLY RESTRICTED. Canada has no passporting regime for EU/UK Electronic Money Institutions; a foreign EMI can only operate as a Money Services Business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Leading providers (Wise, Payoneer, Stripe Treasury) offer cross-border collection and sub-account rails but are not Schedule I banks and cannot independently provide CAD clearing (Lynx/ACSS) or hold deposit insurance (CDIC); funds are typically safeguarded at Canadian bank partners, adding an unsecured counterparty layer. For the BC ULC, the recommended architecture is to use a fintech EMI exclusively for high-velocity B2B card-based collection through Stripe Billing, while the settlement account and any amounts exceeding CAD 25,000 reside with a Tier 1 Canadian bank. Key legal constraints: (i) the EMI's MSB registration requires a Canadian compliance officer and a registered Canadian place of business, which a BC ULC can provide as subsidiary infrastructure; (ii) no EMI can lawfully provide credit, letter-of-credit services, or FX forwards in Canada without a bank or dealer license; and (iii) the EMI account must not be used to route intercompany debt payments to non-resident affiliates in a manner that obscures Part XIII withholding reporting. Contractual subordination of the EMI to the Schedule I bank via automated 'settlement-account top-up' instructions is mandatory to avoid treasury concentration in an unregulated node.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol for the BC ULC receiving a US-parent capital injection or intercompany loan: (1) execute a board resolution and treasury mandate authorizing the ULC's signatories; (2) open the CAD/USD operating account at a Tier 1 bank and request a proof-of-account letter 24 hours before the wire; (3) instruct the remitter to send a SWIFT MT103 (high-value wires should use GPI with the Payment Tracking and STP flag) with the beneficiary line containing the exact ULC legal name, the account number, the receiving bank's SWIFT BIC, and the branch code; (4) populate the Ordering Customer and Beneficiary Customer fields with legal names exactly matching registry records; avoid nested 'For Further Credit To' structures unless a designated sub-account ID is assigned; (5) state the remittance information field (Field 70) as 'EQUITY INJECTION - CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION - SOFTWARE SECTOR - BC ULC' or 'INTERCOMPANY LOAN - LOAN AGREEMENT DATED YYYY-MM-DD - PRINCIPAL WITHOUT INTEREST'; (6) prohibit crypto, gaming, or personal-purpose references in the wire narrative; (7) instruct the receiving bank to file the FINTRAC EFT report for amounts equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 and retain the SWIFT MT103 confirmation with the underlying intercompany agreement; (8) record the equity injection using the Bank of Canada daily noon exchange rate. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM - LEGALLY RESTRICTED. Canada has no passporting regime for EU/UK Electronic Money Institutions; a foreign EMI can only operate as a Money Services Business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Leading providers (Wise, Payoneer, Stripe Treasury) offer cross-border collection and sub-account rails but are not Schedule I banks and cannot independently provide CAD clearing (Lynx/ACSS) or hold deposit insurance (CDIC); funds are typically safeguarded at Canadian bank partners, adding an unsecured counterparty layer. For the BC ULC, the recommended architecture is to use a fintech EMI exclusively for high-velocity B2B card-based collection through Stripe Billing, while the settlement account and any amounts exceeding CAD 25,000 reside with a Tier 1 Canadian bank. Key legal constraints: (i) the EMI's MSB registration requires a Canadian compliance officer and a registered Canadian place of business, which a BC ULC can provide as subsidiary infrastructure; (ii) no EMI can lawfully provide credit, letter-of-credit services, or FX forwards in Canada without a bank or dealer license; and (iii) the EMI account must not be used to route intercompany debt payments to non-resident affiliates in a manner that obscures Part XIII withholding reporting. Contractual subordination of the EMI to the Schedule I bank via automated 'settlement-account top-up' instructions is mandatory to avoid treasury concentration in an unregulated node.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a single statutory local-file/master-file threshold under the Income Tax Act; however, a taxpayer must file Form T106 for any reportable non-arm's-length transaction with a non-resident where the total annual value of all reportable transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000, and CRA contemporaneous documentation is expected for all intercompany transfers. For Country-by-Country Reporting, a multinational group must file when consolidated group revenue is at least CAD 1,000,000,000. CRA also administratively expects more robust Master File / Local File packages for larger taxpayers or those with cross-border vertical cloud licensing and intercompany cost-sharing.

Economic substance scope: No UAE-style economic substance regulations apply in Canada; however, CRA and the courts will disregard transactions lacking commercial substance under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245), the sham doctrine, and treaty anti-abuse/beneficial-ownership principles. A BC ULC must maintain real Canadian economic substance - employees who negotiate contracts and manage risk, substantive board decisions, cloud infrastructure used in a Canadian permanent establishment, and IP development activity in Canada. Where the ULC is treated as a disregarded entity for U.S. purposes under check-the-box, Canadian tax law continues to treat it as a fully taxable Canadian-resident corporation and requires a T2 filing, withholding, and arm's-length transfer pricing documentation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment with gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of understated tax under s. 163(2); criminal prosecution under s. 239(1) with fines between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and uncapped compounded arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally assessed under s. 227.1 for unremitted source withholdings (payroll and withholding tax) and under provincial WCB/GST legislation. In an unrestricted ULC, because the corporation has unlimited liability, CRA can pursue the sole shareholder directly for corporate tax debts; criminal convictions can trigger Canadian border arrest warrants and travel bans, civil restitution orders, and forfeiture of personal assets.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR denies the treaty benefit and recharacterizes the distribution as a taxable dividend subject to 25% Part XIII withholding, interest, and penalties; surplus-stripping under s. 212.1 can reclassify capital gains into dividends;

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced because ULC shareholders have unlimited liability; directors/shareholders are jointly liable for the tax amount determined, plus penalties and interest; criminal money-laundering charges may be laid if the structure is used to disguise beneficial ownership; conviction can impose up to 5 years imprisonment and travel bans.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under s. 247(3) equal to 10% of the amount of transfer-pricing adjustment (or CAD 1,000 minimum, whichever greater); T106 late-filing penalty CAD 25 per day up to CAD 2,500 per filing; adjustments to debt-equity thin-capitalization rules can disallow interest deductions.

Director personal liability: If the ULC cannot pay the assessed tax and penalties, its unlimited liability makes the parent shareholder directly liable for the full Canadian tax exposure; directors who knowingly approve non-arm's-length royalty pricing can be held liable in civil negligence proceedings and can be prosecuted for tax evasion if the pricing is fraudulent; travel restrictions may apply after criminal charges are laid.
AML / Fraudulent Payment Routing

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for entities and individuals; Criminal Code money-laundering offense subject to up to 5 years imprisonment; CRA may reassess unreported revenue and impose 50% gross-negligence penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners who participate in a fraud ring face criminal fines, imprisonment, civil asset forfeiture, travel bans pending trial, and personal liability for all tax, GST, and withholding amounts owed by the ULC, piercing the unlimited liability structure.
