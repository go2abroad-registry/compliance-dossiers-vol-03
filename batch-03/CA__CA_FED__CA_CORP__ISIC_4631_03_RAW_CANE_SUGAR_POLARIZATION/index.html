Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA-ARC). Filer and registration portals include CRA Business Registration Online, My Business Account, and GST/HST NETFILE/Electronic Filing services. Customs administration relevant to exports is handled by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

        
E-government filing portal
For a federal/provincial Canadian business corporation, first obtain corporate existence under the Canada Business Corporations Act or applicable provincial corporations act, or obtain extra-provincial registration if already incorporated elsewhere. The CRA then issues the federal Business Number (BN), which is Canada’s primary tax identification number. A foreign non-resident corporation applies for a BN and the required CRA program accounts by filing Form RC1, Request for a Business Number, by mail or using Business Registration Online. The GST/HST account is identified by the BN followed by the 'RT' program account; payroll uses 'RC'; customs import/export activity uses 'RM'. Supporting documentation for non-residents includes certificate of incorporation or certificate of existence, registered office information, names and addresses of directors and officers, and proof of the Canadian permanent establishment address or authorized Canadian representative. Upon issuance of the BN and program accounts, the entity is recognized for federal corporate tax, GST/HST, payroll, and CBSA electronic export reporting purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation

        
Available election forms
Canada has no US-style 'check-the-box' election. A corporation incorporated under federal or provincial law is irrevocably an entity-level taxpayer under the Income Tax Act; no pass-through election is available for federal income tax purposes. Relevant statutory elections in respect of transaction tax and tax characterization include the closely related corporation election under subsection 156(2) of the Excise Tax Act, which permits registered corporations that are closely related to elect that intercorporate supplies be treated as outside the scope of GST/HST; the special non-resident elections under sections 216 and 217 of the Income Tax Act concerning certain Canadian-source rental or employment income; and elections to determine a corporation’s status as a Canadian-controlled private corporation where factually available. Hybrid mismatch treatment applies mechanically under Canadian domestic rules and does not provide an elective entity classification.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
GST/HST small-supplier registration threshold is CAD 30,000 of total worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated exports, measured over the current calendar quarter plus the four preceding calendar quarters. A corporation that is not a small supplier and that makes taxable supplies in Canada in the course of commercial activity must register for GST/HST. Exporters over the threshold are registrants in practice and report zero-rated exports while claiming input tax credits on input costs.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
A supply of VHP raw cane centrifugal sugar qualifies for zero-rating under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act when the goods are exported from Canada. The exporter must demonstrate that the sugar was sold under a bona fide commercial export contract, delivered into the physical custody of an ocean common carrier or loaded on board a vessel, removed from Canada as soon as reasonably practicable, and not used, consumed, or diverted into domestic Canadian commerce. The export sale may be zero-rated where goods are delivered to a common carrier for direct export or where the goods are otherwise exported by the purchaser in accordance with the statutory conditions. Customs and commercial documentation must consistently describe the sugar by HS classification 1701.14, quantity, polarization value, vessel, loading port, and non-Canadian discharge port. Although Canadian tax law does not prescribe a fixed statutory FX repatriation period, this compliance architecture adopts a 180-day settlement benchmark; remittance beyond that period without commercial justification may undermine the evidentiary integrity of the export sale and trigger further CRA enquiry.

Canada does not impose a federal real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping mandate for GST/HST invoices. Registrants must issue invoices containing the required prescribed particulars under the Excise Tax Act, including supplier identity, recipient identity where required, date, description of goods, consideration, and tax rate or amount. Electronic invoices, EDI files, and PDFs are acceptable if complete, legible, and retained according to CRA record-keeping rules. Canada has voluntarily adopted the PEPPOL-based Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework, but participation is elective. Electronic filing of GST/HST returns is mandatory for registrants with annual gross revenue exceeding CAD 1 million and for selected registrants. Export declarations to the CBSA are transmitted electronically through CERS, which provides the proof-of-export clearance record but is not a fiscal stamping mechanism.

      
CRA audit scrutiny of export zero-rating focuses on the complete documentary chain from Canadian warehouse or load port to foreign discharge and on matching the export declaration, bill of lading, commercial invoice, and foreign currency settlement. Failure to meet the 180-day settlement benchmark, absence of original shipping documents, or evidence of domestic diversion of raw cane sugar will cause the CRA to deny zero-rating, retroactively assess GST/HST on the full selling price at the applicable federal or harmonized rate, recapture input tax credits previously claimed on raw sugar inventory, and charge compound daily interest. False statements or omissions in GST/HST returns attract federal penalties under the Excise Tax Act, including penalties of not less than CAD 100 and additional percentage-based penalties on the tax sought to be avoided; gross negligence or wilful evasion may support criminal prosecution with substantial fines and imprisonment. Under the Income Tax Act, deliberate misstatement or gross negligence related to export revenue may invoke the 50% gross negligence penalty and criminal consequences. The normal reassessment window may be extended beyond the standard period where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, and there is no limitation period in cases of fraud or misrepresentation.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. No exchange-control license is required for capital injection, profit remittance, trade payments, or dividend distributions. Non-resident remittances of dividends or interest are subject to Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) Part XIII withholding tax at 25% unless the applicable income tax treaty reduces the rate. Cross-border wire transfers at or above CAD 10,000 trigger FINTRAC reporting, and banks require source-of-funds evidence for large inbound credits. Profit repatriation is feasible provided the entity has filed its corporate tax returns and documentary support for the distribution exists.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no timeline within which export receipts must be repatriated or exchanged into Canadian dollars. A Canadian exporter may retain VHP raw cane sugar proceeds in USD or other currencies in either resident or non-resident accounts. Compliance obligations are limited to FINTRAC reporting for cross-border monetary transactions of CAD 10,000 or more, tax recognition under the Income Tax Act on an accrual basis, and bank due diligence supported by the export contract, customs entry, and independent surveyor certificates.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and Payments Canada. Canada does not maintain exchange-control approvals; the Canadian dollar is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII obligations.

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails
            Fit: High
          

          
Medium-High as a complementary collection and vendor-settlement corridor only; it should not replace a Tier-1 Canadian bank account. Canada does not recognize UK/EU electronic money institution (EMI) status, and an offshore digital EMI may not take deposits in Canada unless properly structured. A fintech operating for Canadian clients generally must register as a money-services business with FINTRAC and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Direct use ceases to be viable for letter-of-credit issuance and high-volume sugar trade settlement, where a Schedule I Canadian bank account is mandatory.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        A compliant injection uses SWIFT MT103 or MT202 from the foreign parent or investor account to the Canadian beneficiary account, referencing the corporate registry number, legal name, and the stated purpose of 'shareholder capital contribution' or 'shareholder loan'. Canada imposes no exchange-control permit, but the receiving bank must validate source of funds and FINTRAC must receive a report if the transaction equals or exceeds CAD 10,000. The beneficiary should hold injected funds in a separately identified Capital Escrow Account for at least 90 days before repatriation, and the corporate records should timestamp the remittance to satisfy anti-avoidance rules.For raw cane sugar settlements the primary clearing rail is SWIFT: open-account payments use MT103 with the tariff and shipping reference; documentary credits are issued as MT700/MT710 and cover payments as MT202. Canadian-dollar same-day interbank settlement is executed through Payments Canada's High Value Payment System (formerly LVTS); USD settlement is normally via the Federal Reserve wire or correspondent banks. Stage 06
Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

      
Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

    

    

      
Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)
15% federal general corporate income tax rate after the 10% provincial abatement and 13% general rate reduction; combined federal-provincial headline rate approximates 26.5% depending on province of allocation

      
Dividend withholding tax (WHT)
25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) on all dividends paid or credited to a non-resident shareholder.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation is mandatory to protect the reasonable-efforts defence where non-arm's length cross-border transactions exceed C$1,000,000 in a fiscal year; Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting applies to Canadian constituent entities of multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least C$750,000,000 annually; penalty protection requires documentation to be prepared before the tax filing date.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada does not enact the Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule under s. 245 ITA and the common-law substance-over-form doctrine to disregard corporations lacking business purpose. A federally incorporated CBCA corporation must maintain a registered office, corporate minute books, board meeting records, and a minimum of 25% resident Canadian directors. For the VHP sugar arbitrage entity, the CRA will scrutinize decision-making location, commercial risk assumption, vessel charter execution, storage arrangements, and the presence of local employees to prevent treaty-shopping and sham pricing.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion and False Statements
        
Statutory sanction: Civil gross-negligence penalty under s. 163(2) of 50% of the misstated tax; criminal conviction under s. 239 ITA carries a fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; the CRA will reassess all open years and compound interest.

        
Director personal liability: The Crown will pierce the corporate veil: directors are personally liable under s. 227.1 for unremitted withheld taxes and assessed balances; criminal charges may trigger travel bans as bail conditions, civil restitution orders freeze personal assets, and imprisonment up to 5 years applies.

      

      
      

        Transfer-Pricing Misstatement and Shell Pricing
        
Statutory sanction: Penalty under s. 247(3) equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment, subject to the greater of C$5,000 and 10% of gross revenue cap at C$5,000,000 per year; no protection is available without timely prepared Local File documentation; penalty is coupled with reassessment of all years within the statutory limitation period.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who signed the T2 return or approved the mispriced commodity contracts are assessable for corporate withholding and penalty liabilities; findings of gross negligence expose directors to civil restitution, Federal Court certificate enforcement, travel restrictions, and imprisonment.

      

      
      

        Non-Resident Repatriation Tax Evasion
        
Statutory sanction: Under s. 215 ITA the Canadian payer is liable for the full unreported Part XIII amount plus interest; s. 227(8) adds a 10% penalty on unwithheld amounts; s. 214(3)(a) recharacterizes disguised loans as dividends; CRA may assess the payer as agent for the non-resident and extend the limitation period.

        
Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for any amount the corporation failed to withhold and remit; CRA files a Federal Court certificate and seizes personal assets, imposes travel bans in criminal proceedings, and may obtain imprisonment for intentional withholding evasion.

      

      
      

        Shell Company or Lack of Economic Substance under GAAR
        
Statutory sanction: GAAR under s. 245 ITA nullifies the abusive transaction and the entity is reassessed as if it did not exist: full tax is re-determined and a 50% penalty may apply; treaty benefits are denied under limitation-of-benefits clauses; the CBCA may order dissolution for failure to maintain director residency and registered-office requirements.

        
Director personal liability: The court will pierce the corporate veil on sham grounds; nominal directors face civil restitution of the entire tax benefit, travel bans secured through fraud-related criminal charges, and imprisonment up to 5 years under s. 380 of the Criminal Code, which allows a maximum 14-year sentence for fraud over C$5,000.

      

      
      

        Anti-Money Laundering and Banking KYC False Statements
        
Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA s. 86 false-statement penalties: on indictment, a fine of up to C$5,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; Criminal Code s. 462.31 money laundering attracts up to 10 years' imprisonment; FINTRAC may cap administrative monetary penalties at C$1,000,000 for reporting entities; the CRA may seize cargo and documentation.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers who sign KYC forms or direct wire restructuring are exposed as parties to the criminal offence; bail conditions commonly include travel bans and passport surrender; personal and corporate assets are subject to Criminal Code restraint and forfeiture orders, and personal imprisonment up to 10 years may be imposed on a money-laundering conviction.
