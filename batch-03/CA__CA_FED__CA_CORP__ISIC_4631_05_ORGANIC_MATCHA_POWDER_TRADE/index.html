Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — official national tax authority; online infrastructure: Canada.ca Business Registration Online, My Business Account (MABA), GST/HST NETFILE, and the CRA Non-Resident Taxpayer Services desk; customs administration: Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the CARM portal and Canadian Export Reporting System (CESR).

E-government filing portal

The statutory tax identifier is the nine-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RC for federal corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, and RM for import/export. A foreign-owned Canadian Business Corporation may apply online via CRA Business Registration Online; non-resident entities without a Canadian address or Canadian directors submit Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts' to CRA Non-Resident Taxpayer Services; processing typically takes 2-10 business days. The GST/HST account number (BN plus RT suffix) is the VAT identifier and must be secured before making taxable supplies in Canada; supporting documents include certified incorporation articles, foreign registration certificate, proof of a registered agent, and beneficial ownership schedule.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — The statutory tax identifier is the nine-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RC for federal corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, and RM for import/export. A foreign-owned Canadian Business Corporation may apply online via CRA Business Registration Online; non-resident entities without a Canadian address or Canadian directors submit Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts' to CRA Non-Resident Taxpayer Services; processing typically takes 2-10 business days. The GST/HST account number (BN plus RT suffix) is the VAT identifier and must be secured before making taxable supplies in Canada; supporting documents include certified incorporation articles, foreign registration certificate, proof of a registered agent, and beneficial ownership schedule.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; a Business Corporation is a per se resident taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act. Statutory mechanisms relevant to the cross-border matcha trade: (i) hybrid mismatch de-ruling provisions in ITA ss. 18.2 to 18.4 and s. 95 — mandatory, not elective, and treating the entity as a separate taxable person regardless of any foreign transparent classification; (ii) GST/HST closely-related corporation election under ETA s. 156 for nil-consideration intra-group supplies; (iii) GST/HST filing-frequency elections including ETA s. 92(2) non-calendar fiscal year election; (iv) associated-corporation declarations under ITA s. 256 for grouping purposes; (v) advance income tax rulings available from CRA where foreign disregarded entity classification is uncertain.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small supplier threshold measured on annual worldwide taxable supplies for a person carrying on business in Canada; CAD 30,000 of Canada-source taxable supplies for a non-resident not carrying on business in Canada. A matcha distribution corporation must register for GST/HST once taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or over the preceding four quarters; registration is also mandatory where the entity makes taxable supplies or imports taxable goods and is not a small supplier. Voluntary registration allows full input tax credits on imported matcha.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of matcha powder from Canada to a non-resident buyer are zero-rated under ETA s. 10 and Schedule VI Part V if (a) shipped by the vendor by common carrier to a destination outside Canada, (b) delivered to the non-resident purchaser or agent for export, or (c) exported under customs supervision; the goods must leave Canada as soon as practical after the sale, normally within 60 days, and the vendor must retain CRA-style proof of export (GST-11 evidence). Domestic sale to a non-resident who later exports is not zero-rated unless contemporaneous export documentation exists. Inbound matcha powder imported into Canada is not zero-rated: the importer pays 5% GST at CBSA border release, plus applicable HST where the goods are destined to a participating province, and claims a full input tax credit only if registered and acquiring the goods for commercial supply. Offshore services ancillary to the trade, such as freight brokering or quality control performed for a non-resident, are zero-rated if supplied for use wholly outside Canada.

  • CBSA Export Declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CESR) for shipments valued above CAD 2,000, supported by commercial invoice and packing list
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / courier manifest and signed freight contract evidencing physical departure of the matcha cargo from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Destination-country customs entry or import certificate evidencing arrival and clearance of the exported matcha powder
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT MT103 credit advice plus bank statement showing receipt of foreign currency proceeds from the offshore buyer within regular commercial terms

No real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or mandatory digital invoice mandate exists under federal GST/HST law. Registrants must e-file via GST/HST NETFILE when annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5 million, and monthly filing becomes mandatory at that threshold; sub-1.5 million registrants file annually or quarterly. Invoices may be paper, PDF, EDI, or PEPPOL BIS 3.0 under the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL e-invoicing framework, which is voluntary. Import and export reporting is electronic through CBSA CARM and CESR; no digital fiscal stamp is used.

CRA denies zero-rating and re-assesses net tax, with interest from the original due date, if proof of export is incomplete, goods are diverted to Canadian use, or export is not completed as soon as practical. Monetary penalties under ETA s. 280 impose 5% of the net understated tax plus 10% for repeat misstatements; ETA s. 285 adds a 25% gross negligence penalty for false statements made knowingly or under gross negligence. Export and rebate fraud triggers CRA's Export and Rebate Fraud audit program; the normal reassessment period is three years, extended to four years for negligence and unlimited for fraud or misrepresentation. Criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and Income Tax Act can yield fines up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years. Although Canada has no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation clock, CRA expects export proceeds to be received within normal commercial terms consistent with the 180-day benchmark; unexplained receivables outstanding beyond 180 days are treated as unproven export revenue and clawed back with interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible under a floating-rate regime. Canada maintains no exchange-control law limiting current-account payments or capital-account transactions. Repatriation of dividends, interest, royalties and sale proceeds is unrestricted, subject to Income Tax Act Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at a statutory 25% unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty or exempted under specific provisions. There is no central-bank pre-approval requirement for conversion, but every significant cross-border flow must be supported by proper trade or investment documentation. The Investment Canada Act national-security review is separate and does not affect currency convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canadian law does not impose an export-proceeds surrender obligation, a mandatory conversion ratio, or a statutory repatriation timeline for export receipts. Exporters may retain CAD or any freely convertible foreign currency. FINTRAC reporting duties apply under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act to international electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more in a 24-hour period and to cross-border transportation of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more. For corporate tax purposes, foreign-exchange gains and losses on export receipts are included in business income and must be supported by complete trade documentation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); Canada has no centralized foreign-exchange-control organ, with OSFI supervising federally regulated financial institutions and FINTRAC administering AML/CFT and cross-border transaction reporting.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-Low; an offshore EMI is not a compliant primary rail for a Canadian capital escrow or operational account. Canadian payment-service providers are not chartered banks under the Bank Act and cannot obtain Payments Canada settlement membership. An offshore EMI that is not a FINTRAC-registered or equivalent regulated MSB cannot lawfully provide regulated payment services in Canada. It may be used only as a supplemental foreign-currency corridor if the Canadian entity uses a Canadian regulated PSP, the EMI is licensed in its home jurisdiction, and all flows are documented and FINTRAC-reportable when required. The use of an offshore EMI will usually trigger enhanced due diligence and may be declined by the receiving Schedule I bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate rate (Part I, Income Tax Act); effective combined federal-provincial headline rate ranges from 23% to 26.5% depending on province of permanent establishment.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% of gross dividends paid to non-residents of Canada (Part XIII tax, s.212 ITA), unless reduced by an income tax treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 return due within 6 months after the fiscal year-end. Tax balances are due 2 months after year-end for non-CCPCs (ineligible for SBD) and 3 months after year-end for CCPCs eligible for the small business deduction; quarterly/monthly instalments are required when tax payable exceeds CAD 3,000.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) filing thresholds: CAD 1,000,000 for services and most transaction types; CAD 2,000,000 for purchases/sales of tangible inventory property; CAD 500,000 for intangibles/royalties; CAD 1,000,000 for guarantees. Contemporaneous documentation must be prepared before filing where any non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exist. Transfer-pricing penalty (s.247(4)): 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of the taxpayer’s gross revenue and contemporaneous documentation supporting the arm's-length price was not available; penalty is not imposed where the taxpayer has made reasonable efforts (s.247(3)).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime. Substance is nevertheless enforced federally: (1) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245) re-characterizes transactions lacking a bona fide business purpose and misusing treaty benefits; (2) corporate residence for taxation follows central management and control (s.250); (3) the anti-hybrid mismatch rules (s.18(6) etc.) and ITA s.237.1 disallow certain structured arrangements; (4) the MLI's Principal Purpose Test restricts treaty entitlement for letter-box/conduit entities. A federal/provincial registered corporation engaged in active cross-border matcha procurement must have Canadian offices, bank accounts, books and personnel, and its board must genuinely exercise management in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Misrepresentation (Income Tax Act)

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under s.239(1): on indictment, a fine of 100% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; on summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 100% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 2 years. Civil penalty for gross negligence (s.163(2)): the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors/officers who participate in or direct the evasion are personally exposed to criminal charges; courts may pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a mere facade, enabling civil restitution, judicial re-assessments against the individuals, travel restrictions/bans imposed as bail or probation conditions, and detention pending extradition or sentencing.
Failure to Maintain Transfer Pricing Documentation and File Form T106

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing penalty (s.247(4)): 10% of the net adjustment amount over the threshold (lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue). Failure to file T106: CAD 100/day information-return penalty; adjustments to income under s.247(2) with interest and penalties, and possible double taxation absent competent-authority relief.

Director personal liability: Directors can be assessed personally for unremitted foreign withholding and penalties under s.227.1 where the corporation is bankrupt or fails to remit; willful connivance in setting non-arm's-length prices exposes directors to criminal prosecution under s.239 and civil liability for inducing breach of statutory duty.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax and Part XIII Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent remains liable for the gross amount unreported; s.227(9) imposes interest and penalties (late/missing remittance penalty up to 20% of the amount); the amount is collectible by the CRA as a debt due (s.227(10.1)).

Director personal liability: Directors of the remitting corporation are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for unremitted Part XIII tax, interest, and penalties to the extent they did not exercise reasonable diligence; the CRA may certify and collect against personal assets without first executing the corporation.
GAAR and Conduit/Shell Company Abuse (General Anti-Avoidance Rule)

Statutory sanction: GAAR re-determination under s.245 denies the tax benefit and re-characterizes the conduit transaction; CRA may impose 50% gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2) if the arrangement was knowingly misreported; treaty relief is denied under the Principal Purpose Test with full Part XIII liability reinstated.

Director personal liability: Courts will disregard the corporate personality where a 'sham' or 'shallow' corporation is used to mask the true principal; directors can be named in civil restitution for avoided taxes, banned from holding directorships in Canada, and subjected to international travel bans while extradition requests for tax fraud are processed (MLAT channels).
Anti-Money Laundering and Unauthorized Payment Routing (FINTRAC)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties under the PCMLTFA of up to CAD 500,000 per violation (first occurrence) and CAD 1,000,000 (repeat occurrence); non-compliance with wire-transfer reporting for transfers of CAD 10,000+ exposes the corporation to penalties and prohibited-operation orders on the Canadian bank account.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers who deliberately implement or tolerate a non-compliant AML framework for matcha trade proceeds are criminally liable under the PCMLTFA, exposing them to fines, asset forfeiture, prohibition from operating financial services, and imprisonment; Canadian immigration courts may impose irregular-exiter ban orders affecting personal travel.
