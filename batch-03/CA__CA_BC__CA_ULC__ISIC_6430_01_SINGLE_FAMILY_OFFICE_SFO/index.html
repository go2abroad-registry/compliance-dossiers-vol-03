Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – federal GST/HST, corporate income tax, and Business Number registration, administered through My Business Account and Business Registration Online at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services.html; British Columbia Ministry of Finance – BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administration and registration at https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes/sales-taxes/pst

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a BC Unlimited Liability Corporation (ULC) must first incorporate under the BC Business Corporations Act, then apply to the CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN). Registration is completed using Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' and Form RC2 'Business Account Resolution', either by postal/electronic submission or online through CRA Business Registration Online via an authorized representative or director. The ULC must open a separate GST/HST account if it makes taxable supplies in Canada in the course of commercial activity and exceeds the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, and an import/export program account if it engages in cross-border goods movements. For BC PST, the ULC must register with the BC Ministry of Finance for a PST collector number if it sells taxable goods or services in BC or meets PST registration obligations; non-resident ULCs may register using prescribed BC forms and an agent for service.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a check-the-box election for domestic tax classification. A BC ULC is universally taxed as a corporation under the federal Income Tax Act and BC Income Tax Act. However, for foreign (particularly US) tax purposes, the ULC may file IRS Form 8832 to classify itself as a disregarded entity or as an association taxable as a corporation. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules in sections 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act may deny deductions for payments that are treated as deductible in Canada but not included in income in the payee's residence jurisdiction unless a joint election is available. A ULC may also elect, where applicable, to be treated as a corporation for Canadian tax purposes while being disregarded for foreign tax purposes; no Canadian statutory election changes its Canadian entity-level taxation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide gross taxable supplies made in Canada for GST/HST purposes, excluding supplies of financial services (which are exempt), supplies of goodwill, and supplies of a business; the small-supplier threshold is determined before GST/HST and on a sliding 4-quarter basis

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST/HST zero-rating applies to tax-exempt exports of tangible personal property supplied by a GST/HST registrant where the goods are exported as part of the commercial activity and the supplier has documentary evidence that the goods were physically delivered outside Canada. Zero-rating also applies to services supplied to a non-resident, if the service is performed wholly outside Canada, or where the service relates to real property situated outside Canada, or to goods delivered outside Canada, provided the recipient is a non-resident and the supply is not made for a Canadian resident or registered person carrying on business in Canada. Financial and investment management services supplied by a single-family office are generally exempt from GST/HST rather than zero-rated; the family office must nevertheless analyse the precise service supply to determine whether exemption or zero-rating applies.

  • Validated Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A) or Canadian Automated Export Declaration/Canada Border Services Agency export entry for goods requiring declaration
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, rail, or truck) or equivalent transport document evidencing shipment from Canada
  • Commercial invoice and underlying supply contract showing the purchaser, description, quantity, price, and destination
  • Proof of actual export such as carrier's inward manifest, customs release, or foreign customs import certificate
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire confirmation proving receipt of payment in foreign currency from the non-resident purchaser

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime. GST/HST registrants may issue electronic invoices provided the invoice contains all mandatory information and the electronic record is accessible and acceptable to the CRA. GST/HST registrants must file electronically, generally through GST/HST NETFILE or business-to-government EDI/API integration embedded in certified accounting software; large businesses have mandatory electronic filing requirements. BC PST does not require electronic invoicing clearance or stamping, but requires taxpayers to preserve sales records in an auditable electronic or paper format and to report PST through BC eTaxBC.

CRA disallowance and clawback of claimed zero-rated treatment may be assessed at any time if the substantive export conditions are not met, with a 4-year normal reassessment period extended to indefinite where misrepresentation, omission, or fraud is established. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include a 50% gross negligence penalty on the understated net tax and a separate civil penalty of up to CAD 20,000 for knowingly providing false documentation; criminal prosecution for wilfully evading GST/HST may result in imprisonment, additionally to reassessment, interest, and collection costs. Documentary deficiencies are treated as prima facie failure to substantiate export delivery and result in immediate reassessment at 5% GST plus applicable BC PST and interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current- and capital-account convertibility under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. The Canadian dollar floats freely; no prior approval, licensing, or repatriation permit is required for dividends, interest, capital distributions, or intercompany payments to non-resident shareholders of the BC ULC. Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory; reduced to 5-15% under the Canada-US Treaty for a US-resident owner of a ULC) applies to outbound dividends, interest, and royalties on treaty-eligible filings. FINTRAC reports are required for every incoming and outgoing international EFT of CAD 10,000 or more. CRA returns T1135 (foreign property above CAD 100,000), T1134, and T106 are triggered by the foreign investment and cross-border payment activity of the family office.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory repatriation timeline. Export and import settlement data are reported to Statistics Canada for balance-of-payments compilation, but proceeds may be held indefinitely in any currency or foreign bank. Bank of Canada FX intervention is exceptional and does not alter convertibility.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the monetary authority and operator of the Large Value Transfer System (Lynx). Prudential supervision of Schedule I banks rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). FINTRAC administers AML/CFT reporting. Global Affairs Canada administers economic sanctions. Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange control agency and no currency approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Low for primary treasury banking; Medium only as a secondary payment conduit. Canada has no electronic-money institution (EMI) licence comparable to the EU PSD2 regime; non-bank payment service providers must register as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and, for retail payment activities, with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Offshore digital banks and EMIs (e.g., Wise Business, Airwallex, Revolut Business, Mercury) cannot offer securities custody, letters of credit, or private-banking credit facilities, and their accounts are unacceptable to most Canadian custodian banks for SFO flows. Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Layered beneficial ownership through foreign trusts, private foundations, or intermediate holding companies that conceals the identity of the ultimate family principal.

Root cause: Layered beneficial ownership through foreign trusts, private foundations, or intermediate holding companies that conceals the identity of the ultimate family principal.

Defense evidence: Complete ownership chart down to every natural person holding greater than 10%; certified trust deed or foundation charter; corporate-registry extracts for each tier; signed FINTRAC-compliant UBO declaration; sworn affidavit of the family principal identifying all sources of funding.
Insufficient or undocumented source of wealth and source of funds for the initial capital injection into the ULC.

Root cause: Insufficient or undocumented source of wealth and source of funds for the initial capital injection into the ULC.

Defense evidence: Three years of personal and corporate tax returns; audited financial statements; custodian and brokerage statements; sale and purchase agreements for the underlying family assets; inheritance or gift documentation; independent accounting firm's source-of-wealth attestation.
Unlimited liability feature of the BC ULC creating uncertainty as to creditor recourse, shareholder guarantees, and the bank's credit-risk model.

Root cause: Unlimited liability feature of the BC ULC creating uncertainty as to creditor recourse, shareholder guarantees, and the bank's credit-risk model.

Defense evidence: Legal opinion under the British Columbia Business Corporations Act (BCBCA) on s.51 liability; shareholder agreement defining indemnities and contribution rights; statement of net worth of the shareholder; evidence that the ULC maintains adequate liquid assets to meet any unlimited liability call.
Pass-through tax election complexity and perceived de facto partnership or conduit treatment of the ULC for Canadian and US tax purposes.

Root cause: Pass-through tax election complexity and perceived de facto partnership or conduit treatment of the ULC for Canadian and US tax purposes.

Defense evidence: CRA account confirmation; Big Four tax opinion confirming the ULC's Canadian corporate status and any US check-the-box objective; Notice of Articles; executed shareholder agreement; memorandum describing distributions, deemed-dividend risk, and Part XIII withholding application.
Politically exposed person (PEP) status, adverse media, or unexplained prior litigation affecting the family principal or entity.

Root cause: Politically exposed person (PEP) status, adverse media, or unexplained prior litigation affecting the family principal or entity.

Defense evidence: Independent PEP and sanctions screening (World-Check / Reuters Refinitiv); negative-media explanation letter; reputable counsel reference; certified court dockets showing resolution or discharge; Global Affairs Canada sanctions attestation.
Cross-border intercompany loan or funding ambiguity that could implicate aggressive tax avoidance or layering of funds.

Root cause: Cross-border intercompany loan or funding ambiguity that could implicate aggressive tax avoidance or layering of funds.

Defense evidence: Executed intercompany loan agreements with arm's-length interest rates; intercompany transfer-pricing benchmark study; CRA T106 filings; source-of-funds documentation for each loan tranche; bank reference confirming prior lending history.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate for a British Columbia corporation, comprising 15% federal tax and 12% British Columbia tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access an 11% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable limit. Investment income of a CCPC is generally subject to substantially higher refundable tax rates and is not ordinarily eligible for the small-business rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying company that owns at least 10% of the voting stock, and 15% in other ordinary cases. Other treaties commonly provide rates between 5% and 15%, subject to treaty-specific ownership and limitation provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation meeting the statutory conditions. Instalment obligations arise during the taxation year.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Most annual information slips and summaries are due by the last day of February following the calendar year. Part XIII withholding is generally remitted by the 15th day of the month following the month of payment or credit.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents generally triggers T106 reporting and is the principal Canadian threshold relevant to detailed contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. Canadian law does not impose an automatic OECD-style Master File filing obligation solely because a taxpayer exceeds CAD 1,000,000. A Master File may be required or requested in an audit, and Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate entity of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Kingdom or certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax outcomes are nevertheless constrained by the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership requirements, treaty anti-abuse provisions, sham and substance doctrines, residence and central-management-and-control principles, and specific anti-hybrid and interest-limitation rules. A single-family office ULC should maintain Canadian directors, documented decision-making, segregated books and records, commercial investment policies, genuine banking and custody arrangements, and evidence supporting any management-fee, financing, royalty, or investment-management functions. A BC ULC is generally a taxable corporation in Canada and is not automatically a Canadian tax pass-through entity; any foreign check-the-box or hybrid classification requires separate cross-border analysis.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false corporate tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal tax evasion can result in a fine ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to five years, or both. Civil reassessments include tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, and applicable information-return penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized, assented to, or participated in the conduct may face criminal prosecution and personal fines or imprisonment. Directors can also be assessed personally for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability rules, subject to due-diligence defenses. Corporate status does not shield deliberate criminal conduct.
Failure to withhold or remit Part XIII tax

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be liable for the unwithheld or unremitted amount, prescribed interest, and statutory penalties. Repeated or intentional failures may support prosecution and gross-negligence or tax-evasion consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted amounts where statutory conditions are met. Criminal liability may arise for intentional participation, false records, or obstruction.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty where statutory conditions are met, and apply interest. Treaty benefits may be denied under beneficial-ownership, limitation-on-benefits, principal-purpose, or Canadian anti-avoidance rules.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for a corporate transfer-pricing adjustment, but personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in false records, tax evasion, fraud, or source-deduction failures.
Undeclared foreign affiliates, foreign property, or offshore accounts

Statutory sanction: Information-return penalties may accrue daily and can be increased for gross negligence or repeated failure. Foreign-affiliate income may be reassessed, and the CRA may apply extended reassessment periods, penalties, interest, and criminal tax-evasion provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors and controlling persons may face prosecution for deliberate concealment, false statements, or obstruction. Personal director-liability rules apply separately to source deductions and GST/HST.
Anti-money laundering, beneficial ownership, and suspicious transaction failures

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties can be material and may reach CAD 1,000,000 for serious violations, depending on the violation and reporting entity. Criminal money-laundering or terrorist-financing offences can carry substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment for up to ten years or longer for aggravated offences under applicable provisions.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors, officers, and individuals may be prosecuted personally. Courts may order forfeiture or restraint of property. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may follow bail, probation, passport, immigration, or extradition processes.
Corporate records, beneficial ownership, and shell-company abuse

Statutory sanction: Consequences include denial of deductions, shareholder-benefit or appropriation assessments, interest, penalties, corporate-law remedies, audit reassessments, and potential application of the general anti-avoidance rule. A sham or fraudulent structure may also support criminal charges.

Director personal liability: Canadian limited-liability principles do not protect directors from their own wrongdoing, fraudulent conduct, statutory director assessments, or personal guarantees. The ULC's unlimited liability may expose shareholders to corporate creditors under applicable corporate law; it does not create a general shareholder shield.
Improper investment-management or securities activity

Statutory sanction: The British Columbia Securities Commission and other regulators may impose compliance orders, cease-trade or activity bans, administrative penalties, disgorgement, and prosecution under securities legislation. Registration exemptions are fact-specific and generally do not cover commercial services to unrelated families or clients.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal regulatory orders, director-and-officer bans, restitution or disgorgement claims, and criminal prosecution for knowing violations. The corporate form does not immunize individuals from securities-law liability.
