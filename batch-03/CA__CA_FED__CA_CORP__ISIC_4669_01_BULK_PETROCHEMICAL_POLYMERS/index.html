Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); online portal: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account, and Represent a Client. Customs export declaration and import/export account administration: Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal.

        
E-government filing portal
The statutory functional tax identifier is the 9-digit Business Number (BN), assigned by the CRA to a corporation carrying on business in Canada. For a foreign entity establishing a Canadian federal subsidiary: (1) incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or an applicable provincial act, or register the foreign corporation extra-provincially; (2) apply for the BN through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number); (3) open the corporate income tax program account (RC0001), the GST/HST program account (RT0001) when registration is required, and the CBSA import/export (RM) account through the CARM portal for cross-border trade; (4) provide the certificate of incorporation, corporate bylaws, foreign parent constitutional documents, and registered office details; (5) use the BN plus two-letter program account suffix (e.g., BN/RT0001) for all returns, remittances, refunds, and customs filings.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation

        
Available election forms
Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box or general entity classification election; a Canadian business corporation is a mandatory opaque, entity-level taxpayer under the Income Tax Act. Relevant elections available to a petrochemical wholesale group include: (i) the ETA s. 156 election under which closely related corporations may elect to treat intercompany taxable supplies as made for nil consideration, eliminating internal GST/HST charges; (ii) the ETA s. 273 joint venture election, allowing appointment of a designated operator to administer GST/HST obligations of a qualifying joint venture; (iii) the ITA s. 216(1) election permitting a non-resident earning Canadian rental income to compute Part I tax on a net basis; and (iv) transitional elections under the hybrid mismatch framework, noting that ITA ss. 18.2/18.21, effective in stages from 1 January 2023 and 1 January 2024, operate automatically without any general elective carve-out.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
5%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
C$30,000: a person becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant where taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, made in Canada exceed C$30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the four consecutive calendar quarters preceding the calculation; the same threshold applies to non-resident corporations making taxable supplies in Canada unless the small-supplier exception is available.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, section 1, supplies of exported tangible personal property, such as virgin polyethylene and polypropylene granules, are zero-rated where the supplier ships or delivers the goods to a consignee outside Canada, or delivers the goods to a carrier for export, or the goods are exported by the recipient within a reasonable period as evidenced by a customs export declaration. For related offshore services, zero-rating is available where services are performed wholly outside Canada (Schedule VI, Part V) or are supplied to a non-resident and satisfy the conditions of Schedule VI, section 7, subject to exclusions for services related to Canadian real property and local goods. The supplier must retain contemporaneous evidence of exportation per CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17.5; zero-rating preserves input tax credits for GST/HST embedded in domestic purchases, and refunds are obtainable through the GST/HST return where input tax credits exceed tax otherwise payable.

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC) API, or digital stamping mandate at the federal level. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically by registrants with annual taxable revenues exceeding C$1.5 million using CRA-accredited GST/HST NETFILE/EFILE software or EDI solutions; CRA requires digital preservation of books and records and may require standardized electronic extract files in an audit; enhanced GST/HST reporting obligations apply to non-resident sellers and digital platform operators under CRA administrative guidance, but no per-invoice fiscal authorization or digital stamping is required.

      
The CRA may reassess and claw back zero-rated export treatment retrospectively within the normal four-year assessment window, and without any time limit where there is misrepresentation, wilful default, or fraud. If the export substantiation fails, the supply is re-characterized as taxable, exposing the exporter to 5% GST plus applicable provincial HST (13%–15%) on the full export value, prescribed interest, and repayment of refunds. ETA s. 280(1) imposes a penalty of 25% of the tax sought to be evaded for knowingly false statements or statements made under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; ETA ss. 327–328 provide criminal sanctions on summary conviction or indictment, including imprisonment for up to five years and fines of up to 200% of the evaded amount. CBSA may levy administrative monetary penalties of up to C$25,000 for false or misleading export declarations, and directors may be held jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST, penalties, and interest under the ETA.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
Canada maintains a fully convertible currency under a free-floating exchange-rate regime. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are unrestricted, with no foreign-exchange controls, no mandatory central-bank approval, and no separate capital-account licensing requirement. Profit and dividend remittances by a Canadian corporation to non-resident shareholders are freely permitted at market rates, though they remain subject to Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act unless reduced by an applicable bilateral tax treaty. No repatriation tax is imposed in the Canadian federal jurisdiction; however, cross-border transfers over CAD 10,000 trigger FINTRAC reporting obligations by the financial institution, and cross-border currency movements in excess of CAD 10,000 must be reported to Canada Border Services Agency when physically transported.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirements and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts under Canadian federal law. An exporting entity may retain foreign-currency balances in Canadian or offshore bank accounts indefinitely. Export receipts must be properly recorded for income-tax and customs-valuation purposes; transactions involving SWIFT transfers outside Canada require the Canadian bank to file an Electronic Funds Transfer Report with FINTRAC under specific circumstances. No exporter declaration or repatriation timeline is required, but the company should maintain an export ledger, underlying invoices, and bills of lading to evidence commercial bona fides during an AML audit.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); no centralized exchange-control authority exists in Canada. Foreign exchange policy is executed through market operations by the Bank of Canada, while the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers anti-money-laundering transaction reporting and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises prudential banking conduct.

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails
            Fit: High
          

          
Medium-Low feasibility. Canada does not license offshore Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) as banks under the Bank Act, and foreign digital-payment companies cannot access the domestic Large Value Transfer System or Payments Canada clearing rails without a Canadian partner bank. A non-resident EMI can only support the group by acting as an unregulated payment intermediary, which creates additional AML concern. If the EMI is registered domestically as a Money Service Business with FINTRAC, it may aggregate cross-border flows, but direct settlement, trade LC, and escrow services still require a Schedule I Canadian bank. Legal constraint: offshore EMIs are not recognized by OSFI or the Bank of Canada, and holding material corporate treasury balances with an uninsured foreign EMI would expose the Canadian entity to credit and regulatory risk.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        Inbound non-resident equity or shareholder-loan capital must be routed by SWIFT MT103 (single) or MT202 (cover) to the Canadian bank's foreign correspondent before crediting the Canadian operating account. The wire should reference the corporate entity name, the destination account, and the external purpose statement 'equity contribution from non-resident shareholder' or 'shareholder loan account'. Under Canadian law there is no exchange-control approval; nevertheless, the receiving bank will require the appropriate 'foreign asset/liability' statistical code and may request the board resolution and a source-of-funds form if the transfer exceeds CAD 100,000. For CAD receipt through LVTS, same-day settlement is achieved; for USD receipts, the bank's US correspondent network provides clear funds after verification. The company should maintain the original transfer, conversion ticket, and board approval in the treasury compliance file.Trade payments and settlement obligations are cleared via the Payments Canada LVTS for CAD obligations, and via SWIFT MT103 messages through Canadian dollar correspondents for USD/EUR obligations. Commercial payments to suppliers of virgin polyethylene/polypropylene are best structured under irrevocable documentary letters of credit: issuing bank transmits MT700, confirming bank negotiates against compliant documents, and settlement is effected by MT202/MT103. Usance LC terms align with 30-60 day trade receipt cycles. For open-account disbursements, each wire must include the purpose code, trade invoice number, commodity HS code description, and bank-assigned customer reference. Stage 06
Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

      
Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

    

    

      
Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)
15% federal general corporate rate (2024); combined federal-provincial statutory rates range from approximately 23% to 31% depending on the province or territory where permanent establishment or provincial allocation arises.

      
Dividend withholding tax (WHT)
25% on gross dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty. For example, the Canada-U.S. treaty reduces the rate to 15% generally, or 5% for a U.S. corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of the voting shares. Branch remittance tax under s.219 also applies at 25% on after-tax branch profits remitted by a non-resident corporation, reduced to 15% under the Canada-U.S. treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is required under s.247(4) where the aggregate fair market value of transactions or series of transactions between the Canadian corporation and a non-arm's-length non-resident exceeds CAD $1,000,000 in a taxation year, or where loans outstanding between the parties exceed CAD $1,000,000 at any time in the year. If the Canadian entity is part of an MNE group with consolidated global revenue of at least CAD $1 billion, a Country-by-Country report must be filed with the CRA, and the CRA will generally request master file and local file documentation in the course of an audit.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulation like certain offshore jurisdictions. However, economic substance is applied through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245), the common law doctrines of sham and agency, and the requirement that a corporation's 'management and control' be exercised in Canada to establish residence. A shell entity with no business presence, no employees, and no decision-making in Canada may lose treaty entitlement, may be disregarded as a sham, or may be deemed resident under s.250 where the central management and control is exercised in Canada. The CRA may also attack aggressive interposition of an Ontario holding company lacking substance to claim treaty benefits under the OECD Multilateral Instrument provisions and treaty limitation-of-benefits clauses.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion
        
Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s.239(1), upon conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment for up to 5 years (summary conviction) or up to 10 years (indictable offence). Civil gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) is an additional 50% of the understated tax. CRA may reassess beyond the normal three-year period in cases of misrepresentation.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly or negligently participate in tax evasion are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for withheld source deductions and any penalties for failure to remit; in fraud-based prosecutions, directors may face restitution orders, asset freezes, travel bans, and imprisonment under the Criminal Code. The corporate veil may be pierced if the corporation is a mere agent of the directors.

      

      
      

        Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245) to deny the tax benefit, recharacterize transactions, and reassess taxes, interest and penalties. The corporation may be treated as a sham at common law, with the income allocated to the real beneficial owner. Provincial corporate statutes permit dissolution or forced compliance for a passive shell. Penalties under s.162(1) for failure to file, plus the $24,000 transfer-pricing documentation penalty, may apply.

        
Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company may be personally liable for taxes of the company under alter ego principles if the company acts as a bare trustee or agent. Section 160 applies to transfers of property to shareholders for inadequate consideration, making the recipient personally liable for the corporation's outstanding tax debt. Based on case law, directors can also be sued for breach of fiduciary duty if they cause the corporation to enter into artificial transactions.

      

      
      

        AML / Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering)
        
Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties for corporations can reach CAD $1 million per violation; criminal money laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31 is punishable by imprisonment up to 10 years and substantial fines. Seizures of proceeds of crime and asset forfeiture are also authorized.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct, authorize, or tacitly acquiesce to money-laundering activity may be charged as parties to the offence, attracting personal fines and imprisonment. Directors can also be subject to FINTRAC compliance orders and revocation of business registrations. The corporate veil does not shield officers from criminal prosecution under s.462.31.

      

      
      

        Transfer Pricing Misstatement
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust prices or allocations under s.247(2). A 10% penalty applies under s.247(3) to the amount of the net adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD $5 million or 10% of gross revenue. Monthly documentation penalties of up to CAD $24,000 per failure apply under s.247(7). Interest accrues from the original filing date. Criminal prosecution is possible if the misstatement is made knowingly or with gross negligence.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who deliberately approve false transfer pricing documentation or who cause the corporation to misstate taxable income may be liable for penalties and interest as part of the unpaid tax debt if funds have been transferred to them. In severe cases, directors may be prosecuted for tax evasion and face imprisonment. The due diligence defence under s.227.1 may be unavailable where the director intended to understate income.

      

      
      

        Unreported Foreign Affiliate and Offshore Assets
        
Statutory sanction: Failure to file T1134 (foreign affiliate information return) attracts a penalty of $100 per day up to $2,400; failure to file T1135 (foreign income verification statement) attracts $25 per day up to $2,500, and if the failure extends beyond 24 months, an additional $500 per month up to $12,000. False statements attract penalties under s.162(7) and gross negligence penalties under s.163(2). The CRA can reassess beyond the normal three-year period where false statements or omission of the affiliate are found.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who authorize the concealment of a foreign affiliate may be held personally liable for penalties and interest as deemed directors' liability under s.227.1 if the corporation has withheld amounts but failed to remit. In an assessment of the corporation, the director may become liable for up to 100% of the unremitted tax. Criminal prosecution for tax evasion may result from a deliberately hidden foreign affiliate, leading to imprisonment and personal fines.
