Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export supplies are available to persons who sell or supply goods exported to a foreign recipient as part of a commercial export transaction. The supplier must hold evidence of export, such as a completed Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A), a transport document (e.g., airway bill, bill of lading), and proof that the goods have left Canada. Services performed for non-residents, including certain tangible personal property services and advisory services, may be zero-rated under s. 5(a) of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act if the services are supplied to a non-resident and not for use in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A / Canada Border Services Agency export report)
  • Bill of Lading (or airway bill / freight manifest showing export from Canada)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or bank statement evidencing payment received from the foreign buyer)
  • Sales invoice and contract of sale identifying foreign purchaser
  • Export broker or freight forwarder's confirmation where applicable

No national real-time e-invoicing mandate. GST/HST returns are filed electronically via the CRA GST/HST NETFILE (GST34) portal or through approved accounting software; invoices must contain prescribed information in plain text or an electronic format mutually agreed with the recipient. No digital stamping or fiscal clearance requirement under federal law.

CRA may deny or claw back ITCs and refunds where export documentation is insufficient or false. Penalties include: 5% of the overstated refund plus 1% per month (repeated behavior); 50% of misrepresentation penalties under s. 285 of the Excise Tax Act; criminal prosecution with up to $250,000 fine and/or imprisonment for fraudulent zero-rating claims, plus rescinded refunds plus interest accruing under s. 160.2 and s. 298 of the Excise Tax Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account. Canada accepted Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement on 25 March 1952; there are no exchange controls, no licensing for profit remittance, no advance import deposit, and no restriction on equity injection, dividend payment, intercompany financing, or trade settlement for a Canadian Business Corporation. The only frictions are tax- and reporting-driven. (a) Part XIII of the Income Tax Act imposes 25% withholding tax on dividends, interest, and royalties paid to non-residents, reduced to 5% (qualifying corporate shareholder) or 15% (other) under the Canada-US tax treaty and similar rates under other treaties; the payer must hold a valid treaty-residency certificate (CRA NR-series form) on file before applying the reduced rate, plus file NR4 returns and remit the withholding. (b) All cross-border wire transfers of C$10,000 or more are monitored by the receiving bank for the FINTRAC Large Cross-Border Currency Transaction Report; this is a bank filing, not a client permit. (c) The entity must classify each inbound/outbound flow for Statistics Canada's Balance of Payments (e.g., 'Direct Investment - Equity Capital'). Profit repatriation is therefore unobstructed, but the treasury plan should pre-fund the Part XIII gross-up and maintain an economic-substance file (employees, premises, board meetings in Canada) to defend against transfer-pricing and residency reassessment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose surrender, mandatory conversion, or repatriation deadlines for export receipts. A Canadian exporter may invoice in CAD, USD, EUR, or CNY and may retain foreign-currency balances in a foreign-currency bank account at a Schedule I bank without restriction. Compliance obligations are: (i) FINTRAC large-cross-border-transfer reporting for receipts of C$10,000 or more in a 24-hour period (effected by the bank); (ii) accurate balance-of-payments classification as 'Exports of Goods' on the corporate banking remittance instructions; (iii) for income-tax purposes, translation of foreign-currency receivables using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate at each reporting period under the functional-currency rules of section 261 of the Income Tax Act; and (iv) arm's-length pricing of intercompany export sales under section 247. Recommended treasury policy for this Montessori toy exporter: daily sweep of Amazon Pay and Stripe settlement balances into the CAD operational account, or maintenance of a USD concentration account with a pre-set quarterly conversion ladder, to reduce FX exposure and eliminate any perception of unrepatriated trade proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), Ottawa. Canada has no separate foreign-exchange-control agency. The regulatory perimeter comprises: (i) the Bank of Canada under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2), which is the monetary authority, steward of official reserves, and settlement institution for the Lynx real-time gross settlement (RTGS) system; (ii) OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions), prudential supervisor of Schedule I banks; (iii) FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), responsible for AML/ATF intelligence and large-transfer reporting; and (iv) Global Affairs Canada, which administers economic sanctions under the Special Economic Measures Act, the United Nations Act, and the Freeze Assets of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act. The Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) establishes the Canadian dollar as legal tender. No exchange-control permit or central-bank approval is required for any lawful currency transaction by a CBCA corporation.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility: High for ancillary collection and disbursement acceleration; Medium as a sole or primary treasury rail. Legal constraints: (i) Offshore digital EMI/e-money institutions are not banks under the Bank Act of Canada and cannot take deposits in the Canadian legal sense; a foreign EMI performing payment services in Canada must register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business (MSB) under the PCMLTFA and, if it performs electronic-payment functions, must also comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act, 2021 (payment-service-provider registration regime in force for 2024-2025). (ii) EMI balances are not covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC), and there is no bank-resolution protection. (iii) Wise Business, Payoneer, and Airwallex may be used for cross-border FX conversion and for small payables/receivables, but balances should be zero-balanced daily into the Schedule I operational account and never exceed C$100,000 per EMI nor remain overnight beyond five banking days. (iv) Amazon Pay and Stripe must settle as merchant-of-record facilitators into a domestic Schedule I account, because Amazon requires a local bank account for marketplace settlement and Stripe's Canadian payout rail is CAD-denominated ACH/EFT. (v) An offshore EMI is not permitted to hold the capital-escrow proceeds of the CBCA share issuance, because Canadian banking law requires a regulated bank to receive paid-in capital and to provide the source-of-funds audit trail.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000: aggregate value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions with a non-resident that triggers the mandatory T106 Information Return and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation retention. Local File/Master File requirements are aligned with OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines; the CRA's minimum documentation standards apply where transactions exceed the threshold or where a multinational group has consolidated revenue of at least CAD 800 million (Country-by-Country Report filing).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain Designated Activity-specific Economic Substance Regulations in the offshore-regime sense. The applicable doctrine is the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s.245 ITA, which denies the tax benefit of an avoidance transaction that lacks a bona fide business purpose and is contrary to the object, spirit, and scheme of the Act. Additionally, the FCA's corporate jurisprudence, the statutory anti-dividend-stripping rules (s.212(1), s.84), and the transfer pricing substance analysis under s.247 require real commercial substance, commensurate risk allocation, and economic significance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: On summary conviction under s.239(1) of the Income Tax Act: a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment for up to 2 years. On indictment: a fine of 100% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Criminal prosecution under the Criminal Code (s.380) may add additional penalties and restitution orders. Civil penalties include gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax and fully crystallised interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct or participate in the evasion are personally liable for the resulting tax liability, interest, and penalties under s.227.1 (for unremitted source deductions) and can be subject to criminal fraud or money-laundering charges. Corporate veil-piercing occurs where the corporation is a mere facade for fraudulent activities; directors may face travel bans, asset freezes, disgorgement orders, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement / Incomplete Documentation

Statutory sanction: A transfer pricing penalty under s.247(3) of 10% of the net adjustment for the misstatement, applied where the understatement exceeds the greater of CAD 5 million or 10% of gross revenue. The penalty is mitigated only where the taxpayer made reasonable efforts to use the arm's length principle. Separate penalties for failure to file T106: CAD 100 per day, minimum CAD 1,000, maximum CAD 25,000, plus a 5% late-filing penalty schedule.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or knowingly implement a deficient transfer pricing policy may be exposed to civil liability as the directing mind of the corporation; there is no direct statutory penalty on directors for TP misstatements, but the CRA may seek waiver of the corporate veil if the transaction is a sham, and in egregious cases personal civil restitution is pursued. Criminal exposure remains for fraudulent invoicing or abusive loss creation.
Shell Company / Abusive Avoidance Transaction

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under s.245 to deny the tax benefit, full interest on arrears, and a GAAR penalty of 25% of the avoided tax (as applied by the CRA in significant cases). Anti-avoidance assessments may also be sustained under s.17, s.78, s.212(1), s.18(4), or the new revised transactional tests. Civil collection includes garnishment and seizure of Canadian assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who orchestrate a shell scheme risk being found personally responsible under the principle of piercing the corporate veil if the shell was created with an element of fraud, misrepresentation, or maintenance of an illegal purpose. Depending on the criminal finding, directors may be barred from managing a corporation under s.49 of the Canada Business Corporations Act, subjected to travel bans or extradition under MLATs, and sentenced to imprisonment under fraud provisions.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Unexplained Wealth

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, a corporation may be fined up to CAD 2 million for a first administrative monetary penalty and CAD 5 million for a subsequent violation. Criminal liability includes a fine on the corporation and imprisonment of responsible officers for money laundering (Criminal Code s.462.31) with penalties of up to 10 years. For failure to report suspicious transactions, administrative fines apply per non-compliance.

Director personal liability: A director who exercises direct control and fails to implement a compliance program or knowingly allows laundering through the corporate entity is personally liable for criminal fines and imprisonment. Directors are also exposed to civil asset- forfeiture proceedings, which override the corporate veil, and to the denial of passport/travel privileges in related tax investigations.
