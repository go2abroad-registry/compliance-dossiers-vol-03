Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online, My Business Account, and Represent a Client. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers customs registration and export processes through the CARM Client Portal. British Columbia provincial tax registrations are administered by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance.

E-government filing portal

A British Columbia unlimited liability corporation must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number (BN), generally through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1. The corporation must provide its legal name, incorporation jurisdiction, registered office, mailing address, director and authorized representative information, effective date of operations, fiscal year-end, and applicable program accounts. The corporation should register for a corporate income tax account (T2), payroll account if it has employees, and a GST/HST account where registration is required or voluntarily elected. A non-resident corporation conducting business in Canada must obtain a BN and register for applicable CRA program accounts using the CRA non-resident registration process. GST/HST registration may require evidence of commercial activity, Canadian business operations, authorized representative authority, and, in certain non-resident cases, security or a Canadian fiscal representative. A separate CBSA import-export RM account is required for commercial customs activity and is created through the CARM Client Portal. British Columbia PST registration is separate and is required only where the corporation makes taxable PST supplies or purchases requiring self-assessment. Canada has no general VAT identification number separate from the CRA GST/HST account; the GST/HST registration number is generally the BN followed by the RT program identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A British Columbia ULC is a legal corporation and is generally subject to Canadian corporate income tax at the entity level. Canada has no general check-the-box election that converts a Canadian corporation into a fiscally transparent entity for Canadian income tax. A ULC may be eligible to make a United States federal tax classification election on IRS Form 8832, commonly to be treated as a disregarded entity or partnership for U.S. tax purposes, subject to U.S. eligibility rules, ownership structure, treaty limitations, and applicable anti-hybrid and hybrid-mismatch provisions. The U.S. election does not change the corporation's Canadian legal status or Canadian corporate tax treatment. Canadian foreign-affiliate, treaty, transfer-pricing, thin-capitalization, and hybrid-mismatch rules must be separately reviewed where the ULC is owned by non-residents.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters for the small-supplier test. The threshold generally applies to the person and associated persons and excludes certain exempt supplies. Registration is required from the effective date prescribed by the Excise Tax Act after the threshold is exceeded. Voluntary GST registration is permitted before the mandatory threshold is reached. Separate British Columbia PST registration has no equivalent general CAD 30,000 threshold and depends on whether the business sells or leases taxable goods, software, taxable services, or otherwise conducts taxable PST activity. Corporate ESG, carbon-accounting, and advisory services are generally not automatically subject to BC PST merely because they are supplied in British Columbia, but taxable software, goods, or other prescribed services can create PST obligations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or shipment outside Canada and retention of evidence demonstrating export. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the service falls within the exported-service rules and the recipient is not resident in Canada, subject to detailed exclusions. For corporate Scope 1-3 carbon-footprint auditing, ESG advisory, and carbon-accounting services, zero-rating commonly depends on the identity and residency of the contractual recipient, the location and nature of the work, and whether the service relates to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. A service may fail to qualify where it is performed in relation to Canadian real property, Canadian tangible personal property, or a Canadian-based business activity of the non-resident recipient. The supplier should obtain and retain non-resident status evidence, contracts, statements of work, customer master data, service-delivery records, correspondence, work-product location and use evidence, payment records, and a documented GST/HST technical analysis. Zero-rating does not eliminate the requirement to register where taxable supplies exceed the registration threshold and may permit recovery of eligible GST/HST incurred on inputs through input tax credits or a net tax refund.

Canada and British Columbia do not generally impose a universal real-time GST/HST or PST invoice-clearance mandate, mandatory government e-invoice platform, or digital tax stamp for ordinary business-to-business supplies. Invoices must contain sufficient information to support GST/HST collection, input tax credits, zero-rating, and audit substantiation, including supplier identity and GST/HST registration number where applicable, recipient information, supply description, consideration, tax rate, tax amount, and invoice date. Electronic records are acceptable if they remain reliable, accessible, and retained for the statutory record-retention period. GST/HST registrants must file returns and remit tax through CRA systems, generally electronically where required by applicable filing rules. Customs import-export declarations and related data are submitted through the CBSA CARM Client Portal. Future federal digital-reporting or e-invoicing initiatives should be monitored, but no general real-time clearance requirement applies to this business profile as of the assessment date.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange control regime requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within 180 days, and no general statutory inward-remittance certificate is required for GST zero-rating. CRA may reassess incorrectly zero-rated supplies as taxable, assess unremitted GST/HST, deny input tax credits or GST/HST refunds, and charge arrears interest. Penalties may apply for false statements, omissions, gross negligence, repeated failure to file or remit, failure to keep adequate records, and participation in fraudulent arrangements. Deliberate tax evasion can result in prosecution and criminal fines or imprisonment. CBSA may impose customs administrative monetary penalties for inaccurate export declarations, deficient records, or non-compliance with reporting requirements. Where a refund or input tax credit was obtained on the basis of unsupported export treatment, the amount may be clawed back retroactively with applicable interest and penalties. The corporation should maintain contemporaneous transaction-level evidence and apply a documented review process before treating advisory services as zero-rated.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current and capital accounts under Canada's OECD Capital Liberalisation commitments; there is no approval, quota, declaration, or minimum-stay requirement for cross-border capital movements, dividends, or loan repayments. Profit remittance constraints are purely tax-driven: Income Tax Act (Canada) Part XIII imposes 25% statutory withholding on dividends and interest paid to non-residents, treaty-reduced (e.g., Canada-US Treaty), and distributions by a BC/AB ULC to non-resident shareholders made after 9 August 2022 are recharacterised as interest for Part XIII purposes, subject to a limited safe harbour based on the ULC's Canadian-source earnings.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline applies in Canada for export or advisory-fee proceeds. A BC ULC may retain 100% of receipts in any foreign currency held in a Schedule I bank foreign-currency account, with conversion to CAD at the company's commercial discretion. Documentary requirements are limited to retention of wire instructions, underlying service contracts, and FINTRAC cross-border records for every transaction above CAD 10,000, consistent with the PCMLTFA six-year record-keeping duty.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), the sole issuer of Canadian bank notes and custodian-manager of the Exchange Fund Account on behalf of the Minister of Finance; foreign-exchange supervision is completed by the Department of Finance Canada, OSFI (prudential bank regulation) and FINTRAC (cross-border monetary and AML reporting). Canada maintains no general exchange-control statute; the Foreign Exchange Control Act was abolished in 1951 and the CAD is a free-floating currency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium-High as a supplementary collections and FX-conversion rail; Low as the primary treasury relationship. Canada has no EU-style EMI licence; an offshore digital EMI must register as a money services business with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (Bank of Canada registration) before serving a BC resident client, cannot accept deposits in Canada without a bank charter or BC trust licence, and provides no CDIC insurance or bank-grade credit and derivative capacity. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.0% combined statutory general rate (Federal 15.0% after general rate reduction; British Columbia provincial 12.0%). For Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPC) eligible for the small business deduction, the combined effective rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income is 11.0% (Federal 9.0%; BC 2.0%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25.0% Part XIII withholding tax under Income Tax Act s. 212(2) on all dividends paid or credited by the BC ULC to non-residents of Canada.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 must be filed where the aggregate fair market value of reportable non-arm's length transactions with each non-resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000 for tangible goods or CAD 250,000 for services, intangibles, interest, rents, and royalties. The CRA's administrative TP documentation standard (Local File and Master File aligned to OECD Chapter V) is required as evidence of 'reasonable efforts' to establish arm's length pricing; multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of CAD 900,000,000 or more must also file a Country-by-Country Report (Form RC4649) and notification (Form RC4651).

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to offshore ESR regimes, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245) denies tax benefits from transactions found to be avoidance transactions lacking a bona fide business purpose, non-arm's length economic substance, or real risk-shifting. The common law sham doctrine and CRA anti-shell initiatives similarly recharacterize artificial arrangements. A BC ULC (unlimited liability entity, frequently elected as a U.S. disregarded entity under check-the-box) must demonstrate in jurisdiction: physically present professional staff, an active BC office, board minutes evidencing real management in BC, and genuine economic rationale for profit attribution; otherwise, CRA may allocate income to the non-resident shareholder, deem a permanent establishment, or recharacterize intercompany charges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 238(1): summary conviction fine of 50% up to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; s. 327(1): indictment for evasion with imprisonment up to 5 years and unlimited fines; Excise Tax Act parallel penalties for GST/HST; CRA criminal investigation and publication of the taxpayer's name in the federal tax evasion registry; gross negligence civil penalty of 50% of understated tax under s. 163(2).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who sign or participate in false returns may be convicted as parties to evasion under Criminal Code s. 21; directors are statutorily liable under ITA s. 227.1 for unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and Part XIII taxes withheld from non-resident payments; CRA can pursue civil restitution, freeze corporate and personal bank accounts, impose travel bans through criminal courts, and obtain jail terms of up to 5 years against individual directors.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Unreported Cross-Border Recharges

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 247(3): penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the greater of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue; the CRA may reassess within extended limitation periods upon failure to file T106; unpaid Part XIII tax on deemed payments to non-residents with interest at the prescribed rate (currently 9%); gross negligence penalty of 50% under s. 163(2) for knowingly deficient documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who execute TP documentation attesting to arm's length conclusions face personal exposure under s. 163.2 for false statements that they knew to be false or made in circumstances bordering on wilful neglect; aiding and abetting evasion (Criminal Code s. 21) exposes officers to criminal fines and imprisonment up to 5 years; director liability under ITA s. 227.1 attaches to unpaid Part XIII withholding on cross-border royalty/interest payments.
Shell Company and Artificial Tax Avoidance

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization under ITA s. 245(2) denies the tax benefit, reassessing dividends paid to non-residents at the full 25.0% Part XIII rate and disallowing intra-group deductions; sham doctrine voids transactions retroactively; CRA can levy gross negligence penalties (50%), interest, and reallocate income to the substance-less non-resident shareholder; BC Business Corporations Act records may be administratively struck or dissolved for chronic non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Once a sham is established, courts pierce the corporate veil and directors incur personal liability for the ULC's unpaid taxes, withholdings, and third-party debts given the entity's unlimited liability nature; directors can be assessed under ITA s. 227.1 for all unremitted foreign and domestic withholdings; Canadian border and criminal courts may issue travel bans and seizure orders preventing directors from leaving Canada pending restitution.
AML and Proceeds of Crime (Offset Brokerage Payment Rails)

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for entities and up to CAD 500,000 for senior officers; criminal conviction under s. 75 PCMLTFA or Criminal Code s. 462.31 carries up to 10 years imprisonment; FINTRAC public compliance notices and enforcement disclosures materially impair DTA relief applications and banking relationships.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers who knowingly fail to file STRs or implement an AML program are liable to fines, seizure of personal and corporate assets, and imprisonment of up to 10 years; travel bans pending criminal proceedings or sentencing are available to courts; personal directors' oaths and filings for the BC ULC expose individuals to civil restitution claims by fraud victims, and Courts have declined to shield directors where AML failures amount to willful blindness.
