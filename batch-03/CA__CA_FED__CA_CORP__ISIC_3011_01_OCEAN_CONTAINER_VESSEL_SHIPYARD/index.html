Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online service, My Business Account, and Represent a Client portal. Export and import customs declarations are administered through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) client portal. Provincial tax registrations may additionally be required with the relevant provincial revenue authority.

E-government filing portal

A corporation must obtain a Canada Revenue Agency Business Number (BN) and the applicable program accounts. The applicant submits incorporation documents, registered office and mailing address, directors and authorized representatives, business activities, fiscal year information, ownership and contact details through CRA Business Registration Online, by telephone, or through an authorized representative. A non-resident entity without a Canadian permanent establishment may apply for a BN and, where it carries on taxable activities in Canada or makes specified supplies, a GST/HST program account. The CRA may request certified constitutional documents, proof of identity, authority documentation, Canadian business addresses, contracts, customs records, and information concerning Canadian activities. A GST/HST account is generally required before the first taxable supply after the registration obligation arises. Separate payroll, import-export, excise, and provincial registration accounts may be required. A federal corporation must also maintain its corporate registration with Corporations Canada; corporate registration does not itself create a CRA tax account.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian corporation is generally a separate taxable person subject to federal income tax and applicable provincial or territorial income tax on taxable income. Canada has no United States-style check-the-box regime. A federally incorporated corporation does not automatically receive a federal-only income-tax treatment because provincial or territorial corporate income tax generally depends on the allocation of taxable income and the corporation's permanent establishment or business activities. A non-resident corporation is generally taxable in Canada on income from a Canadian business, income attributable to a Canadian permanent establishment, and specified Canadian-source income, subject to treaty relief. A Canadian-resident corporation is generally taxable on worldwide income, subject to applicable foreign tax relief.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a general check-the-box election to classify an eligible corporation as a partnership or pass-through entity. A corporation may make specific statutory elections where conditions are met, including elections relating to capital dividends, tax-deferred transfers of eligible property under section 85 of the Income Tax Act, amalgamations, wind-ups, foreign affiliates, functional currency, and GST/HST joint ventures or elections. A Canadian unlimited liability corporation may have different classification consequences under certain foreign tax systems, but it remains a corporation under Canadian domestic law and does not constitute a general Canadian pass-through election. Hybrid mismatch outcomes must be analyzed under Canadian anti-hybrid rules, treaty provisions, and the law of the counterparty jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies in the relevant small-supplier calculation, generally measured over the applicable single-calendar-quarter and preceding four consecutive calendar-quarter periods. The threshold does not generally apply to certain public service bodies, charities, taxi or commercial ride services, and non-residents making supplies through specified Canadian activities or arrangements. A person carrying on business in Canada may be required to register even where the small-supplier threshold is not exceeded, depending on the nature of its taxable Canadian supplies and applicable non-resident rules. Voluntary registration is generally available, subject to statutory conditions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Qualifying exported goods are generally zero-rated for GST/HST when the goods are supplied for export and the statutory conditions are satisfied, including exportation from Canada in the prescribed circumstances and retention of evidence that the goods left Canada. Commercial shipbuilding supplies require transaction-specific analysis because treatment may differ between domestic construction, delivery or transfer of a vessel, repair or modification services, components, tooling, subcontracted services, and supplies made to non-residents. Certain vessels and supplies used in commercial activities may qualify under specific zero-rating provisions, but non-resident status alone is insufficient. Services may be zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident and the statutory conditions are met, subject to exclusions for services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, litigation or dispute matters before Canadian courts or tribunals, and services performed in whole or in part in Canada where a specific exclusion applies. Documentary support should establish customer identity and non-residence, contractual scope, place of supply, vessel location and use, export or delivery facts, customs status, shipping records, and payment trail. Exporters may claim eligible input tax credits and request a net tax refund, but must substantiate the zero-rated output and all related input tax credits.

  • CBSA export declaration or applicable export reporting record submitted through the prescribed Canadian export-reporting system, where reporting is required
  • Commercial invoice and sales or shipbuilding contract identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods or services, value, currency, delivery terms, vessel or project reference, and destination
  • Bill of lading, sea waybill, cargo manifest, delivery receipt, or equivalent carrier documentation evidencing shipment or delivery outside Canada
  • Proof of export and destination evidence, including customs records from the importing jurisdiction where available
  • For shipyard construction, repair, or modification: vessel registration or identification records, work orders, completion and delivery certificates, custody-transfer records, shipyard location records, and evidence of the vessel's commercial use
  • GST/HST invoices and accounting records supporting the zero-rated supply and related input tax credits
  • Payment and banking records, including remittance evidence where commercially available. An inward remittance certificate is not a standard Canadian statutory document and is not generally required solely to substantiate GST/HST zero-rating
  • Non-resident customer certification and business records demonstrating the customer's status and the commercial purpose of the supply, where relevant

Canada has no general nationwide real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance or electronic tax-invoicing mandate for ordinary business-to-business transactions. Registrants must issue invoices or other prescribed documentation containing required information, retain reliable electronic records, and support input tax credit claims. Electronic invoices are generally acceptable if integrity, authenticity, accessibility, and record-retention requirements are met. No general digital tax stamp is required for GST/HST invoices. Exporters must comply with CBSA export reporting and customs requirements, including the CARM framework and applicable Canadian Export Reporting System procedures. Certain regulated goods, controlled goods, excise products, public-sector arrangements, or provincial regimes may impose additional electronic reporting or documentation requirements. GST/HST registrants generally file periodic returns electronically where required by CRA filing rules and remit tax by the statutory payment deadline.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange control regime requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within a prescribed number of days, and no general statutory 180-day repatriation deadline applies. CRA may reassess GST/HST where export conditions are not met, supporting records are inadequate, tax was incorrectly charged or omitted, or input tax credits and refunds were overstated. The reassessment may include the GST/HST otherwise payable, denial or clawback of input tax credits or net tax refunds, arrears interest, and applicable administrative penalties. Gross-negligence penalties may apply where a false statement or omission results from gross negligence; criminal prosecution may apply for tax evasion, fraud, or other offences. False customs declarations may result in CBSA reassessment, seizure, administrative monetary penalties, interest, and prosecution under applicable customs legislation. Records must generally be retained for six years from the end of the relevant year, subject to statutory extensions and specific record-retention rules. Voluntary disclosures may be available before audit or enforcement action if the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no exchange-control licenses, no prior approval for foreign-currency invoicing, and no currency-quota restrictions in the federal jurisdiction. Profit and dividend remittances by a CA_FED business corporation are permitted without Bank of Canada approval; the only substantive hurdles are Canadian federal income tax compliance (including Part XIII withholding on dividends paid to non-residents) and the need to evidence the remittance against retained earnings or contributed surplus. Foreign-currency bank accounts in USD, EUR, GBP and other major currencies are routinely maintained without marginal approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender of export proceeds, no statutory conversion ratio, and no time-limited repatriation window for export receipts. A Canadian commercial shipyard classified under ISIC 3011/NAICS 336611 may retain export receivables in the invoiced foreign currency in Canadian chartered bank accounts or in foreign accounts. The applicable statutory requirements are, instead, reporting-oriented: cross-border monetary reporting for movements above CAD 10,000 under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and accounting/tax deferral rules under the Income Tax Act for unrealized foreign-exchange gains. There is no regulatory compulsion to convert CAD.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting with OSFI for chartered bank prudential supervision and FINTRAC for AML/CTF monitoring; no centralized foreign-exchange approval authority exists because Canada operates an open capital account and does not maintain exchange controls on cross-border banking transactions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as an auxiliary collection/disbursement channel; Low as a primary treasury or escrow custodian. Canada does not recognize the 'Electronic Money Institution' (EMI) licensing category. Offshore digital EMIs (including Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, or similar) must either register as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC or operate under a Canadian chartered bank sponsorship to hold CAD-linked balances and route CAD settlement. FedPro and provincial securities laws do not confer deposit-taking status on unlicensed EMIs. For a Medium_Risk_Enhanced shipyard treasury, EMIs may be used only for small-value EPC-stage disbursements; capital calls, LC-backed milestone draws and escrow balances must remain in Schedule I Canadian bank accounts.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Non-resident controlling shareholders with no Canadian nexus and a UBO chain ending in a high-risk or FATF-listed jurisdiction.

Root cause: Non-resident controlling shareholders with no Canadian nexus and a UBO chain ending in a high-risk or FATF-listed jurisdiction.

Root cause: Sanctions/dual-use exposure in trade financing — high-value marine engines, compressors, navigation systems, or export of a completed deep-sea container vessel to non-OECD buyers.

Defense evidence: End-user certificates, commercial vessel classification letter confirming ISIC 3011/NAICS 336611 civilian dry-cargo production, comprehensive HS-code bill of materials, export sales contract with Incoterms, appropriate customs declarations, and a written compliance statement that neither the vessel nor its components are military/dual-use controlled items under Canadian and UN sanctions.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward equity or subordinated debt should be routed by SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for large clearing) to a CAD or USD treasury account at a Schedule I Canadian bank (preferably RBC, TD, or BMO). The payment narrative must classify the transaction under the Statistics Canada / Bank of Canada International Transactions Reporting System category for 'Canadian corporate capital / equity participation.' The complete compliance routing package must include: share subscription agreement, board resolution for capital issuance, updated Corporations Canada federal corporate profile, share certificates, beneficial-ownership declaration, and, where escrow release is required, an explicit instruction matching the escrow conditions. For CAD-denominated domestic transfers, clearing occurs via Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS); cross-border USD consolidations use the SWIFT network with U.S. correspondent bank Fedwire/CHIPS clearing. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as an auxiliary collection/disbursement channel; Low as a primary treasury or escrow custodian. Canada does not recognize the 'Electronic Money Institution' (EMI) licensing category. Offshore digital EMIs (including Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, or similar) must either register as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC or operate under a Canadian chartered bank sponsorship to hold CAD-linked balances and route CAD settlement. FedPro and provincial securities laws do not confer deposit-taking status on unlicensed EMIs. For a Medium_Risk_Enhanced shipyard treasury, EMIs may be used only for small-value EPC-stage disbursements; capital calls, LC-backed milestone draws and escrow balances must remain in Schedule I Canadian bank accounts.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% (federal general corporate income tax rate: 38% basic rate less 10% provincial tax abatement less 13% general rate reduction; combined federal-provincial rate may approximate 26.5% to 31% depending on the province of permanent establishment)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% (Part XIII, sections 212(2) and 215 ITA) on all dividends, including deemed dividends, paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder; this is a gross-basis withholding tax collected by the Canadian paying corporation.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 — aggregate annual value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with any single related non-resident that triggers mandatory Form T106 filing and the expectation of contemporaneous Transfer Pricing Local File / Master File documentation. Country-by-Country reporting applies to multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750,000,000 (approximately CAD 1.1 billion).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulations instrument; equivalent discipline is imposed through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 ITA), the transfer-pricing recharacterization rule (section 247(2) ITA), and common-law sham doctrine. An ocean container vessel shipyard must demonstrate genuine operational substance: physical dry docks and gantry cranes, production manpower, real procurement and invoicing flows, and board management in Canada. A nominal Canadian shell corporation used to route hull fabrication profits to a tax-haven parent while lacking operational substance is vulnerable to GAAR disallowance, sham recharacterization, and reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraud

Statutory sanction: Indictable offence under section 239(1) ITA: fine between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to five (5) years; summary conviction: fine of 50% to 100% plus imprisonment up to two (2) years. The evaded tax is reassessed with daily compounding interest and a section 163.2 gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understatement.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who directed, consented to, or acquiesced in the evasion are criminally prosecutable as principals; the CRA may pierce the corporate veil, assess directors personally for unremitted taxes under section 227.1 ITA, garnish personal assets, and obtain court-ordered travel bans and passport restrictions pending collection of CAD 25,000+ federal tax debts.
Gross Negligence and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Section 163.2 ITA penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax for the false statement, plus reassessment of tax, interest, and potential 100% penalty for third-party misrepresentations; a shipping contract auditor or tax preparer who counsels the misstatement faces separate third-party penalties.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil does not shield directors who signed or caused false returns; personal gross negligence penalties may be imposed directly on the signatory directors, and the CRA may seek civil restitution against directors for amounts the corporation cannot satisfy.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Subsection 247(3) ITA penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds prescribed thresholds and documentation obligations are unmet; the full adjustment is added to taxable income with daily compounded interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically criminally liable, but they may be held personally liable for the unpaid corporate tax debt if the shipyard is stripped of assets; deliberate mispricing with intent to deceive exposes directors to fraud-based civil claims and CRA collection through the corporate veil.
AML and Money Laundering (FINTRAC)

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code section 462.31: imprisonment up to fourteen (14) years for laundering the proceeds of crime; PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for a first violation and up to CAD 1,000,000 for a second; the vessel and related proceeds are subject to seizure, forfeiture, and criminal restraint orders.

Director personal liability: Directors, compliance officers, and beneficial owners who knowingly direct laundering schemes face individual criminal liability up to 14 years' imprisonment; FINTRAC assesses responsible individuals personally, and courts may impose travel bans, asset freezes, and personal financial penalties.
Sham and Shell Subsidiary / GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR disallowance of the entire tax benefit under section 245(2) ITA, plus a statutory penalty of 10% of the disallowed benefit under subsection 245(4) where the taxpayer fails to establish reasonable acceptance; general sham doctrine permits the CRA to treat the transaction as a nullity for tax purposes, reassessing the real economic owner.

Director personal liability: Canadian courts will pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a sham or an agency of the foreign parent; directors and shadow directors are personally liable for abetting the scheme, subject to civil restitution, director liability assessments under section 227.1, and possible prosecution for counselling tax evasion.
