Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - Business Registration Online (BRO) at www.canada.ca/business-registration and CRA My Business Account portal at www.canada.ca/my-cra-business-account; provincial counterparts: BC Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC for BC PST and provincial corporate income tax), BC Registries and Online Services (BC corporate registry), and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for export declarations.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the ULC under the Business Corporations Act (SBC 2002, c. 57) via BC Registries Corporate Online; obtain the Certificate of Incorporation and BC company number (no minimum authorized share capital; unlimited shareholder liability for corporate obligations). Step 2: Obtain the federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) with CRA program accounts by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or through BRO, attaching the BC incorporation certificate, articles, Form 1 (Notice of Registered Office) and Form 10 (Notice of Directors); a non-resident beneficial owner must appoint a Canadian resident agent for service and disclose a Canadian address. Step 3: Activate the GST/HST account (BN program identifier RC0001) via BRO; registration is compulsory once supplies (including zero-rated supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter; voluntary registration below the threshold is available to recover input tax credits (ITCs). Step 4: Register for the BC corporate income tax account (program identifier BC0001, attached to the same BN) and, if applicable, BC PST through the eTaxBC portal (Form REG 121); freight-forwarding and international charter services are generally PST-exempt. Step 5: Enrol the BN with CBSA as the customs account number and obtain an Export Reporting identification code in CERS. Step 6: File an annual T2 Corporation Income Tax Return within six months of fiscal year-end; a ULC resident in Canada is taxed on worldwide income under Part I of the Income Tax Act, while a non-resident ULC carrying on business in Canada is taxed on Canadian-source business income and may be subject to Part XIV branch tax or Part XIII withholding on distributions.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - A BC ULC is a body corporate under the Income Tax Act (subsection 248(1)) and, for Canadian income tax purposes, is taxed at the entity level under Part I as a corporation; no Canadian partnership or flow-through election exists. Territorial scope: a Canadian-resident ULC (incorporated after 1965 and resident in Canada) is taxable on worldwide income with foreign tax credit relief; a non-resident ULC is taxable only on Canadian-source employment, business, and taxable property gains. The ULC is not a tax-exempt zone entity.

Available election forms

(i) US check-the-box election under Treas. Reg. Section 301.7701-3 via Form 8832: a wholly owned Canadian ULC may be classified as a disregarded entity (single member) or a partnership (multiple members) for US federal tax purposes, creating a hybrid entity used in Canada-US treaty planning (Rev. Rul. 88-53); this does not alter Canadian entity-level taxation. (ii) Fiscal-period election under ITA subsection 249.1(4) to align the ULC taxation year with the US member's year, deferring/reducing taxable income inclusion timing mismatches. (iii) Canada-US Treaty Article X(6) election to reduce Canadian dividend withholding to 5% for a qualifying US corporate member holding at least 10% of voting shares, supported by a certificate of residence; Part XIV branch tax may likewise be reduced. (iv) Treaty-based limitation-of-benefits certification and ITA Part XIII/Part XIV elections for non-resident ULCs to exempt or reduce withholding on eligible Canadian-source payments where treaty conditions are met.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in aggregate worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies and supplies made by associated persons) in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter (small supplier threshold under ETA section 148); once exceeded, GST/HST registration is compulsory within 30 days and remains mandatory for all subsequent Canadian taxable supplies by the foreign-owned ULC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under ETA Schedule VI, Part V. (i) Exported goods (s. 1): a registrant's supply of tangible personal property is zero-rated where the registrant ships or delivers the property to a place outside Canada, or delivers it to a common carrier for export, within a reasonable time. (ii) Freight transportation services (s. 7): a supply of freight transportation service that is part of a continuous freight movement (CFM) beginning or ending outside Canada is zero-rated; air cargo charter brokerage fees and commissions directly allocable to an international CFM qualify. (iii) Services to non-residents (s. 23): a service supplied to a non-resident person who is not a GST/HST registrant is zero-rated unless the service relates to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed; a broker arranging offshore charter flights for foreign cargo thus qualifies. Domestic legs, warehousing, ground handling, and incidental services performed wholly in Canada and not part of the export CFM remain taxable at 5% GST and may attract BC PST in non-exempt circumstances. Input tax credits (ITCs) are claimable for GST/HST paid on Canadian inputs used in zero-rated export supplies; any ITC clawback follows a failed zero-rating substantiation.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) report or G13 export declaration containing the exporter BN, transaction date, cargo description, tariff classification, quantity, and value, plus any applicable export permit or licence for controlled goods
  • Air Waybill set: Master Air Waybill (MAWB) and House Air Waybill (HAWB) indicating foreign destination, consignee, origin airport, and flight details, together with the cargo charter agreement or booking confirmation evidencing the international continuous freight movement
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or SWIFT/Fedwire wire credit advice showing foreign-currency settlement from the overseas charterer or consignee, matched with a dated foreign-exchange conversion record evidencing the actual receipt of export proceeds

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime; no CRA API pre-authorization or third-party invoice clearance is required. Voluntary Pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing follows the Peppol AS4/UBL 2.1 framework. Mandatory electronic obligations: (i) GST/HST returns must be filed electronically by registrants via GST/HST NETFILE with remittances through CRA My Payment; (ii) electronic books and records must be retained in an accessible machine-readable format for CRA inspection; (iii) export declarations must be transmitted electronically to CBSA via CERS (XML/EDI or eManifest web portal) before departure or when required by CBSA regulations, with each report referencing the exporter BN, cargo description, value, tariff classification, and destination.

CRA may reassess a claimed zero-rated export as a taxable Canadian supply where the documentation bundle is incomplete, adding 5% GST on the consideration plus arrears interest under ETA section 280. False statements or omissions attract a penalty of the greater of CAD 400 and 25% of the net tax (ETA subsection 297(1)), increased to 50% where the registrant acted knowingly or with gross negligence (ETA subsection 297(2)); willful evasion is an offence punishable on summary conviction by a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment up to two years (ETA section 296). Directors and officers of the ULC incur joint and several liability for unremitted GST (ETA section 323). While no Canadian statute prescribes a strict 180-day remittance deadline, CRA audit practice treats foreign-currency settlement beyond 180 days without commercial justification as an indicator of a simulated export and will deny zero-rating and associated ITCs retrospectively. Income Tax Act subsections 15(2) and 90(6) may deem a shareholder benefit or dividend where amounts due by a shareholder remain outstanding at the year-end, and US anti-hybrid rules under IRC Section 894 and Treas. Reg. Section 1.894-1(d) may deny US treaty benefits on hybrid mismatched payments, exposing the US member to additional US tax and penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. A British Columbia unlimited liability corporation may generally receive foreign currency, convert funds, make cross-border shareholder-loan or dividend payments, acquire foreign assets, and remit profits without prior foreign-exchange authorization. Canada does not impose a general withholding restriction on outbound payments, but Canadian withholding tax may apply to dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, rents, and similar amounts, subject to domestic exemptions and applicable tax treaties. Dividends require legally available corporate funds and proper corporate authorization. Interest, guarantee fees, and related-party payments require commercially supportable terms, transfer-pricing compliance, beneficial-owner analysis, and applicable withholding assessment. Banks may delay or reject transactions where beneficial ownership, source of funds, sanctions screening, aviation counterparties, trade documentation, tax status, or payment purpose is unclear. Large-value or unusual transactions may generate regulatory reporting without constituting a legal prohibition.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to a Canadian bank, convert export receipts into Canadian dollars, or convert a prescribed percentage of export receipts. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be received through offshore correspondent banks, subject to contractual banking terms, accounting records, tax reporting, sanctions compliance, customs documentation, and anti-money-laundering requirements. There is no general statutory conversion deadline for ordinary air-freight brokerage or cargo-charter export receipts. Exporters and service providers must retain sufficient records to substantiate the underlying supply, invoice, customer, currency, settlement account, and tax treatment. Electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, including linked transfers meeting applicable aggregation rules, may trigger reporting by the financial institution or money-services business to FINTRAC; this is a reporting obligation, not an export-proceeds conversion rule.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada generally has no exchange controls, capital controls, mandatory foreign-exchange conversion requirements, or central-bank approval requirement for ordinary corporate cross-border payments. Financial institutions remain subject to applicable anti-money-laundering, sanctions, payment-transparency, prudential, and reporting requirements administered or supervised by authorities including the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, the Canada Revenue Agency, and the Canada Border Services Agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment collection, foreign-currency conversion, and limited treasury functions; low as a substitute for a principal Canadian operating bank. An offshore electronic-money institution does not automatically provide a Canadian deposit account, Canadian-dollar clearing access, CDIC deposit protection, or unrestricted access to Canadian domestic payment rails. The entity must verify the provider's licensing jurisdiction, permitted services, safeguarding model, correspondent-bank chain, sanctions controls, segregation of client funds, transaction limits, reserve requirements, data controls, and ability to onboard a non-resident-owned British Columbia corporation. A foreign EMI may be unable to support regulated payment activity involving third-party funds, customer money, escrow, remittance, or money-services functions unless properly licensed. A Canadian entity should not represent an EMI wallet as a Canadian bank account or rely on it for all settlement liquidity. Use a Canadian bank for payroll, taxes, statutory payments, core operating receipts, and contingency liquidity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity subscriptions and shareholder loans from the documented funding entity's bank account directly to the British Columbia corporation's Canadian bank account or approved foreign-currency account. Use a named originator and beneficiary, accurate legal name, registered address, account number, SWIFT/BIC, intermediary-bank details, and a payment narrative identifying the transaction as equity contribution or shareholder loan. Canada does not use a universal statutory foreign-exchange purpose-code system for ordinary corporate wires; the bank may nevertheless require an internal purpose classification and supporting documents. Provide the executed subscription agreement or loan agreement, board and shareholder approvals, capitalization schedule, source-of-funds evidence, beneficial-owner information, tax-residency information, and expected-use schedule before sending funds. Avoid split wires, unexplained third-party funding, cash deposits, and circular transfers. Reconcile the incoming wire to the general ledger and retain payment confirmations. For material foreign investment, assess Statistics Canada investment reporting, CRA tax reporting, transfer-pricing, thin-capitalization, withholding-tax, and related-party debt requirements. Commercial letters of credit may be used for large or higher-risk supplier commitments, subject to the issuing bank's credit approval, collateral requirements, UCP 600 terms, sanctions screening, and document-compliance controls. Match every payment to a signed contract, invoice, shipment or charter reference, airway bill or equivalent transport record, counterparty bank-account verification, and approved purchase order. Apply dual authorization, segregation of duties, callback verification for bank-account changes, sanctions screening, fraud monitoring, and daily reconciliation. Canadian domestic payments may use EFT, Interac e-Transfer for limited low-value purposes, Lynx for high-value Canadian-dollar payments, and established correspondent-bank rails for foreign currencies. Complex leveraged or speculative structures should be prohibited unless separately approved under a documented risk policy.Hedging should be linked to forecast or committed exposures, measured against approved hedge ratios, documented in treasury records, and reviewed for Canadian accounting, tax, disclosure, derivatives-reporting, and anti-speculation requirements. The corporation should obtain independent legal and tax advice before entering derivatives with offshore dealers or using customer funds as collateral.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for payment collection, foreign-currency conversion, and limited treasury functions; low as a substitute for a principal Canadian operating bank. An offshore electronic-money institution does not automatically provide a Canadian deposit account, Canadian-dollar clearing access, CDIC deposit protection, or unrestricted access to Canadian domestic payment rails. The entity must verify the provider's licensing jurisdiction, permitted services, safeguarding model, correspondent-bank chain, sanctions controls, segregation of client funds, transaction limits, reserve requirements, data controls, and ability to onboard a non-resident-owned British Columbia corporation. A foreign EMI may be unable to support regulated payment activity involving third-party funds, customer money, escrow, remittance, or money-services functions unless properly licensed. A Canadian entity should not represent an EMI wallet as a Canadian bank account or rely on it for all settlement liquidity. Use a Canadian bank for payroll, taxes, statutory payments, core operating receipts, and contingency liquidity.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally access an 11% combined rate on eligible active business income within the applicable federal and British Columbia small-business limits, subject to associated-corporation, taxable-capital, passive-income, and other restrictions. A non-resident-controlled ULC generally cannot qualify as a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax generally applies to dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident. A tax treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for qualifying corporate direct-investment dividends, subject to the particular treaty, ownership threshold, beneficial-ownership, limitation-on-benefits, and anti-avoidance requirements. Treaty benefits for payments by a Canadian ULC require specific hybrid-entity analysis; the Canada–U.S. treaty may deny benefits under Article IV(7) in relevant circumstances.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not generally impose OECD-style Master File and Local File thresholds. Under Income Tax Act section 247, a taxpayer must make or obtain contemporaneous documentation describing its related-party cross-border transactions and arm's-length pricing, generally no later than the documentation due date. The separate T106 reporting requirement generally applies where the aggregate reportable transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 during the taxation year; that amount is a T106 reporting threshold, not a general exemption from transfer-pricing documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general regime titled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian transfer-pricing, anti-avoidance, beneficial-ownership, treaty anti-abuse, foreign-affiliate, and interest-deduction rules may nevertheless challenge arrangements lacking commercial substance or credible operational support. Maintain evidence of decision-making, personnel, premises, functions, assets, risks, services, and the commercial rationale for the Canadian business. A ULC's foreign tax classification does not determine its Canadian tax classification.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer pricing and inadequate documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under Income Tax Act section 247. A transfer-pricing penalty may generally equal 10% of the net adjustment where the statutory threshold is met and reasonable efforts to determine and use arm's-length prices were not made. T106 failures can attract daily and other statutory penalties, including a general late-filing penalty of CAD 25 per day up to CAD 2,500 and more severe penalties for gross negligence or intentional non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically personally liable for corporate income tax or transfer-pricing adjustments. Personal exposure may arise under statutory director-liability provisions, including for unremitted source deductions or GST/HST, or for knowing participation in an offence. The corporate veil is not routinely pierced; imprisonment or travel restrictions require applicable statutory grounds and due process.
Failure to withhold and remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted withholding tax, interest, and penalties under the Income Tax Act, including penalties for failure to deduct or remit and for deficient information returns. Treaty relief does not excuse failure to establish entitlement or comply with remittance and reporting obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted amounts, including source deductions and certain statutory remittances, subject to statutory defences, limitation periods, and due-diligence rules. Personal criminal liability requires proof of the relevant offence and individual participation or other required elements.
Tax evasion, false statements, or fraudulent records

Statutory sanction: Civil tax reassessments, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and criminal prosecution may apply. Tax evasion offences under the Income Tax Act can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; additional consequences may apply under other statutes.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, participate in, or knowingly assist an offence may face personal prosecution and imprisonment. Liability depends on evidence and statutory requirements; corporate office alone does not establish criminal guilt. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty.
Unreported foreign property, affiliate, or related-party information

Statutory sanction: Information-return penalties may apply per day, per return, or by reference to gross negligence or intentional non-compliance, in addition to reassessment, interest, and other penalties. The applicable amount depends on the form, period, and conduct.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic for a corporation's information-return default. Directors or officers may face exposure where a specific statutory liability or offence applies and the required elements are established.
Corporate residence, permanent establishment, and treaty misclassification

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, reassess tax, impose withholding liability, interest, and applicable penalties, and apply anti-avoidance or transfer-pricing provisions where their requirements are met.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is limited to applicable statutory director-liability provisions or personal participation in an offence. Treaty denial alone does not establish director liability or justify veil piercing.
Aircraft charter licensing, aviation, and proceeds-of-crime compliance

Statutory sanction: Separate aviation, transportation, sanctions, and criminal laws may impose administrative measures, monetary penalties, licence suspension or cancellation, or prosecution. The applicable authorization and sanction depend on whether the entity acts solely as a broker or operates, markets, or controls air services.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal sanctions or criminal liability where the governing statute provides for it and their conduct satisfies the relevant legal test. Incorporation as a ULC does not immunize individuals from their own unlawful acts.
