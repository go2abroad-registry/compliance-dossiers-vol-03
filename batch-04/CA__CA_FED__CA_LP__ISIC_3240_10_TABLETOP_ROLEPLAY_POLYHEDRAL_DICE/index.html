Competent corporate registry

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - Business Registration Online / My Business Account (federal Business Number and program accounts); Ontario Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery - Companies and Personal Property Security Branch (CPSPB) (formation registry); Corporations Canada (ISED) only if a CBCA corporate general partner is incorporated.

Governing statute

Canada has no federal general limited partnership registry; the LP is constituted under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. L.8, ss. 2, 3 and 9 (formation, declaration contents and name). A corporate general partner, if used, is incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-44, s. 5. E-15, s. 240(1). Carrying on business in other provinces requires extra-provincial registration in each such province.

Formation / constitutional filings

Declaration of Limited Partnership (Form 3 under O. Reg. 260/05) filed with the Ontario CPSPB; federal Business Number request via CRA RC1A or the Business Registration Online portal; extra-provincial registrations in each other province of business activity.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); Business Registration Online portal and My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business-taxes/registrations.html).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a Canadian limited partnership must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through the CRA's Business Registration Online portal or by filing Form RC1/RC1A. Registration is effected by providing the LP's legal name, registered office or place of business in Canada, fiscal year-end, list of general and limited partners, and intended program accounts. No separate TIN is issued; the 9-digit BN plus a program account identifier (e.g., RT for GST/HST) constitutes the required tax numbers. The LP must register for GST/HST if it is engaged in a commercial activity and is not a small supplier. Non-resident partners may also require a non-resident partnership information return if the LP carries on business in Canada, has Canadian-source income, or disposes of Canadian taxable property.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. A Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent for federal income tax purposes: the LP itself is not a taxpayer, and each partner reports its allocable share of partnership income, gains, losses, and credits under section 96 of the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST purposes, however, the partnership is treated as a separate 'person' and can register as a registrant in its own right. The partnership has no minimum capital requirement and permits non-resident ownership, which is relevant to Part XIII withholding and section 216 return elections.

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a US-style 'check-the-box' election. The default pass-through classification of a limited partnership is imposed by statute and cannot be converted to entity-level taxation. Available elections include: (i) partner allocations made in the limited partnership agreement for tax purposes, subject to the reasonable expectation of profit and specified partnership rules; (ii) fiscal period elections under section 249.1 of the Income Tax Act; (iii) an election under subsection 102(3)/102.2 as applicable for specified partnership business income; (iv) for GST/HST, election to treat supplies between the partnership and its members as financial services in certain circumstances, and the 'restricted review period' election under the Excise Tax Act. For hybrid mismatch purposes, Canada applies the OECD hybrid mismatch rules to deny deductions where payments give rise to a deduction/no-inclusion outcome; no separate check-the-box election is available to override the partnership's transparent status.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: An entity is a small supplier if its total worldwide taxable supplies in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (or in the current calendar quarter) are CAD 30,000 or less. Once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration is required within 29 days of the threshold being exceeded. The threshold applies to the worldwide taxable supplies, not Canadian activity only.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A limited partnership may zero-rate a supply of handmade sharp-edge epoxy resin liquid core RPG dice when the dice are sold or delivered to a non-resident person and are exported from Canada in accordance with Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. Zero-rating applies to goods: (a) supplied by the LP and exported by the LP or the recipient under the terms of the supply; (b) delivered by the LP to a common carrier or by mail/courier for export, with proof that the goods were exported; (c) exported and consumed outside Canada. Qualifying offshore services supplied to a non-resident may also be zero-rated if the services relate to tangible personal property sold to the non-resident (e.g., fulfillment or design services) or are performed wholly outside Canada, subject to the exceptions for services performed in Canada or relating to Canadian real property. The LP must obtain and retain documentary evidence that within a reasonable time (generally contemporaneous or within 180 days) the goods left Canada and payment was received from the non-resident.

  • Form B13A - Canada Customs Export Declaration (or CESAR / Export Reporting System transmission where applicable)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing shipment out of Canada
  • Commercial invoice describing the goods and indicating the foreign buyer
  • Packing list
  • Delivery or courier tracking record proving export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/payment rail evidence (e.g., PayPal, Stripe) showing proceeds received from the non-resident buyer

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, central digital stamping, or mandatory API-based e-invoicing regime for GST/HST. Registrants may issue electronic or paper invoices. CRA requires that all electronically generated records be retained in a legible, readable, and accessible format and be made available to the CRA upon request. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA NETFILE (GST/HST NETFILE) or tax preparation software submitted to CRA's My Business Account; threshold filers with a specified amount may be subject to mandatory electronic filing and remittance. There is no federal requirement for an XML invoice exchange or government clearance, but CRA accepts standardized electronic records for audit.

CRA scrutinizes export zero-rating claims for documentary sufficiency. If a supply is later found not to be zero-rated because goods were not exported or evidence is inadequate, the LP is reassessed for GST/HST at the full 5% federal rate on the selling price, with interest (exigible under section 280 of the Excise Tax Act) and penalties. A false statement or fictitious export may trigger the gross-negligence penalty under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act, which is generally the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the amount of the misstatement. Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act may follow for wilful evasions, with fines and possible imprisonment. The normal reassessment period for GST/HST is four years, but it is extended indefinitely where the taxpayer has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default. Certain exporters have a statutory requirement to file related information under section 186.1 or to correct incomplete declarations; failure to comply may result in deemed taxable supplies and denial of refunds. Canada does not impose a formal 180-day FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day standard is used as an evidentiary benchmark for proving export proceeds and satisfying CRA's documentary requirements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized regime with no general exchange-control legislation. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are freely convertible; the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) and the Bank of Canada Act govern instruments and settlement but impose no central-bank approval, licensing, or repatriation requirements on capital inflows, profit remittances, or partnership distributions. Non-resident limited partners may receive distributions without Bank of Canada or FINTRAC approval; however, remittances to non-residents may attract federal withholding tax under Income Tax Act Part XIII where the payment is characterized as interest, rent, royalties, management fees, or other prescribed amounts, whereas genuine pass-through partnership distributions are generally not subject to Part XIII withholding unless Canadian-source income is deemed to be paid to the non-resident partner. Practical friction points are bank-level rather than regulatory: enhanced FINTRAC reporting obligations on certain large cross-border virtual-currency transactions (CAD 10,000 threshold), the bank's obligation to characterize the source and purpose of funds, and the need to evidence the commercial substance underpinning inward wires, particularly from crowdfunding aggregators and payment service providers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender, mandatory conversion, or repatriation timeline on export proceeds. The limited partnership may retain 100% of foreign-currency export earnings indefinitely in a multicurrency or USD-denominated account at a Canadian-chartered bank, with no reporting to the Bank of Canada. For income tax and GST/HST purposes, foreign-currency amounts are translated to CAD in accordance with CRA administrative guidance using Bank of Canada daily exchange rates or CRA-published annual/quarterly average rates for GST/HST registrants. No FINTRAC report is triggered by ordinary export settlement, provided the underlying transaction is not a listed or virtual-currency transaction and no suspicious transaction indicators are present.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; exchange-control and AML/ATF supervision exercised by the Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential regulation of federally chartered banks, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing compliance.

Stripe Fit: High

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route exclusively via bank-to-bank SWIFT MT103 using the receiving bank's correspondent network; instruct the originator to insert in field 72: '/REC/ CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION TO CA_LP — NO COMMERCIAL INVOICE'. Applicable purpose code: equity contribution / partnership capital; no Bank of Canada or FINTRAC pre-approval is required, but the receiving branch must receive a KYC pre-alert containing the contributor's identification, the capital contribution agreement, and the draft wire to avoid a 5-7 business day compliance hold. CAD leg settles with finality through Payments Canada Lynx (RTGS); USD leg clears via Fedwire or CHIPS through the bank's US correspondent, after which funds are either converted to CAD at the bank's prevailing spot rate or credited to the USD sub-account. Do not use PayPal, Stripe, or crowdfunding platforms for capital injection; these rails are reserved for commercial receivables only.Primary rail: SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier payments (epoxy resin, pigments, mold tooling, packaging) with settlement prefunded in the invoice currency to eliminate FX settlement risk. Secondary rail: irrevocable at-sight commercial letter of credit issued by the tier-1 bank against 100% cash cover for large consigned raw-material orders, with confirmation from the supplier's advising bank where required. Domestic CAD supplier payments clear through Payments Canada ACSS batch, Lynx for high-value items, or Real-Time Rail (RTR) where the beneficiary bank is connected. PayPal-denominated supplier disbursements should be repatriated first to the operating account and then executed by wire or EFT to preserve a complete audit trail; direct PayPal 'Send' payments to suppliers are discouraged for material amounts. All disbursements must reference the underlying purchase order and invoice to satisfy CRA record-keeping and bank trade-surveillance review.Deliverable CAD/USD FX forwards (outright and swap-based) with tenors up to 12-24 months, executed under the bank's ISDA Master Agreement or a simple Certificate of FX Authority; recommended strategy: sell USD / buy CAD covering projected quarterly supplier invoice cycles while retaining a USD retention balance for USD-denominated supplier settlements.CAD/USD FX swaps (swap-in and swap-out rollovers) for liquidity rebalancing between the CAD operating account and the USD settlement sub-account.CAD/USD currency options (puts and calls) offered through the bank treasury desk, subject to premium collateralization and credit approval; appropriate for protecting the USD-revenue conversion rate during the Kickstarter presale funding window.Cross-currency swaps (CAD/USD) for longer-duration capital structure matching where non-resident partner loans or intercompany funding are serviced on a fixed schedule.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are not market-standard for CAD because the currency is freely convertible; NDFs are permissible only for exotic crosses where correspondent deliverability is unavailable, and require express treasury-desk approval and ISDA documentation.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Moderate feasibility with structural limitations. Non-Canadian digital-only EMIs (Wise Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, Intergiro) can hold CAD and provide USD/CAD clearing, but (i) they are not CDIC-insured banks and are treated as money-services businesses (MSBs); (ii) foreign-incorporated EMIs must register with FINTRAC as MSBs and implement a full compliance program unless exempt; (iii) several Canadian chartered banks reject incoming SWIFT credits routed through EMI aggregation accounts where beneficiary-name matching is unreliable, creating return and delay risk; (iv) EMI rails are appropriate for PayPal and Kickstarter (Stripe) payout concentration but must not replace a primary tier-1 operating account because CRA remittance obligations (GST/HST, partnership T5013 information returns) and trade counterparty trust are best serviced by a domestic bank. Rating: Supportable as an auxiliary collections/disbursement rail; not compliant as the sole corporate treasury anchor.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the LP entity level (fiscally transparent); Canadian corporate partners pay federal corporate income tax at 15% general rate, with 9% small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding on dividends paid by a taxable Canadian corporation; limited partnership distributions are not dividends and are not subject to Part XIII dividend withholding, but a 25% withholding applies to remittances of Canadian-source partnership income to non-resident partners unless reduced by treaty

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate non-arm's length cross-border transactions with a single non-resident related party in a fiscal year triggers the T106 information return and is the de facto threshold for preparing contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation; group consolidated revenue exceeding CAD 916 million triggers country-by-country reporting

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a jurisdiction-specific ESR like the UAE; however, CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245), the business purpose test, and substance-over-form doctrine. For limited partnerships, the at-risk rules under s.96(2.2)–(2.4) restrict loss claims to capital genuinely contributed; partners must demonstrate real business activity, local premises, operational personnel, and commercial substance for dice manufacturing and sales

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of tax evaded under ITA s.163(2); criminal conviction under ITA s.239 carries fines of 50–200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; interest accrues on all outstanding assessments

Director personal liability: The general partner and directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unpaid partnership taxes and penalties; intentional fraud or de facto management allows CRA to pierce the corporate veil and pursue personal bank accounts, travel bans, and seizure of personal property
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: 10% penalty on the gross transfer pricing adjustment under ITA s.247(3); the CRA may reassess beyond the normal limitation period in cases of misrepresentation, impose interest, and deny tax deductions for related-party payments without arm's length support

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner can be assessed personally for the corporate partner's transfer pricing penalty and unreassessed tax if they knowingly caused or acquiesced to the failure; cross-border fee agreements may be recharacterized as gifts or non-deductible distributions
Part XIII Withholding Tax Default

Statutory sanction: The unremitted amount is held in trust for the Crown under ITA s.227(4); penalties include 10% of the deficiency (20% on a second or subsequent failure) under s.227(8), plus daily interest and potential criminal prosecution for willful failure to deduct

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner are personally liable for unremitted withholdings under ITA s.227.1; CRA can issue director's certificates, register liens on personal assets, initiate bankruptcy proceedings, and restrict international travel without a clearance certificate
GAAR and Artificial Partnership Structure

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterizes the offending transaction, denies the tax benefit, and imposes a penalty of 15% of the amount of the tax benefit under ITA s.245(5); CRA may add interest and reassess beyond the normal reopening period

Director personal liability: Promoters and tax advisors face penalties for avoidance transactions under ITA s.163.2 up to CAD 100,000; directors of the corporate general partner who signed false statements are exposed to criminal charges and civil restitution, and may be jointly liable for all tax, penalties, and interest
