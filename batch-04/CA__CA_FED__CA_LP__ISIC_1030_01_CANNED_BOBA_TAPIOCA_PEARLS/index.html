Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Primary registration portal: Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/ business/register.html; ongoing administration via CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/represent-a-client.html).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a Canadian limited partnership (LP) must first register the LP provincially (e.g., Ontario Ministry of Government and Consumer Services for an extra-provincial or domestic LP). The LP then obtains a 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA using BRO or Form RC1, which serves as the sole federal tax identifier. If the LP makes taxable supplies exceeding CAD $30,000 in any calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters, or where the LP is engaged in a commercial activity in Canada, the LP must open a GST/HST account on the same BN (Excise Tax Act, subsections 240(1), 241(1)). The partnership itself is a distinct 'person' for GST/HST under section 272.1 of the Excise Tax Act. Non-resident partners are not required to obtain an individual BN solely as passive partners, but the non-resident partner carrying on business through the LP may be required to file a Canadian income tax return (Section 150, Income Tax Act) and to appoint a representative for service under section 130 of the Excise Tax Act. The BN application requires: legal name, provincial LP registration number, principal address, authorized individuals, and description of activities (NAICS 311999). Online BN issuance is immediate; GST/HST account activation is effective on the date of registration.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible: A Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act. The LP does not pay federal income tax; computes income at the partnership level; each partner is allocated a share of income, loss and taxable capital gains and is taxed on that share personally or at the corporate level if a corporate partner. The LP files an information return (T5013) annually within 5 months of fiscal year-end. For GST/HST, the LP is the registered person (section 272.1 ETA) and must charge and remit GST/HST on all taxable supplies. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible: A Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act. The LP does not pay federal income tax; computes income at the partnership level; each partner is allocated a share of income, loss and taxable capital gains and is taxed on that share personally or at the corporate level if a corporate partner. The LP files an information return (T5013) annually within 5 months of fiscal year-end. For GST/HST, the LP is the registered person (section 272.1 ETA) and must charge and remit GST/HST on all taxable supplies. Territorial scope: a non-resident partner carrying on business in Canada through the LP is taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act on its share of Canadian-source business income, and may also be subject to Part XIII withholding tax on distributions of passive income (e.g., interest, royalties) under section 212. The LP's principal business (ISIC 1030) is process manufacturing; no special payroll or excise licensing is required for this activity, but the LP must obtain a federal import-export account (RM number) with the Canada Border Services Agency if it imports the finished goods or raw materials, and it must register with the CFIA or Safe Food for Canadians Act if food-grade tapioca products are exported (license is mandatory under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations).

Available election forms

As a common-law jurisdiction, Canada does not operate a 'check-the-box' entity classification regime akin to the US. However, the following statutory elections are available: (1) GST/HST closely-related election under subsection 156(1) of the Excise Tax Act, permitting a corporate parent and a wholly-owned LP or another closely-related entity to elect (Form GST25) to treat taxable supplies at nil consideration, thereby eliminating GST/HST on inter-party manufacturing services, R&D services, management fees and intellectual property licensing; (2) voluntary GST/HST registration for small suppliers (section 239 ETA) below the CAD $30,000 threshold; (3) the election by a general partner or the partnership itself under subsection 96(3) of the Income Tax Act to establish a fiscal period for the partnership that is different from the taxation year of the partners; (4) an elective joint filing for a qualifying parent-subsidiary group (subsection 156(1) of the Income Tax Act) is not applicable to partnerships, but an LP can be part of a 'related group' for section 256 rules; (5) for non-resident partners, the treaty election under subsection 161(1) of the Income Tax Regulations allows the CRA to recognize the filing of a simplified return where the only Canadian-source income is business income through a Permanent Establishment. For hybrid mismatch (BEPS Action 2) scrutiny, Canada's Income Tax Act contains specific provisions (e.g., section 94.2 and the foreign affiliate rules under 113(2)) that target mismatched treatment; the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 ITA) and the anti-hybrid rules in Part XVI.1 (sections 97.1-97.5) apply to deny deductions for hybrid financial instruments and hybrid transfers, with immediate effect from fiscal years commencing after December 31, 2023.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000: the small-supplier threshold under subsection 148(1) of the Excise Tax Act. An LP becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant on the day it ceases to be a small supplier, i.e., when its total taxable supplies (worldwide, excluding zero-rated financial supplies) exceed CAD $30,000 in the current calendar quarter or over the last four calendar quarters (section 149 ETA). Once registered, the LP must remain registered for at least 12 months. For LPs operating exclusively in an HST province (e.g., Ontario at 13%), the registrant charges the combined federal-provincial HST; for LPs in British Columbia, Saskatchewan, or Manitoba, the additional provincial PST is collected separately, and the federal rate remains 5%. A non-resident LP is subject to the same threshold rules for supplies made within Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for exports of canned boba tapioca pearls (tangible personal property) is available under section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, which zero-rates a supply of tangible personal property that is exported from Canada by the supplier or the supplier's agent, or delivered by the supplier to a carrier, consignee, or custodian for delivery outside Canada. The supplier must retain proof of export as enumerated below. Canned boba pearls classified under HS heading 2001.90 or 2106.90 may also qualify as zero-rated basic groceries under Part III of Schedule VI (e.g., processed food for human consumption), except where excluded (beverages, confectionery, snack foods, or products in a sealed container prepared by the supplier with more than 10% of the price attributable to preparation). For export purposes, the exporter should rely on Part V section 1, which is unconditional and does not depend on the nature of the goods. Contract manufacturing services rendered to a non-resident for goods destined for export may be zero-rated under section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI, provided the services are performed in Canada and the goods are exported by the recipient. The LP must also be a registered GST/HST registrant to claim an input tax credit (ITC) on its input costs (ingredients, packaging, machinery) and to claim the export zero-rating.

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, SAF-T filing, or mandatory digital stamping on invoices. The compliance regime is self-assessment-based, with a statutory invoice rule: under subsection 223(1) of the Excise Tax Act, a registrant must provide a tax invoice to the recipient within 20 days of the day the supply is deemed made. Invoices must contain supplier BN, date, description, tax amount, and GST/HST registration number. Effective July 2025, Canada's Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework (PCIUS) allows voluntary participation through accredited service providers using the Peppol BIS 3.0 PINT standard; a business-to-business e-invoicing exchange is not yet mandatory. For export documentation, the CRA requires the export evidence to be retained for at least 6 years from the end of the fiscal year (subsection 286(2) ETA; section 230(4) ITA). The CRA receives electronic customs data via the Canada Border Services Agency's Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), which reconciles the B13A export declaration with GST/HST returns.

The CRA performs targeted audits of export zero-rating claims under the GST/HST Export Verification Program, cross-referencing B13A export declarations in CERS with GST/HST returns, T5013 filings, and bank records. Deficiencies in documentation (e.g., mismatched descriptions or missing inward remittance) lead to deemed taxable supplies and reassessment at 5% GST with arrears interest. Specific penalties include: (a) failure to register: penalty of CAD $250 plus 0.25% of the unreported tax (section 280.1 ETA); (b) false statements / tax evasion: penalty under section 285 ETA equal to 50% of the tax sought to be evaded (minimum CAD $100); (c) gross negligence: escalating penalties up to 100% under subsection 285(1); (d) six-year reassessment window for misrepresentation and unlimited reassessment for fraud (subsection 298(5) ETA); (e) criminal prosecution under section 327 ETA for willful evasion, with fines up to CAD $25,000 and imprisonment up to 12 months on summary conviction, and up to 5 years imprisonment plus unlimited fines on indictment; (f) clawback of refunds paid on zero-rated exports, with interest at the CRA's prescribed rate compounded daily; (g) for exports routed through a foreign free-trade zone or later diverted into the domestic stream, the CRA may assess the supplier for uncollected GST as though the supply was taxable domestically, and the CBSA may seize the goods under the Customs Act with forfeiture of up to 50% of the value.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full IMF Article VIII convertibility on both current and capital accounts; the Canadian dollar is a free-floating currency with no licensing or prior-approval requirement for inbound partner capital contributions, trade payments, or profit and distribution remittances to non-residents. Residual hurdles are tax-mechanical (Part I filing obligations for non-resident partners, Part XIII withholding on treaty-covered passive payments, and NR4 information returns) and AML-information-based (FINTRAC reporting for electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more, large cash transaction records, and beneficial ownership disclosure under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act).

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender regime exists in Canada: there is no mandatory conversion ratio, no timeline to repatriate export receipts, and no central bank claim on foreign currency earnings. Exporters may retain and deploy USD/CAD balances freely; the only obligations are Canada Border Services Agency export declarations under the Customs Act for shipments at or above the C$2,000 reporting threshold and FINTRAC EFT reporting for fund movements of C$10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating under the Bank of Canada Act and the Currency Act; foreign exchange statutory administration and the Exchange Fund Account are vested in the Minister of Finance, with the Bank of Canada acting as fiscal agent. AML/CFT supervision is exercised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and prudential bank supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

European-style EMI licences are not recognised in Canada. Digital payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (registration window 2024-2025) and, where offering foreign exchange dealing or international remittance, must also register with FINTRAC as a money services business. Viability rating: Medium for RPAA-registered Canadian PSPs such as Stripe Canada and Nuvei for ancillary collections; Low for offshore non-registered EMIs, which face blocked correspondent banking and FINTRAC enforcement. Core trade settlement must remain on Schedule I bank rails.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Digital payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (registration window 2024-2025) and, where offering foreign exchange dealing or international remittance, must also register with FINTRAC as a money services business.
