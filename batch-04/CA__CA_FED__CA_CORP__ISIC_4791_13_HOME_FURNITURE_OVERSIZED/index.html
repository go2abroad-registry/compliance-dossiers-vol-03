Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), 555 Mackenzie Avenue, Ottawa, Ontario, Canada; centralized tax administration portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; electronic services through CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business.html.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act via Corporations Canada online filing (or, for a foreign entity, register extra-provincially in Canada) and obtain the certificate of incorporation. Step 2: Obtain the CRA Business Number (BN), Canada's federal tax identification number, through Business Registration Online for immediate issuance or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number); a CBCA-incorporated corporation will receive its BN automatically from CRA data exchanged with Corporations Canada. Step 3: Register for the GST/HST program account (BN with RT suffix) by selecting GST/HST in BRO or adding the account on Form RC1; a non-resident seller without Canadian physical presence may instead use the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration procedure, submitting foreign registry documents and designating a Canadian representative. Step 4: Confirm the registration nexus of gross taxable supplies in Canada exceeding C$30,000 in a calendar quarter or over the preceding four quarters; voluntary registration is permitted to claim input tax credits when making zero-rated exports. Step 5: Select the reporting frequency (monthly, quarterly, or annual, based on revenue; non-residents under the simplified program generally file annually with quarterly installments) and enroll in CRA My Business Account to receive notices of assessment, file returns, and effect payments via bank transfer or SWIFT. Step 6: Maintain separate CRA program accounts for payroll (RC) or import/export (RM) only where needed; provincial indirect tax accounts (Québec QST, British Columbia PST, Saskatchewan PST, Manitoba RST) are administered by the respective provincial revenue authorities and are not covered by the federal BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A federal business corporation resident in Canada is taxed at the corporate level under Part I of the Income Tax Act: the general federal rate is 38%, reduced by a 10% provincial abatement to 28%, further reduced to 15% under the general corporate rate; a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) qualifies for the 9% small business rate on the first C$500,000 of active business income. The territorial scope is worldwide income for resident corporations, with foreign tax credits for foreign-source earnings; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada are subject to Canada-source net-basis tax under Part I and a 25% branch tax under Part XIV (subject to treaty reduction). Canada does not offer pass-through or Subchapter S-equivalent status for corporations.

Available election forms

Canada does not have a US-style check-the-box election. Relevant statutory elections include: (1) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election for closely related corporations to treat GST/HST supplies as made for no consideration; (2) ETA s. 211 election for financial institutions to group GST/HST liabilities; (3) ETA s. 273 election authorizing a non-resident person's agent to file and remit GST/HST; (4) Income Tax Act s. 216 election for a non-resident to file a taxable Canadian rental income return in lieu of 25% Part XIII gross-basis withholding; (5) ITA s. 217 election for a non-resident to file a Canadian employment income return; (6) ITA s. 89(11) election to designate a dividend as a capital dividend; (7) ITA s. 93(1)(a) election on a foreign affiliate share disposition to convert capital gains into an exempt intercorporate dividend; (8) hybrid mismatch rules under ITA ss. 18.2 and 18.21 disallowing certain excessive interest arrangements, with mandatory disclosure rules for reportable transactions. For GST/HST export zero-rating, no separate election is required; the classification is statutory, and the small supplier may elect to register voluntarily under ETA s. 148(3).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000. Under ETA s. 148(1), a person is a small supplier if total consideration for taxable supplies made in Canada in a current calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters does not exceed C$30,000; effective July 1, 2021, non-resident suppliers (including online sellers of oversized furniture) must register for GST/HST once taxable sales to Canadian consumers exceed C$30,000 in any 12-month period, and online marketplace operators are deemed suppliers and must register on the same C$30,000 threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of oversized furniture and home decor exported from Canada qualify for the 0% GST/HST rate under s. 1, Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act, provided the vendor exports the goods in the course of its commercial activity and obtains proof of export within 60 days after the supply is made, or delivers the goods to a common carrier for export. Offshore services related to the exported goods (e.g., white-glove delivery arrangements or international logistics coordination) may be zero-rated under s. 7, Part V, Schedule VI where the service is supplied to a non-resident. A registered Canadian corporation selling zero-rated exports remains entitled to input tax credits for GST/HST paid on Canadian inputs (warehousing, freight, marketing), and the threshold does not restrict ITC recovery for zero-rated exporters.

  • Commercial invoice with vendor Business Number, purchaser name and address, HS codes, Incoterms rule, and the zero-rating notation 'Exported - Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1, Excise Tax Act'
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (including house/ocean bill for oversized containerized furniture freight) evidencing departure from Canada to a foreign destination
  • Packing list with oversized and overweight cargo marks (dimensions, weight, shipment count) consistent with the customs declaration
  • Proof of export (CERS confirmation, carrier attestation, customs-stamped export declaration, or foreign customs entry document)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT/bank credit advice evidencing receipt of foreign currency proceeds within the prescribed 180-day repatriation period

Canada does not currently impose a statutory real-time fiscal clearance, API, or digital stamping mandate for GST/HST invoices. CRA requires prescribed invoice content under ETA s. 223 (supplier identity and BN, date of invoice, description of taxable supplies, consideration, and GST/HST charged) and retention of all books, records, and electronic invoices for at least six years. The Pan-Canadian e-Invoicing initiative promotes voluntary Peppol-compliant B2B and B2G e-invoicing, and CRA accepts EDI, XML, and PDF invoices; however, no government pre-clearance or blockchain-stamping requirement exists. Where the vendor sells through an online marketplace, the marketplace operator must collect, remit, and identify GST/HST on the supply, and the marketplace receipt serves as the fiscal invoice.

CRA audits zero-rated export claims in the e-commerce furniture sector with targeted scrutiny, cross-matching CERS export declarations, GST/HST returns, banking inflows, and online marketplace seller records. Where adequate proof of export is absent, CRA reassesses the sale at 5% GST, denies tied input tax credits, and assesses statutory interest at prescribed quarterly CRA rates (currently in the 6%-7% annual range for corporate balances). Specific penalties include: ETA s. 280(1) failure-to-register penalty of 25% of the net tax; ETA s. 287 false-statement penalty equal to the greater of C$100 and 25% of the tax deficiency; ETA s. 287.1 gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated net tax; and ETA s. 327 criminal prosecution for evasion, with fines up to C$10,000 plus an amount up to 100% of the evaded tax and/or imprisonment for up to five years, applicable to fraudulent 'phantom export' schemes where goods never leave Canada. The CRA normal reassessment period is four years, extended to ten years where misrepresentation or fraud exists, and a non-resident's assessment period is not limited where the person has failed to file a statutory return. Export zero-rating claims discovered to be fraudulent are clawed back retroactively with full interest and penalties, and relief is available only through a successful Voluntary Disclosures Program application before CRA contact.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has held IMF Article VIII status since 1952. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are fully convertible with no prior approval, no import/export licensing for FX, and no profit-remittance authorization. Non-resident repatriation of dividends, branch profits, interest, royalties, and management fees is unrestricted, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25%; treaty-reduced, e.g., 15% or 5% under the Canada-US Treaty) and CRA reporting. The Investment Canada Act applies a post-closing notification obligation (within 30 days to ISED) for establishment of a new Canadian business by a non-Canadian investor, and a net-benefit review only for high-value controlling acquisitions above indexed thresholds (CAD 1.37B WTO-enterprise threshold for 2025); ordinary treasury flows are not reviewable. No capital controls, no Tobin-type taxes, no mandatory conversion or deposit requirements apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no designated account requirement for export receipts. All proceeds from the online retail export of oversized furniture may be retained in any eligible currency (USD, EUR, CAD) in either resident or non-resident bank accounts. The only obligations are accounting/tax-related: accrual recognition under ASC 606/IFRS 15, GST/HST zero-rating of exported goods with documentary proof (B13A, courier waybill, customs declaration), and FINTRAC EFT reporting obligations of the bank for transfers of CAD 10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as central monetary authority; the Department of Finance Canada holds policy responsibility for the Exchange Fund Account; OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) supervises federally chartered banks; FINTRAC administers AML/CTF supervision under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); Payments Canada operates Lynx, ACSS, and the Real-Time Rail with Bank of Canada oversight. No dedicated foreign-exchange control agency exists because Canada maintains no exchange controls.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-Medium as primary treasury rail; Medium-High as supplementary collection or disbursement account. Canada has no EU-style EMI passporting regime. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Mercury) may open Canadian-dollar settlement balances via local partner banks, but as of the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration deadlines (registration opened November 2024, deadline September 2025), any payment service provider processing Canadian payments must be registered with the Bank of Canada. An unregistered offshore EMI is a material compliance blocker for Tier-1 Canadian onboarding relationships and may trigger de-risking reviews. Legal constraints: (i) foreign EMI cannot be a direct participant in Payments Canada clearing; (ii) must route via a licensed Canadian bank partner; (iii) client funds held at a foreign EMI are not covered by CDIC; (iv) CRA and OSFI scrutiny of pooled accounts; and (v) structure should be limited to pre-authorization gateways rather than primary treasury custody.

Non-resident shareholder injections must be routed as SWIFT MT103 (or Fedwire/CHIPS for USD) to the Canadian Tier-1 bank using the beneficiary's Canadian bank account format: 7-digit transit number + 5-digit institution number + 10-digit account number (CABA), or the IBAN-equivalent SWIFT BIC (e.g., ROYCCAT2, TDOMCATTTOR, BOFMCAM2). Purpose-of-payment narrative must state 'Share Capital Contribution' or 'Direct Investment — New Canadian Business' and reference the shareholder resolution; foreign financing statements should include the benefitting entity name and the subscription agreement reference. The bank will verify the source of funds (bank statements, audited financials of the remitter) and the bank must report EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC within 30 days. The company must file the Investment Canada Act notification (ISED Form 2) within 30 days of establishing the Canadian business. Funds are then held in a CAD operational or escrowed capital account; no prior approval is required. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Low-Medium as primary treasury rail; Medium-High as supplementary collection or disbursement account. Canada has no EU-style EMI passporting regime. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Mercury) may open Canadian-dollar settlement balances via local partner banks, but as of the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration deadlines (registration opened November 2024, deadline September 2025), any payment service provider processing Canadian payments must be registered with the Bank of Canada. An unregistered offshore EMI is a material compliance blocker for Tier-1 Canadian onboarding relationships and may trigger de-risking reviews. Legal constraints: (i) foreign EMI cannot be a direct participant in Payments Canada clearing; (ii) must route via a licensed Canadian bank partner; (iii) client funds held at a foreign EMI are not covered by CDIC; (iv) CRA and OSFI scrutiny of pooled accounts; and (v) structure should be limited to pre-authorization gateways rather than primary treasury custody.
