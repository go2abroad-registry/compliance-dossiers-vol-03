Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Federal online portal: My Business Account (MBA) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html. Corporations Canada and provincial registries administer incorporation filings, but all federal Part I income tax and GST/HST accounts are administered exclusively by the CRA; an entity carrying on business in Quebec also liaises with Revenu Quebec for the Quebec Sales Tax (QST) after securing its federal registration.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian Business Corporation obtains a 9-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA. Immediately upon federal or provincial incorporation, the corporation must use the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) portal to activate program accounts: RC (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), and RP (payroll) where applicable. A foreign entity entry process generally requires: (i) incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act with a Canadian registered office and at least one resident Canadian director (or 25% of the board for larger boards), (ii) submission of articles, initial notice of directors, and agent-for-service details through the registry, and (iii) completion of CRA Form RC1 or BRO online screening. The BN is issued in approximately 1 to 5 business days for online applications; GST/HST registration is immediate in most cases, and the RT account must be opened before taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxable legal person under the Income Tax Act; corporate profits are taxed at the entity level, and no pass-through of income or losses to shareholders occurs. For CA_CORP engaged in cross-border tax structuring advisory, the standard federal classification is entity-level taxation, notwithstanding that a foreign jurisdiction may treat the Canadian corporation differently under its own entity classification rules.

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a US-style check-the-box election; an incorporated entity is classified as a taxable corporation at the entity level under the Income Tax Act. Important statutory elections for cross-border structuring include: ITA s. 85(1) rollover (tax-deferred asset transfer to a taxable Canadian corporation), ITA s. 88(1) winding-up transfer, ITA s. 39(4) deemed capital gains election, ITA s. 93(1) foreign affiliate dividend-sourcing election, ITA s. 216 non-resident rental income election, and ETA s. 156 elective nil-consideration treatment for taxable supplies between closely related corporations. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are curtailed by the ITA s. 245 general anti-avoidance rule and the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL), which applies to interest and financing deductions for tax years beginning on or after 1 October 2023.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. Under section 148 of the Excise Tax Act, a Canadian corporation is a small supplier exempt from mandatory GST/HST registration only if the aggregate consideration for worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies and supplies by associated persons) does not exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or across the four preceding consecutive quarters. Professional fees charged by a cross-border tax structuring consultancy are taxable supplies; once worldwide billings exceed CAD 30,000, an RT (GST/HST) account is mandatory regardless of the client being a non-resident, unless the supply is zero-rated as an exported service.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 of the Excise Tax Act, a registered supply of professional advisory services to a non-resident person is zero-rated (0% GST/HST) when the service does not relate to real property situated in Canada or to tangible personal property situated in Canada. This applies irrespective of where the advisory work is physically performed, provided the recipient is not a GST/HST registrant, or, if the recipient is registered, the service is not supplied for the non-resident's Canadian commercial activity. Tax structuring and management-consulting fees rendered to an overseas corporate group therefore normally qualify as zero-rated exports, permitting the full claim of input tax credits on related acquisition costs; a portion of the service relating to Canadian-situated assets, Canadian real estate transactions, or a Canadian-resident beneficiary of the advice would break the zero-rating for that part and trigger 5% GST or applicable HST.

  • CBSA export declaration (Form B13A or Canada Border Export Report) — required only where tangible deliverables or equipment physically cross the border; for pure consulting services, no customs declaration is imposed
  • Bill of lading or air waybill together with carrier master files — supporting physical export movement when goods accompany the service mandate
  • Signed engagement letter / statement of work naming the non-resident client and the overseas advisory scope
  • Certificate of non-residency of the client (foreign tax identification number, foreign certificate of incorporation, W-8BEN-E, or equivalent attestation) and confirmation that the client is not registered for GST/HST in Canada in relation to the service
  • Commercial invoice stating the 0% GST/HST export classification (Schedule VI, Part V, section 7) with a clear supply classification memorandum
  • Inward remittance certificate / bank wire records (SWIFT MT103) proving settlement from the non-resident jurisdiction
  • Transaction and performance records (deliverables, meeting notes, workpapers) demonstrating that the advisory work is performed for the non-resident and not in respect of Canadian real or tangible property

No real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory e-invoicing regime is in force in Canada. The CRA does not require digital stamping, API-based pre-clearance, or government-side validation of invoices for GST/HST purposes. The CRA operates a voluntary PEPPOL B2G e-invoicing pilot and is consulting on a future national e-invoicing framework. Mandatory electronic reporting applies to GST/HST net-filing and to T2 corporate income tax returns for most corporations; registrants must retain books and records in Canada (or produce them within 30 days of CRA demand) under subsection 286(3) of the Excise Tax Act.

CRA may reassess GST/HST for up to six years after the filing due date in ordinary cases and without time limitation where misrepresentation or omission is established. If an export zero-rating position is disallowed, the corporation is liable for the full unremitted GST/HST (5% or the applicable provincial HST component), daily prescribed interest, and a section 280 ETA penalty equal to 6% of the unremitted amount; a separate penalty of up to CAD 250 plus 25% of the deficiency may apply under section 285 for careless or false claims. The correspondingly claimed input tax credits are also clawed back on reassessment, creating a potential effective recovery of up to 100% of the unrecognized 5% to 15% tax. Criminal liability under section 327 ETA or section 239 ITA can trigger fines and imprisonment. For multinational reporting groups, CRA will cross-check zero-rated positions against Forms T1134, T1135, and the Country-by-Country Report; a compliance file containing the export substantiation bundle and intra-180-day FX repatriation evidence is the primary defense against audit adjustments.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current account and capital account. There are no exchange control restrictions, advance approvals, two-tier exchange rates, or balance-of-payment thresholds applicable to foreign participation in a federally incorporated business corporation. Profit remittance is unrestricted in form, but economic friction arises through the Income Tax Act: Part XIII withholding tax applies to payments to non-residents (dividends, interest, rents, royalties, management or administration fees) at a 25% statutory rate, with treaty-reduced rates generally ranging from 0% to 15% depending on payment character, ownership threshold, and permanent establishment status. For an ISIC 7020 tax consultancy, outbound fees must be supported by transfer pricing documentation under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. Non-Canadian investors are also subject to the Investment Canada Act: training or notification obligations apply depending on control type and enterprise value thresholds (WTO-investor review threshold for 2025 is approximately C$1.353 billion; a new unrelated business requires a simple notification). No federal or provincial approval is required by the Bank of Canada for inbound share capital wires.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds or foreign currency receipts. A Canadian business corporation may retain, pay, lend, or convert export earnings in any currency without advance approval. The only obligations are reporting: FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reports (EFTR) for any inbound/outbound international wire transfer of C$10,000 or more, and Statistics Canada international transactions reporting used in balance-of-payments compilation. There is no obligation to convert USD receivables into CAD within a specified period.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign exchange control administration; exchange control competence is residual, shared with FINTRAC (transaction monitoring and $10,000+ reporting), OSFI (prudential regulation of financial institutions), and the Minister of Finance (Investment Canada Act review for non-Canadian acquisitions and new businesses). The Bank of Canada exercises monetary policy, operates the Lynx payment system, and oversees the Retail Payment Activities Act registration regime for payment service providers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium with material legal constraints. A Canadian-regulated fintech payment service provider that partners with a Schedule I bank can provide multi-currency wallets, virtual IBANs, and cross-border collections; such providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA framework in force since November 2024) and comply with OSFI outsourcing rules. Offshore or non-Canadian electronic money institutions (EMIs), including EU-licensed virtual IBAN issuers, cannot lawfully take deposits or clear through Canadian payment rails (Lynx or Real-Time Rail) without holding, or contracting through, a Canadian-regulated clearing bank. An offshore EMI may be used only as a supplementary collection rail if it does not solicit deposits from, or market deposit services to, Canadian residents; using it as the primary corporate treasury account would create unlicensed deposit-taking risk under the Bank Act and trigger RCMP/OSFI concern. Recommendation: use a Canadian Schedule I bank as the primary rail and a licensed fintech corridor (e.g., Wise Platform or equivalent via a bank partner) for low-value payout aggregation only.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing: inbound share capital may be wired by SWIFT MT103 in USD to the Canadian bank's US correspondent (Fedwire/CHIPS) with internal conversion into CAD at the bank's commercially agreed FX rate, or directly in CAD as an international MT103 into the beneficiary's Lynx-participant account. The remittance information field (field 70) must explicitly state 'Share Capital Subscription – [Corporation Name], CBCA Corporation No. [number]', or alternatively 'Intercompany Loan – Maturity [date] – CBCA No. [number]'. The payee bank will require a bank confirmation of source, a subscribed share resolution, an updated register of individuals with significant control, and the Investment Canada Act notification reference where the investor is non-Canadian. For reporting, the payment must be classified under the appropriate FINTRAC EFT purpose code for equity capital or securities transaction; an EFTR is mandatory for every international transfer of C$10,000 or more. The capital may be held in an escrow account until the closing conditions are met, then released to the operational account via an internal book transfer that preserves the audit trail.Clearing rails: (i) CAD disbursements settle through Lynx, Canada's real-time gross settlement system, providing finality and eliminating counterparty settlement risk; (ii) USD disbursements clear via CHIPS/Fedwire using the Canadian bank's US correspondent network; (iii) cross-border fee payments to overseas subcontractors or advisors use SWIFT MT103 with full field 70 narrative referencing the engagement agreement invoice number; (iv) commercial documentary credits are issued as SWIFT MT700/MT701 and advised/confirmed through correspondent banks, and stand-by letters of credit as MT760 for performance bonds in US and offshore client contracts; (v) low-value instant payments may use Real-Time Rail (RTR) for domestic Canadian legs; the international leg still requires MT103. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium with material legal constraints. A Canadian-regulated fintech payment service provider that partners with a Schedule I bank can provide multi-currency wallets, virtual IBANs, and cross-border collections; such providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA framework in force since November 2024) and comply with OSFI outsourcing rules. Offshore or non-Canadian electronic money institutions (EMIs), including EU-licensed virtual IBAN issuers, cannot lawfully take deposits or clear through Canadian payment rails (Lynx or Real-Time Rail) without holding, or contracting through, a Canadian-regulated clearing bank. An offshore EMI may be used only as a supplementary collection rail if it does not solicit deposits from, or market deposit services to, Canadian residents; using it as the primary corporate treasury account would create unlicensed deposit-taking risk under the Bank Act and trigger RCMP/OSFI concern. Recommendation: use a Canadian Schedule I bank as the primary rail and a licensed fintech corridor (e.g., Wise Platform or equivalent via a bank partner) for low-value payout aggregation only.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate before provincial/territorial allocation; combined federal-provincial rates generally range from approximately 23% to 31% depending on the province or territory and permanent-establishment allocation.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a fixed C$ threshold for Local File or Master File documentation; all cross-border non-arm's length transactions must be contemporaneously documented under s.247 of the Income Tax Act. The T106 information return is mandatory where aggregate reportable transactions with a non-resident exceed C$1,000,000 in a taxation year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations such as those in the UAE. Instead, the CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule under s.245, sham and agency doctrines, and transfer-pricing rules under s.247 to disregard artificial transactions, shell entities, and arrangements that lack real economic substance, business purpose, and arm's-length remuneration.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under s.239 of the Income Tax Act can result in substantial fines and imprisonment of up to five years; civil gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2) of 50% of the amount of tax understated are also assessed, plus interest and daily compounding.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, participate in, or acquiesce to evasion may be personally prosecuted; CRA may pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a fraudulent façade, enabling civil seizure of personal assets, garnishment, freeze orders, and travel restrictions while criminal proceedings are ongoing.
