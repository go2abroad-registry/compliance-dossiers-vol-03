Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), under the Minister of National Revenue; administered through the CRA My Business Account portal and Business Registration Online (BRO); customs and export declarations are filed through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada or supplying taxable digital services to Canadian recipients must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST account designation (suffix 'RT'). Steps: (1) assess Canadian nexus (permanent establishment for income tax under s. 400(2) ITR, or Canadian 'qualifying revenue' exceeding CAD 30,000 under the simplified GST/HST regime for digital services); (2) register electronically via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO), or file Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' with the Sudbury Tax Centre; (3) where the non-resident has no Canadian address, appoint a Canadian representative and file Form RC59 'Business Number Request for a Non-Resident Business'; (4) receive the BN/RT account and Notice of Registration; (5) enrol in CRA My Business Account, elect a GST/HST reporting period (monthly/quarterly/annual), and file all returns electronically. Failure to register triggers a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 1% of gross Canadian revenue.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act (Inc./Corp.) is a taxable Canadian corporation subject to Part I of the Income Tax Act on worldwide income. The federal statutory rate of 38% is reduced to 15% after the 10% provincial abatement and 13% general corporate reduction; the 9% small business rate applies to Canadian-controlled private corporations on active business income up to the federal business limit. Non-resident corporations are taxable only on Canadian-source business income attributable to a permanent establishment in Canada (Part I) plus a 25% branch tax under Part XIV, commonly treaty-reduced. For GST/HST, the corporation is a distinct registrant; there is no pass-through, flow-through, or tax-exempt-zone classification available to a standard CBCA corporation.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' elective classification. The operative statutory elections are: (i) functional currency election under subsection 261(2) ITA to compute Canadian Part I tax in USD; (ii) subsection 216(1) election for non-residents to file a net-basis Part I return for Canadian rental income instead of gross-basis Part XIII withholding; (iii) section 85 ITA rollover election to defer capital gain on qualifying transfers to a taxable Canadian corporation; (iv) GST/HST elections to change the fiscal period and reporting basis for non-resident registrants; (v) treaty-based permanent-establishment and residency claims through Form NR301 and related CRA administrative relief. Hybrid-mismatch financing structures are constrained by s. 247 transfer-pricing rules, Part VI.1, Part II.2 and the foreign affiliate dumping provisions of Part XVII; no elective hybrid-mismatch carve-out exists under federal law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — general 'small supplier' threshold measured on the aggregate value of worldwide taxable supplies in the four preceding calendar quarters (or the current calendar quarter for a new business); a non-resident carrying on business in Canada must register when Canadian taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000. Under the simplified GST/HST framework effective July 1, 2021, non-resident suppliers of remote-screen-share, telemetry and other digital services must also register when 'qualifying revenue' from Canadian recipients exceeds CAD 30,000 in any calendar year; voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating (0% GST/HST) is available where the supply is made in Canada and is either an export of goods or a supply to a non-resident. Under Part V of Schedule VI to the ETA: (1) tangible personal property exported from Canada is zero-rated (items 1 and 3); (2) a service supplied to a non-resident is zero-rated under item 14 unless the service is supplied in respect of real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or the service is performed in Canada; (3) services in respect of intangible personal property (software licences, IoT telemetry data) situated outside Canada at the time of supply are zero-rated. Remote desktop support and unattended-machine telemetry software supplied to non-resident customers whose machines are located outside Canada may be zero-rated where the recipient is a bona fide non-resident not registered for GST/HST, service performance occurs outside Canada (offshore server-side access), and no Canadian situs attaches to the service. The vendor must maintain contemporaneous evidence of export, including the customer's foreign address, non-resident status, foreign IP/proxy telemetry, exclusion of Canadian-located equipment from the service scope, and non-resident payment records.

  • CBSA export declaration (CARM portal) or Canada Customs Invoice for any physical goods forming part of the exported supply
  • Commercial invoice and executed software subscription/remote-support agreement identifying the non-resident customer and foreign service location
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill or electronic carrier manifest for goods; for pure digital IoT telemetry and remote-desktop services: server access logs, IP-geolocation reports, and usage audit trails evidencing service delivery outside Canada
  • Bank credit advice, foreign-exchange conversion statement, or electronic fund transfer record from a Canadian financial institution evidencing receipt of foreign-currency proceeds (Canada does not issue statutory inward remittance certificates)
  • Certificate of incorporation, foreign residency certificate, or non-resident declaration of the customer where available to corroborate non-resident status

No real-time fiscal clearance, API digital stamping, or per-invoice CRA pre-approval exists. E-invoicing is voluntary; Canada supports the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and Peppol BIS 3.0 e-invoicing for B2G procurement. CRA accepts paper, PDF/XML, EDI (ANSI X12 / UN/EDIFACT), and API-submitted records through GST/HST NETFILE/EFILE and My Business Account correspondence. Since April 2024, the federal Financial Data Reporting (FDR) framework requires financial institutions to report electronic fund transfer data to the CRA, enabling automated cross-checking of invoicing, payment flows, and declared export revenue; however, there is no statutory mandate for electronic clearance or digital stamping of invoices.

CRA may reassess beyond the normal four-year limitation period where a taxpayer has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, and may retroactively deny zero-rating, requiring repayment of uncollected GST/HST plus compound daily interest at the prescribed CRA rate. Gross-negligence false-statement penalties under ETA s. 285 equal the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the tax understated or rebate/refund overstated; failure-to-register penalties equal the greater of CAD 250 and 1% of gross consideration; failure-to-file penalties accrue at 1% of net tax plus 25% of that amount per full month of default, capped at 12 months. Criminal evasion under ETA s. 327 carries, upon conviction on indictment, fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to five years (summary conviction: CAD 50,000 and up to two years), in addition to forfeiture of input tax credits, interest charges, and public listing by the CRA.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has a fully liberalised, floating-rate open economy with no operative exchange controls (the former Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951); current and capital account flows are freely convertible without central bank approval or remittance licences. Canadian dollars are legal tender under the Currency Act (R.S.C. 1985, c. C-52), but businesses may hold USD or other foreign currency bank balances without restriction. Income and transactions must be reported in CAD for tax purposes (Income Tax Act s. 261); profit remittance by way of dividend, interest or royalty is subject to Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory, reduced under treaties, e.g., Canada-US 5% for a 10%+ corporate shareholder) but carries no FX liquidity or convertibility obstacle. Software subscription receipts from foreign customers may be retained in original currency or converted freely; receiving banks handle FINTRAC large-transaction reporting.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio and no repatriation quota on foreign-currency receipts. Exporters may hold 100% of export proceeds in CAD, USD or other currencies, onshore or offshore. Obligations are limited to statistical reporting: goods exporters file Electronic Export Reporting (EER) through the Canada Border Services Agency, and service exporters (including remote desktop access and IoT telemetry software) respond to the Statistics Canada International Transactions in Services Survey when sampled. Foreign-currency gains and losses on export receivables are computed at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate for income-tax purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (monetary authority and financial system stability); Department of Finance Canada (foreign-exchange policy); Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision of commercial banks; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF supervision under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general rate 15% (computed as 38% base less 10% provincial abatement less 13% general rate reduction); combined federal-provincial statutory rates range from 23% to 31% depending on province, with Ontario at 26.5%, British Columbia at 27%, and Quebec at 26.5%. Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) are eligible for the 9% federal small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income, subject to federal-provincial allocation and business limit reduction on taxable capital.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on taxable dividends paid to non-residents; reduced to 15% for portfolio shareholders, 5% for a company resident in a tax treaty country that controls at least 10% of the voting shares (e.g., Canada-United States Tax Convention Article X); zero-rate exemptions for certain pension fund or governmental investors.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory de minimis exemption for transfer pricing documentation; every corporation engaged in cross-border non-arm's length transactions must maintain contemporaneous Local File and Master File-level records sufficient to demonstrate arm's length pricing, including transactional benchmarking, functional analysis, and an intercompany agreement. Form T106 mandatory when the aggregate value of reportable transactions with related non-residents equals or exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in the fiscal year. Administrative penalty protection: the 10% mispricing penalty is not applied where the net transfer pricing adjustment is equal to or less than the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue for the year. For small businesses with gross revenue below CAD 10,000,000, simplified documentation is accepted in practice, but must still include the basis for pricing and identification of comparable transactions.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply a formulized Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) regime comparable to the UAE or BVI. Instead, the Income Tax Act contains a comprehensive General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245) that disallows tax benefits from transactions or series of transactions where no bona fide purpose exists other than to obtain a tax benefit, and where the transaction results directly or indirectly in misuse or abuse of the Act read as a whole. CRA's transfer pricing rules under section 247 allow recharacterization of a transaction as that which would have been reasonable had the parties dealt at arm's length, including imputation of income to the Canadian entity where the economic substance of the functions, assets, and risks lies outside the nominal contractor. For a remote screen-share IoT business, economic substance is demonstrated through actual Canadian employees performing software development and user support, Canadian-based servers or cloud contracts, invoices and payment flows through the Canadian entity, board minutes, and substantive intercompany agreements; a deviceless entity that only invoices via Stripe/Paddle through a Canadian mailbox without operational activity will be reattributed under the fact-based GAAR and section 247.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for a term of up to 2 years on summary conviction (section 239(1)); on indictment (conspiracy to evade), fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; in addition, gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax under section 163(2), plus arrears interest and any professional costs.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized or acquiesced in the fraud are jointly and severally criminally liable; CRA may issue a formal demand for the amount under section 227.1 where the corporation fails to remit withheld amounts, but directors can also be prosecuted as parties to the offence under section 21 of the Criminal Code. Civil restitution extends to the evaded tax, penalties, and interest; departure prohibitions may be imposed at the Canada-US border if the fraud involves amounts above CAD 200,000, and a warrant for arrest may issue under the Extradition Act.
Shell Corporation / Artificial Restructuring (GAAR)

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under section 247 to recompute income on an arm's length basis; GAAR recharacterization under section 245 with loss of the tax benefit, plus a 25% penalty of the tax benefit for transactions occurring after January 15, 2023; the CCRA may also revoke the Canadian corporation's business registration and impose director penalties under section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions; fines up to CAD 25,000 per false information return.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal assessment of the unreported tax under section 227.1 and potential court costs; where the arrangement is found to be a sham, the corporate veil is pierced and shareholders holding a 10% or greater interest are assessed the portion of the tax benefit allocated to them; travel bans and civil contempt sanctions can be ordered for failure to comply with production orders.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: 10% transfer pricing penalty equal to the amount of the net adjustment (section 247(3)); additional penalty of CAD 100/day per delayed T106 return up to CAD 25,000, and CAD 500/day up to CAD 125,000 where the failure was knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; the penalty is incurred only if the aggregate adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue, but the CRA does not exercise prosecutorial discretion for chronic T106 non-filing; statutory interest accrues on all revised assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are not automatically liable for corporate transfer-pricing penalties, but a personal misrepresentation claim under section 163.2 can be asserted against a director who signed a false or reckless document; the director's foreign travel may be restricted if a CRA compliance order is granted under the Canada-US Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty; unpaid penalties imposed on the corporation can be collected against director assets if the corporation is dissolved without reserving funds.
AML / Sanctions and Unauthorised Remote Access (SEMA, PIPEDA, CIFAR)

Statutory sanction: Up to CAD 25,000,000 fine per count for organizations, and up to CAD 20,000,000 for subsequent offences, or imprisonment of executives up to 10 years for wilful breaches of orders made under SEMA; civil liability under PIPEDA for damages and administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 100,000 per violation; under Canada's Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act, repatriation and seizure orders may be issued; the corporation may also be added to the Canadian Consolidated Autonomous Sanctions List, destroying the Stripe/Paddle merchant account.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who approved sales to sanctioned entities may be prosecuted personally; travel bans under the Canadian Passport Order are available against officers whose conduct poses a serious threat; personal monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years apply to individuals directly involved in prohibited transactions; assets held in Canadian financial institutions can be frozen, and an arrest warrant can trigger extradition under mutual legal assistance treaties with allied states.
False SR&ED Claims and Financial Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Recovery of the full credit plus interest; a gross negligence penalty of 50% of the overstated credit under section 163(2); prosecution under section 239(1) for making false statements in a return, with fines from 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 2 years; additional administrative revocation of SR&ED accreditation with potential exclusion from future claims.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed the T661 declaration are personally liable for the false statement under the Criminal Code; CRA may pursue directors of a dissolved company to the extent of distributions received during the winding-up; professional negligence claims by minority shareholders can expose directors to personal no-cap restitution where the company is insolvent; international travel restrictions may be imposed in parallel with a tax investigation if assets exceed a materiality threshold.
