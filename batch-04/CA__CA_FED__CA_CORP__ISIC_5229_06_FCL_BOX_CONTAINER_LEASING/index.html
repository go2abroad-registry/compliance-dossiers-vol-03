Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal; GST/HST account registration via My Business Account

E-government filing portal

Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must register for a Business Number (BN) with CRA. For GST/HST, a foreign entity must register if it is a non-resident that makes taxable supplies in Canada in the course of a business carried on in Canada or exceeds the small supplier threshold (C$30,000 in global taxable supplies over the preceding four calendar quarters, inclusive of Canadian and foreign supplies). Registration is effected online through CRA's Business Registration Online, by completing the RC1 form, or through a representative with a valid authorization. The BN is also used for payroll, import/export, and corporate income tax accounts. Provincial corporate registration may be required separately under provincial business corporations acts; the federal BN is the principal tax identifier for federal GST/HST purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation – a business corporation resident in Canada is taxed at the corporate level under the Income Tax Act; profits distributed as dividends are generally deductible to the paying corporation and taxed in the hands of shareholders subject to the dividend gross-up and tax credit mechanism. Non-resident corporations are taxable on Canadian-source income and, in certain circumstances, may elect under section 216 to pay tax on rental income from Canadian real property or timber royalties as if resident, subject to Part XIII withholding tax analysis. No pass-through election is available for the federal corporate income tax.

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a 'check-the-box' entity classification election akin to the United States. However, the Income Tax Act provides specific elections relevant to corporate groups and hybrid entities: (a) a Chapter 6 election under subsection 89(11) to deem a U.S. LLC to be a tax-indigent corporation in certain circumstances; (b) an election under subsection 34.1(5) governing shared-use revenue streams; (c) partnership and joint venture elections for deemed taxable Canadian property; and (d) GST/HST elections, including the closely related entities election under the Excise Tax Act, which allows related corporations to net taxable supplies for GST/HST liability purposes. Hybrid mismatch rules (Sections 18.4-18.6 ITA) also apply to limit deduction/financing mismatches. There is no free-form entity classification election; the statutory classification is determined by the governing corporate law and the residence attributes in the Income Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

A non-resident supplier carrying on business in Canada who is a 'small supplier' is not required to register for GST/HST until its gross revenue from worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) and those of its associates exceeds C$30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Once exceeding threshold, registration is mandatory effective on the date the threshold is exceeded. For a non-resident that only makes taxable supplies in Canada, the CRA may nevertheless require registration if the supplies are made in the ordinary course of a business carried on in Canada (physical presence or digital nexus factors). A GST/HST registration creates an obligation to charge and remit GST/HST on supplies made in Canada and permits input tax credit recovery on eligible Canadian expenses.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A taxable supply made by a GST/HST registrant is zero-rated when the property is delivered or exported to a recipient outside Canada for consumption outside Canada. Specifically: (a) goods exported from Canada by the supplier, or by the recipient for export, within prescribed time limits (generally within 60 days of delivery), qualify for zero-rating; (b) services are zero-rated when performed on real or tangible personal property situated outside Canada at the time of performance, or when supplied to a non-resident person who is not registered for GST/HST, and the services are not in respect of Canadian real property or tangible personal property that is supplied in Canada; (c) intermodal ISO container leasing to a non-resident lessee may qualify for zero-rating if the supply is made to a non-resident individual or entity not registered under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act and does not otherwise become situated in Canada for use or consumption. The supplier must retain evidence establishing the export/foreign use nexus. The zero-rating cannot be claimed on supplies to Canadian residents unless the goods/containers are actually exported within a demonstratesed export period (requiring export documents).

  • Customs Export Declaration – Form B13A (Canada Border Services Agency e-manifest data) or equivalent automated export declaration
  • Bill of Lading or through-air waybill showing the goods/containers were consigned to a foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire/swift transfer evidence demonstrating the payment from the foreign lessee/customer (for FX repatriation and evidence of the cross-border nature of the revenue)

Canada does not operate a real-time e-invoicing clearance model or fiscal digital stamping regime comparable to Mexico or Italy. CRA establishes electronic filing mandates for GST/HST returns (mandatory e-filing for businesses with taxable supplies threshold over C$1.5 million annually, and for all GST/HST registrants by July 2024 for certain periods). Electronic payment of assessed amounts is required for amounts over C$10,000 via online banking or remittance voucher. E-invoicing is not a tax clearance precondition; however, keeping accurate electronic billing records in a standard format (e.g., EDI, xml) is required. CRA accepts electronic invoices as evidence for input tax credit claims provided the supplier and purchaser are Canadian GST registrants, the invoice captures the 8 mandatory disclosure elements under section 3 of the Input Tax Credit Information (GST/HST) Regulations, and the recipient maintains retrievable digital records. There is no pre-approval or real-time stamping obligation.

CRA and CBSA jointly audit export zero-rating claims. If an exporter cannot substantiate the export within the prescribed documentary requirements, the zero-rated supply is reassessed to taxable at 5% GST/HST, and the registrant is assessed interest under the Excise Tax Act plus late remittance penalties (typically 1% to 6% of the unreported net tax). False or fraudulent zero-rating claims or deliberate misrepresentation in export documents expose the exporter to penalties under section 281 of ETA (amount equal to gross negligence: up to the greater of 50% of the tax evaded or C$1,000) and under section 239 of Income Tax Act if income tax filings are involved; potential criminal prosecution carries fines up to C$25,000 (summary conviction) or C$200,000 (indictable) and imprisonment up to five years. Additionally, holding the zero-rated gains offshore without repatriation within the available statutory timeframe may trigger currency control concerns under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) reporting obligations, but no general federal fx repatriation timeframe applies beyond the Income Tax Act's 'filing basis' recognition rules. For goods exported but not actually removed from Canada, retrospective clawback applies with reassessment window 4 years ordinary, unlimited in cases of misrepresentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely floating, fully convertible currency with an open capital account. Current-account flows (ISO container lease rentals, dividends, interest, royalties, management fees) and capital-account movements (equity injections, shareholder loans, principal repatriation, portfolio investment) are unrestricted and require no Bank of Canada or Ministry of Finance exchange license. Profit remittance is hindered only by ordinary fiscal measures: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (25% statutory, typically reduced to 5%–15% under treaty, e.g., the Canada–US Tax Convention) and Canadian transfer-pricing documentation for intercompany lease recharges.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no statutory deadline, no surrender obligation, and no mandatory conversion ratio for export or lease rental proceeds. USD/EUR/CNH receivables may be held offshore or onshore in foreign-currency bank accounts at the treasury's discretion. For Canadian income tax purposes, unrealized foreign-exchange gains/losses on qualifying short-term foreign-currency obligations (ISA-denominated debt, term ≤1 year) are marked to market annually; longer-term lease receivables follow realization. The treasury should maintain separate CAD and USD ledgers to support CRA reporting.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; however, Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control institution. Cross-border financial supervision is distributed among FINTRAC (AML/CFT transaction reporting), Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) (customs and trade data), and the Minister of Innovation, Science and Economic Development (Investment Canada Act screening for FDI).

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15.0% federal general corporate income tax rate (plus provincial/territorial corporate tax rates ranging from 0% to 16%),

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid to non-residents under subsection 212(2), subject to treaty reduction (e.g., 15% for portfolio shareholders under Canada-US/UK/China treaties, reduced to 5% for a corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% voting control).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 return must be filed within 6 months after the end of the taxation year. The total tax balance must be paid within 2 months after the fiscal year-end for most corporations (3 months for eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations).

Late or deficient instalment payments accrue daily compound interest at CRA prescribed rates; failure to e-file a required T2 may trigger a separate penalty of C$250 to C$2,500.
Line 210 - Net Income Before Taxes Net income determined under Canadian financial accounting principles before income tax expense, after deducting operating expenses, depreciation, financing costs, and administrative expenses attributable to the Canadian operations. Non-resident corporations must maintain separate books and records for the Canadian PE. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 Information Return is mandatory when the aggregate value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions with non-residents exceeds C$1,000,000 in a taxation year. CRA transfer pricing practice expects a Master File and Local File where a taxpayer's cross-border related-party transaction exceeds C$5,000,000 and gross revenue exceeds C$50,000,000; contemporaneous supporting documentation must be prepared before the filing deadline to preserve penalty relief.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate ESR (Economic Substance Regulations) regime; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under Section 245 of the Income Tax Act requires economic substance, a bona fide business purpose, and a tax benefit not frustrate the Act. For a container leasing entity, CRA will scrutinize Canadian PE presence, hull/box asset location, employees, decision-making, and risk management to determine whether the corporation is genuinely carrying on business in Canada or is a conduit without substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Criminal)

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239: upon summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, up to 5 years. Civil gross negligence penalty under s.163.2 is 50% of the re-assessed tax deficiency plus daily interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for unremitted employee source deductions and other withholdings; when fraud is present, personal liability for civil restitution and criminal fines attaches. Under asset flight concerns, Canadian courts may grant travel bans, asset freezing orders, and extradition processes.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA can recharacterize transactions, deny treaty benefits under GAAR and the later-in-time 'principal purpose test', reassess at the full 25% Part XIII withholding rate, assess gross negligence penalties of up to 50%, and impose Part I tax on deemed Canadian-source business income.

Director personal liability: Where the corporate veil is pierced under CRA's anti-avoidance framework, directors and incorporators may be personally assessed for penalties and withheld taxes; directors may also face charges of conspiracy to evade tax, civil restitution, and prohibition orders restricting travel or international banking.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Subsection 247(3) penalty: 10% of the net understatement of income resulting from transfer pricing adjustments, unless the taxpayer demonstrates reasonable efforts and complete documentation. Late-filed T106 incurs a penalty of C$100 per day up to C$2,500; interest accumulates on any additional tax assessed.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for transfer pricing penalties, but if they sign false financial statements or are complicit in documentation fraud, they may face personal joint liability under the Income Tax Act and Criminal Code sanctions for fraud. Directors may also be barred from travel if criminal charges are laid.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / Financial Transaction Reporting

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties for large corporations up to C$500,000 per violation; criminal penalties on conviction up to C$2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years. FINTRAC also issues compliance orders and can suspend or revoke MSB registration.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for failing to implement an AML compliance program, including failure to appoint a compliance officer; directors may be prosecuted as principals for authorizing unlawful transactions, face restitution orders, receiving travel bans pending trial, and imprisonment in egregious cases.
