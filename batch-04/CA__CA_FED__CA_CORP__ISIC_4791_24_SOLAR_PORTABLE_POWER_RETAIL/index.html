Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - federal tax administrator for corporate income tax, GST/HST, excise taxes, and payroll; primary digital portals: CRA My Business Account (Mon Dossier d'entreprise), Business Registration Online (BRO), GST/HST NETFILE/CLIENT, and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Canadian International Trade Single Window (CITSW) for exports and customs declarations.

E-government filing portal

Stepwise procedure for a foreign-controlled or non-resident online retail corporation: (1) Determine whether the corporation is 'carrying on business in Canada' under ITA s. 253; active solicitation of Canadian e-commerce orders, maintenance of Canadian inventory or fulfilment, or employment of Canadian staff creates Canadian tax registration exposure. (2) Obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number'; the BN is the master identifier for all CRA program accounts and replaces a separate TIN. (3) Open required program accounts under the BN: corporate income tax (RC), GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP) if Canadian employees exist, and customs/export (RM) if goods are imported or exported. (4) Register for GST/HST either mandatorily when taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold (ETA s. 148) or voluntarily to recover input tax credits on Canadian warehousing, logistics, marketing, and other costs; a non-resident corporation soliciting Canadian orders must register if it makes taxable supplies in Canada. (5) Submit corporate existence certificates, charter documents, and director identification from the home jurisdiction, and appoint a Canadian representative for service of process and CRA notices if no Canadian address is maintained. (6) Execute registration electronically via BRO or by mail through the CRA's centralized non-resident registration unit; online BN issuance is immediate, and GST/HST account activation is same-day for online filers.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation - A Canadian Federal Business Corporation (Inc. / Corp., CBCA or provincial equivalent) is a separate taxable legal person under Part I of the Income Tax Act; no pass-through or check-the-box election is available. The corporation pays federal corporate income tax at a general rate of 15% (38% basic rate less 10% provincial abatement less 13% general rate reduction), with a reduced 9% federal rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income of a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC); shareholders are taxed again on dividends under the integration system. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - A Canadian Federal Business Corporation (Inc. / Corp., CBCA or provincial equivalent) is a separate taxable legal person under Part I of the Income Tax Act; no pass-through or check-the-box election is available. The corporation pays federal corporate income tax at a general rate of 15% (38% basic rate less 10% provincial abatement less 13% general rate reduction), with a reduced 9% federal rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income of a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC); shareholders are taxed again on dividends under the integration system. Territorial scope: a Canadian-incorporated corporation is resident in Canada and taxed on worldwide income, with foreign tax credits for source-country taxes; the federal jurisdiction is not a territorial or tax-exempt-zone regime and no federal tax-exempt zone applies to this e-commerce retail business.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification; foreign entity classification is determined under Canadian domestic law and CRA administrative positions (e.g., a US LLC that is fiscally transparent in the US is generally treated as a corporation for Canadian tax purposes). Available statutory elections and mechanisms relevant to this cross-border retail corporation include: (a) ITA s. 216 election for a non-resident to file on a net-income basis for Canadian rental or timber income instead of the 25% gross Part XIII withholding; (b) ITA s. 219(3) election increasing Part XIV branch tax liability to obtain a refundable tax credit and eliminate double taxation; (c) corporate reorganization elections: continuance under CBCA s. 187, ITA s. 85 asset rollover on incorporation, s. 85.1 share-for-share exchange, s. 86/51 capital reorganization, and s. 88 amalgamation or winding-up rollovers; (d) Capital Dividend Account (CDA) elections for tax-free surplus distributions to Canadian shareholders; (e) GST/HST elections: ETA s. 156 closely-related-corporations 28% election, Quick Method election under ETA s. 230.1 for simplified net tax, and the small-supplier or net-tax simplification elections; (f) hybrid mismatch rules introduced by Part VI.1 ITA (S.C. 2021, c. 10) which, rather than permitting a positive election, deny deductions on hybrid mismatch arrangements but include a taxpayer-favourable deduction-capture operation in ITA s. 18.4(6) and related provisions; and (g) transfer-pricing Advance Pricing Arrangements under ITA s. 247. Canadian law does not recognize US Form 8832 check-the-box elections as dispositive for federal tax classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - the 'small supplier' threshold under ETA s. 148; a person becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant at the earlier of the calendar month or fiscal quarter once worldwide taxable supplies (including Canadian e-commerce sales and zero-rated export sales) exceed CAD 30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters. Registrants below the threshold may voluntarily register; a non-resident supplier with a Canadian establishment or actively soliciting Canadian online orders is generally required to register once Canadian taxable supplies exceed the threshold. The threshold is measured on worldwide taxable supplies of the business, not Canada-only revenue, and zero-rated exports count as taxable supplies for threshold purposes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Schedule VI, Part V, item 1 of the Excise Tax Act to supplies of tangible personal property (portable battery generators, solar panels, inverters, and related accessories) that are exported from Canada. The supplier must ship or deliver the goods to a destination outside Canada within 60 days of delivery to the carrier (or within two years where the goods are manufactured to the order of a non-resident and are exported as soon as practically possible); ownership may pass in Canada, but possession must transfer to the foreign purchaser outside Canada. The goods must be acquired for consumption or use exclusively outside Canada. Services provided in direct respect of exported goods, and services supplied to non-residents (e.g., remote warranty, software support, or design services), may be zero-rated under Part V, item 7. For e-commerce, export-only sales shipped from Canadian inventory to foreign customers are not charged GST/HST; where zero-rating is correctly applied, no refund application is necessary, and input tax credits on the related inventory, freight, and Canadian operating costs are recoverable on the registrant's regular GST/HST return. Substantiation must meet CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17.2 ('Proof of Export') standards using direct evidence (carrier documentation, CBSA export declaration, foreign customs entry) and supporting commercial evidence.

  • Canada Export Declaration (B13A) or CBSA export reporting through the Canadian International Trade Single Window (CITSW) / eManifest, required for goods valued above CAD 2,500 unless exempted by destination or low-value provisions
  • Commercial invoice and packing list evidencing seller, purchaser, goods description, quantity, unit value, total consideration, HS classification, and Incoterms
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) / Air Waybill (air) / CBSA Cargo Control Document (truck or rail) issued by the carrier and evidencing that the goods physically departed Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent banking / payment service provider settlement record (SWIFT, foreign-currency exchange receipt, Stripe payout statement) proving receipt of export proceeds in foreign currency in the Canadian corporate bank account
  • CBSA export transaction records, eManifest confirmation, foreign customs entry documentation, and, where applicable, purchase order, insurance certificate, and CUSMA (formerly NAFTA) certificate of origin for preferential tariff claims

Canada currently has no mandatory continuous transaction controls (CTC), no real-time fiscal clearance API, and no digital/QR stamping for GST/HST invoices. Electronic filing is mandatory for GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million, and electronic remittance is mandatory above CAD 50 million; all registrants must issue tax invoices containing the prescribed elements under ETA s. 221 and s. 223 (supplier name and Business Number, purchaser, date, description, quantity, consideration, rate and amount of tax) and retain digital books and records for six years in an electronically accessible format. CRA introduced penalties in 2024 targeting electronic sales-suppression software that manipulates point-of-sale records. For cross-border e-commerce, CBSA requires electronic export and import declaration data through the Canadian International Trade Single Window (CITSW) and eManifest processes; no federal digital stamping, XML clearance gateway, or pre-clearance invoice approval API applies to this retail business.

CRA maintains specialized cross-border audit programs targeting exporter zero-rating claims; audit focus includes missing or fabricated export evidence, shipments to straw foreign consignees, overvaluation of export goods, and related-party offshore invoicing. Where the required proof of export is not produced, CRA reassesses the supply at the regular 5% GST (or applicable HST rate), denies related input tax credits, and charges arrears interest. Penalties include: ETA s. 280 late-filing/late-remittance penalty of 5% of net tax plus 1% compounded per month (up to 12 months); ETA s. 285.1 false-statement penalty of the greater of CAD 5,000 and 10% of the tax at issue for knowing or grossly negligent misstatements, with enhanced repeat-offender amounts; ITA s. 163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the federal tax understated; ITA s. 247(3) transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment amount where the adjustment exceeds the statutory threshold; and criminal prosecution under ETA s. 327 and s. 328 and ITA s. 239 for wilful evasion, with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. Retrospective clawback follows the statutory limitation windows: CRA may reassess within four years of the filing due date, extended to seven years where a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default is established, and without time limit for fraud. CRA may also cancel or vacate a GST/HST registration with retroactive effect where a non-resident's Canadian business presence has ceased or the registration was obtained fraudulently. There is no comprehensive statutory 180-day export-proceeds repatriation rule for arm's-length sales, but the 180-day benchmark aligns with ITA s. 17 interest-imputation practice for amounts receivable from non-residents and CRA's transfer-pricing documentation expectations for outstanding intercompany balances.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility of the Canadian dollar for both capital and current account transactions. Canada imposes no general foreign-exchange controls, no prior transfer approvals, and no statutory restrictions on dividend, interest, or principal remittances. Profit repatriation is subject only to source-level withholding taxes under the Income Tax Act (Canada), which are addressed separately in the tax compliance pipeline. Banks are required to report cross-border transfers of CAD 10,000 or more under FINTRAC obligations, but this does not constitute exchange control.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada has no export-proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion requirement. Eligible exporters may receive, retain, and use foreign currency proceeds freely through authorized Canadian financial institutions, with settlement at market rates.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank oversight; foreign exchange policy administered under the Currency Act and Canada's flexible exchange-rate framework.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium, but with meaningful constraints. Fully digital and offshore EMI/non-bank payment institutions cannot directly provide Canadian-dollar clearing, LVTS access, or deposit-taking functions under the Bank Act. Offshore EMIs can only facilitate cross-border payments by partnering with a licensed Canadian Schedule I or II bank as a settlement sponsor. The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) will require Canadian payment service providers to register with the Bank of Canada by 2024–2025, thus adding compliance requirements for an EMI-based treasury route. Domestic fintechs such as Wealthsimple, Koho, or lender-led BNPL platforms can be used for merchant payment acceptance, but core corporate treasury and FX settlement should be maintained through a Tier 1 licensed bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions (equity subscription or shareholder loan) must be transmitted via SWIFT MT103 from the remitting institution with a clear instruction in Field 70 (Remittance Information) indicating 'Capital contribution under the Canada Business Corporations Act' or 'Shareholder loan'. Canada does not require a specific central-bank purpose code; however, the receiving bank will apply a standard Payment Notification (PNT003) transaction nature code. For equity, use 'Equity' or 'Capital increase'; for intercompany debt, use 'Financial transaction – Related company loan'. A corporate resolution authorizing the transfer and the latest audited interim financials should be pre-submitted to the receiving bank. Funds are credited via LVTS final settlement and must be held in a designated operational or escrow account pending the issuance of shares or the execution of a shareholder loan agreement.Outbound trade settlements to suppliers should be executed via SWIFT MT103 for direct wire payments, or via Commercial Letter of Credit (MT700/MT707) for order-based financing. Canadian-dollar disbursements to domestic vendors use Lynx (LVTS) for high-value real-time settlement; low-value ACH via Payments Canada's Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS). Common trade clearing rails include SWIFT for USD/EUR/CNY settlements, with CAD-cleared trades via LVTS member banks. Bank reference data will require the beneficiary's IBAN or BIC/SWIFT and account number; for Chinese suppliers, the CNAPS code and bank local branch address are mandatory.Vanilla FX forward contracts up to 24 months for hedging committed CAD/USD or CAD/EUR trade flows.FX swap (swap of principal with offsetting forward leg) for short-term working-capital conversion between CAD and foreign-currency account balances.Cross-currency swap for longer-term borrowing and equity-debt structure to reduce currency risk on intercompany loans.Non-deliverable forward (NDF) for receivable or payable exposure to non-fully-convertible currencies (e.g., CNY, INR, BRL) when onshore deliverable forwards are prohibited.FX options (purchased, not written) to hedge uncertain future cash flows, provided the option is documented as an enterprise-level risk hedge and not for speculative trading.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium, but with meaningful constraints. Fully digital and offshore EMI/non-bank payment institutions cannot directly provide Canadian-dollar clearing, LVTS access, or deposit-taking functions under the Bank Act. Offshore EMIs can only facilitate cross-border payments by partnering with a licensed Canadian Schedule I or II bank as a settlement sponsor. The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) will require Canadian payment service providers to register with the Bank of Canada by 2024–2025, thus adding compliance requirements for an EMI-based treasury route. Domestic fintechs such as Wealthsimple, Koho, or lender-led BNPL platforms can be used for merchant payment acceptance, but core corporate treasury and FX settlement should be maintained through a Tier 1 licensed bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate rate; 9% federal small business rate for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPC) on active business income up to CAD 500,000 per year. Provincial corporate income tax is assessed separately by the applicable provincial jurisdiction and is outside this federal dossier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder; subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty. Under the Canada-US treaty, the general rate is 15% and the rate is 5% for a shareholder owning at least 10% of the voting stock.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No de minimis threshold applies for contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation under Income Tax Act s.247: CRA requires documentation for every cross-border non-arm's-length transaction regardless of amount. The Country-by-Country Report is mandatory only for Canadian ultimate parent entities with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000. Local File and Master File thresholds are not separately codified in the ITA, but CRA applies OECD-aligned expectations and may impose s.247 penalties where documentation is absent.

Economic substance scope: No UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations apply at the federal level. Canada applies the general anti-avoidance rule under ITA s.245, the factual comparability requirement within the arm's-length principle, and the corporate-residence test. For direct online retail, the Canadian entity must evidence real employees, inventory risk, marketing decision-making, and revenue-generating activity in Canada to avoid recharacterization as a sham, conduit, or GAAR-avoidance structure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and wilful omission of revenue

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under ITA s.163(2) of 50% of the understated tax plus interest. Criminal conviction under ITA s.239(1) carries a fine between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to two years on summary conviction or up to five years on indictment; separate GST/HST penalties apply under the Excise Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted source deductions and non-resident withholding taxes. Where a director participated in the evasion, criminal liability, restitution orders, seizure of personal assets, and travel restrictions through bail conditions or a penal sentence may apply.
Transfer pricing manipulation, thin capitalization, and conduit financing

Statutory sanction: CRA may recompute arm's-length amounts and assess additional tax plus arrears interest. A penalty of 10% of the gross transfer-pricing adjustment applies under ITA s.247(3) where the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts and provide contemporaneous documentation. Excessive interest deductions may be disallowed under interest-stripping and thin-capitalization rules, and GAAR may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign inaccurate intercompany agreements or approve non-arm's-length payments may be assessed under director-liability provisions for unremitted withholding tax. In egregious cases, courts may pierce the corporate veil where a related affiliate is an agent or sham used to conceal taxable profits, exposing directors to civil restitution and seizure of personal Canadian assets.
Failure to withhold and remit Part XIII non-resident tax on distributions

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the unremitted tax plus daily compounding interest and penalties under ITA s.227(8) and s.227(9). For gross negligence, a penalty of 50% of the unremitted tax applies. Repeated failures may lead to criminal charges and fines equal to the amounts withheld plus imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the full amount of unremitted Part XIII withholding under ITA s.227.1 unless they exercised due diligence. CRA may issue a director liability assessment and enforce through lien, garnishment of personal bank accounts, and, in serious criminal proceedings, restrictions on cross-border travel.
AML/CFT compliance and payment-processing irregularities

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC may impose administrative monetary penalties for non-compliance. Criminal sanctions include fines and imprisonment where a person knowingly or recklessly contravenes reporting requirements, provides false information, or acts as an unregistered money services business if required to register.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be charged personally for directing or counselling financial transactions in violation of Canadian AML/CFT law. Convictions can trigger arrest warrants, travel restrictions through bail or pre-trial release conditions, confiscation of assets, and imprisonment.
