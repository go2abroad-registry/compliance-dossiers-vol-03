Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); corporate income tax and GST/HST administered through the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) portal, My Business Account (MyBA), and GST/HST NETFILE / EDI electronic filing systems.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident or foreign-incorporated entity must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and GST/HST registration account before commencing taxable commercial activity in Canada. The standard procedure is: (1) apply via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or file Form RC1, Request for a Business Number; (2) provide the entity's full legal name, country and jurisdiction of incorporation, registered office address, director/officer particulars, and a Canadian contact person or authorized representative (mandatory for non-resident registrants); (3) select the required program accounts, specifically the GST/HST account, corporate income tax account, and, if applicable, payroll and import/export accounts; (4) declare the small-supplier threshold status based on worldwide taxable supplies; and (5) receive the CRA confirmation of BN and GST/HST registration, typically issued within 1 to 5 business days for online applications. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must also be familiar with the permanent establishment concept under the Canada-US and other income tax treaties for branch tax and reporting obligations.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian business corporation is a per se taxable entity under the Income Tax Act (Canada). Resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income at graduated federal corporate rates (generally 15% base rate after the 10% provincial abatement, with a reduced 10.5% small business rate available on qualifying active business income of a Canadian-controlled private corporation up to the annual business limit). Non-resident corporations are taxable only on Canadian-source income, subject to treaty relief and the 25% branch tax under ITA s. 219 unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian business corporation is a per se taxable entity under the Income Tax Act (Canada). Resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income at graduated federal corporate rates (generally 15% base rate after the 10% provincial abatement, with a reduced 10.5% small business rate available on qualifying active business income of a Canadian-controlled private corporation up to the annual business limit). Non-resident corporations are taxable only on Canadian-source income, subject to treaty relief and the 25% branch tax under ITA s. 219 unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty. No pass-through check-the-box classification is available at the federal level.

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a check-the-box regime equivalent to the United States; federally incorporated and provincially incorporated companies are corporations per se. Relevant statutory elections and ordering mechanisms include: (1) the ITA s. 216(1) election permitting a non-resident corporation earning Canadian rental or timber royalties to compute Canadian tax on net income rather than gross basis; (2) joint elections among associated Canadian-controlled private corporations to allocate the small business deduction limit (Form T2013); (3) the section 219 branch-tax planning and deemed-dividend elections in respect of non-resident corporations; and (4) Canada's BEPS Action 2 hybrid mismatch rules (effective from 2020-2022) that operate by law to deny deductions or include income in respect of hybrid instruments, hybrid entities, and imported mismatches; such rules are not elective check-the-box mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or across the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters; non-resident suppliers of digital goods and services to Canadian consumers are subject to the same CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, with voluntary registration permitted below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act (Canada) where the supplier exports the goods as part of the supply. Services such as post-production, subtitle transcription, AI voice cloning, and audio dubbing supplied to a non-resident client qualify for zero-rating under Schedule VI, Part VI if the supply is made to a non-resident person, the service is not performed in Canada on tangible personal property that remains in Canada, and the service is not in connection with real property located in Canada. The supplier must maintain contemporaneous evidence that the recipient is a non-resident and, where applicable, not registered for GST/HST, and that the service or product is delivered and used outside Canada.

  • CBSA G13A Export Declaration or ACI eManifest export filing where a physical export declaration is required
  • Bill of Lading, Airway Bill, or freight forwarder cargo receipt evidencing physical export from Canada
  • Commercial invoice explicitly stating 0% GST/HST and referencing the applicable ETA Schedule VI zero-rating provision
  • Signed service contract or purchase order from the non-resident client for the post-production/subtitling/dubbing services
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or bank wire transfer receipt evidencing receipt of foreign currency from the non-resident party
  • Internal accounting records, intercompany agreements, and subcontractor documentation substantiating the offshore supply chain and absence of Canadian consumption
  • Proof of delivery of the digital master files or finished subtitle/AI voice assets to the non-resident recipient outside Canada

Canada imposes no national real-time electronic invoice clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) regime at the federal level. GST/HST registrants must issue and retain valid sales invoices and supporting records for six years, and most registrants are required to file GST/HST returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE, EDI, or certified third-party software, with penalties for non-electronic filing. The CRA uses enhanced data analytics and e-commerce outreach programs to cross-match payment rails and digital platform data, but it does not mandate pre-clearance of invoices or API-based fiscal stamping.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated export within the normal four-year statutory limitation period, extended to eight years where a taxpayer has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or fraud, and may retroactively claw back the 0% GST/HST treatment, reinstate tax, plus interest at the prescribed daily compounded rate. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include the s. 280 penalty for false statements or evasion (up to CAD 25,000 per false statement), the s. 285.1 gross-negligence penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the additional tax payable, and criminal prosecution with fines and imprisonment for fraudulent export zero-rating. On the income tax side, CRA may impose a gross-negligence penalty under ITA s. 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax and apply the extended 8-year reassessment window for aggressive or unsupported export arrangements.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada generally imposes no exchange-control approval, mandatory conversion, capital-import registration, or foreign-exchange surrender requirement for ordinary corporate receipts, dividends, service fees, loan proceeds, or repatriation of investment capital. Practical restrictions arise from bank sanctions screening, beneficial-ownership verification, source-of-funds review, tax withholding, transfer-pricing requirements, non-resident reporting, corporate-law solvency requirements for distributions, contractual lender covenants, and restrictions involving sanctioned or high-risk jurisdictions. Dividends, interest, royalties, and certain management or service fees paid to non-residents may require Canadian withholding tax unless reduced or eliminated under an applicable tax treaty and supported by appropriate residency documentation. Cross-border related-party financing and service arrangements must be supportable under Canadian transfer-pricing rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory requirement for exporters to surrender foreign-currency proceeds to a Canadian bank, convert proceeds within a prescribed period, or sell a mandatory percentage of export receipts to the central bank. Export receipts may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the corporation's discretion. Exporters must nevertheless comply with applicable customs reporting, sanctions, tax, anti-money-laundering, economic-statistics, and contractual requirements. Banks may request invoices, contracts, shipping or service evidence, beneficiary information, and a commercial rationale before processing or releasing funds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Foreign-exchange transactions are also subject to federal financial-sector, anti-money-laundering, sanctions, tax, customs, and payment-service rules administered or enforced by authorities including the Department of Finance Canada, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, the Canada Revenue Agency, the Canada Border Services Agency, and Global Affairs Canada.

Route shareholder equity through a Canadian operating or designated capital account using an authenticated bank-to-bank transfer, ordinarily SWIFT MT103 or the correspondent-bank equivalent.
