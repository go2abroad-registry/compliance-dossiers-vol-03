Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

Registration is effective from the earlier date that taxable supplies ceased to be below the threshold or the first taxable supply in Canada.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box election; a Limited Partnership is statutorily flow-through for income tax under ITA s. 96, but it is a separate 'person' and GST/HST registrant under the Excise Tax Act. Available statutory elections include: (i) partnership fiscal-period election under ITA s. 96(12) and s. 249.1(4) to use a non-calendar year-end; (ii) GST/HST quick method of accounting election under ETA s. 225; (iii) election to file annual GST/HST returns under ETA s. 228(2.3); and (iv) section 102(2) designation if all partners are Canadian residents (a 'Canadian partnership'). Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 12.2(1), 18.4–18.6) may disallow deductions or require income inclusions where a Canadian LP is viewed as transparent in Canada but opaque in the owner jurisdiction; no domestic election cures the mismatch except transitional grandfathering of pre-existing arrangements and, where applicable, treaty-based mutual agreement procedures.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (including the supplies of associated persons) over the preceding four calendar quarters; mandatory GST/HST registration applies once the supplier ceases to be a small supplier. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada and making taxable supplies before exceeding the threshold may register voluntarily; CRA may require security in an amount determined by the Minister (ETA s. 240) where the registrant is a non-resident.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of dental zirconia blocks is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1 of the Excise Tax Act where the manufacturer supplies the goods by way of sale and delivers them directly to a destination outside Canada or to a common carrier that transports them outside Canada. The supplier must retain documentary proof of actual export within a reasonable period and demonstrate that the goods were not acquired for consumption, use, or supply in Canada. CAD/CAM design and milling services performed in Canada and supplied to a non-resident person who is not registered for GST/HST may also be zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 when the service relates to tangible personal property delivered outside Canada. All zero-rating claims require the supplier to maintain freight and customs records evidencing foreign destination.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or Canadian Export Reporting System electronic filing)
  • CBSA-approved export manifest or carrier cargo manifest
  • Commercial invoice issued in the name of the Canadian exporting entity (ETA s. 223-compliant, indicating zero-rated supply)
  • Non-negotiable or negotiable Bill of Lading or multimodal waybill evidencing delivery to a foreign destination
  • Packing list and proof of loading at the Canadian port of exit
  • Freight forwarder or carrier certificate confirming actual shipment and foreign port of discharge
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank Swift/TT confirmation showing payment of foreign currency proceeds into the exporter's account

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, digital stamping, or a mandatory national e-invoicing clearance platform. GST/HST registrants must issue ETA-compliant invoices (ETA s. 223) containing the BN, date, description, and tax status; electronic invoices are permitted. Mandatory electronic filing applies to registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1.5 million, using CRA GST/HST NETFILE, EDI, or approved API channels. Voluntary e-invoicing adoption aligns with the Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework based on the Peppol PINT standard, but no statutory deadline for mandatory clearance exists.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated export where the documentary bundle is incomplete or where goods are diverted into the Canadian market. Reversal of zero-rating results in assessment of GST/HST at the applicable rate (5% federal GST; plus provincial HST components where applicable) on the full selling price, statutory interest under ETA s. 280, and penalties for false statements or omissions under ETA s. 280.1 (greater of CAD 250 and 5% of the net remittance error). Gross negligence or wilful participation in sham export schemes attracts a 50% penalty under ETA s. 285.1, potential prosecution under ETA s. 327, director/officer joint liability under ETA s. 323, mandatory security requirements, loss of efficient filing status, and retrospective clawback for up to the normal statutory reassessment period (four years), extended to seven years or more where misrepresentation or fraud is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is freely convertible on both current and capital accounts; Canada imposes no foreign-exchange controls under the Bank of Canada Act or the Currency Act and the CAD floats against all major currencies. Non-resident capital contributions, dividend-like distributions, and profit remittances are unrestricted, subject only to (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at 25% (reduced under an applicable tax treaty) on distributions of Canadian-source income to foreign limited partners, (ii) Part I tax on non-residents carrying on business in Canada, and (iii) FATCA/CRS reporting. No prior regulatory approval is required for inbound or outbound wires; banks report all international electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC as a pure reporting obligation, not an authorization mechanism.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory repatriation deadline. A Canadian LP may retain export receivables in USD/EUR offshore accounts, provided that (i) receivables are accrued in CAD for tax purposes using the Bank of Canada daily noon exchange rate, (ii) transfer-pricing documentation complies with subsection 247(4) ITA for intercompany sales, and (iii) any wire transfer of CAD 10,000 or more entering or leaving Canada is reported to FINTRAC as an EFT under the PCMLTFA.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential supervision remains with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for federally chartered banks, and AML/FINTRAC reporting obligations are supervised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). The Minister of Finance retains residual crisis powers under the Currency Act and the Emergency Economic Measures Act, but no peacetime exchange-control authority exists in Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Rating: MEDIUM as a payment overlay, LOW as a primary or escrow banking rail. Fintech EMIs in Canada (Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury) operate as money service businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA, not as Schedule I banks under the Bank Act; they cannot issue irrevocable commercial letters of credit (MT700), are not members of Payments Canada's Lynx high-value settlement system, and hold no CDIC-insured deposits. Legal constraints: (i) escrowing partner contributions with an uninsured MSB creates material counterparty and failure risk; (ii) MSB accounts are ineligible for the Canadian depositors' protection regime; (iii) the same FINTRAC EFT reporting threshold (CAD 10,000) applies. Recommended deployment: merchant collection of small cross-border dental-lab receivables with a daily automated sweep into a Tier-1 bank operating account; prohibited for capital escrow and CAPEX disbursement.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant SWIFT MT103 routing: (1) each partner executes the capital contribution agreement and instructs a SWIFT MT103 wire to the LP's CAD or USD account at the Tier-1 bank, with SWIFT Field 70 reading 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION — LIMITED PARTNERSHIP INTEREST' plus the LP registry number, and Field 72 naming the GP as beneficiary agent; Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Not applicable at the Canadian federal partnership level. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay federal corporate income tax on allocated partnership income. Canadian-controlled private corporations may qualify for a 9% federal small-business rate on eligible active business income, while other corporations generally apply a 15% federal general corporate rate, before provincial or territorial tax.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The general Canadian Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. Under the Canada-China income tax treaty, the rate is generally reduced to 10% where the beneficial owner is a company that owns at least 10% of the voting power of the Canadian payer, and 15% in other treaty-eligible cases. Treaty relief requires residence, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits or anti-treaty-shopping compliance, and other applicable conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general transaction-value threshold below which the arm's-length rule in section 247 of the Income Tax Act ceases to apply and no general statutory Local File/Master File threshold in the OECD terminology. Contemporaneous documentation is required where section 247 applies and must generally be provided to the CRA within three months of a written request. Form T106 reporting commonly applies when the total reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1 million, subject to detailed statutory rules. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone regime commonly called Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax outcomes are nevertheless constrained by the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership requirements, treaty anti-abuse rules, sham principles, debt-versus-equity doctrine, and source and permanent-establishment rules. Canadian operations should maintain real personnel, production assets, decision-making, inventory control, quality systems, customer contracts, risk assumption, and commercially substantiated functions. A limited partnership with no personnel, no operational control, circular funding, artificial royalty flows, or allocations disconnected from functions and risks is high risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, reassessment beyond ordinary periods in fraud or misrepresentation cases, interest, and repayment of benefits. Criminal tax evasion may result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors and managers may face criminal prosecution, personal liability for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST amounts, fines, restitution, and imprisonment. Corporate status does not protect deliberate fraud or personal participation in unlawful conduct.
Failure to File Partnership and Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties generally accrue at CAD 25 per day subject to applicable minimums and maximums for specified information returns. Failure to provide information, repeated non-compliance, and false statements may attract additional penalties, reassessment, interest, and loss of administrative relief.

Director personal liability: Responsible persons may become personally liable for trust amounts and source deductions where statutory director-liability conditions are met. Criminal liability may arise for knowingly false returns or obstruction.
Source-Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer can be assessed for the unremitted withholding tax, arrears interest, and penalties. A failure-to-deduct or failure-to-remit penalty may apply, generally up to 10% and potentially 20% for repeated failures or amounts knowingly withheld and not remitted.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where the statutory due-diligence defence is unavailable. Criminal prosecution is possible for wilful non-remittance.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may make a primary transfer-pricing adjustment, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny unsupported deductions, and charge interest. Secondary adjustments and treaty competent-authority procedures may also become relevant.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, shareholder claims, tax penalties, and criminal exposure where pricing is part of deliberate tax fraud. Transfer-pricing documentation does not cure an artificial transaction lacking commercial substance.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied under beneficial-ownership principles, the principal-purpose rule where applicable, the Canadian anti-treaty-shopping rule, or the general anti-avoidance rule. The payer may be assessed for the 25% statutory withholding shortfall plus interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and advisers may face civil liability, professional sanctions, and criminal tax-fraud exposure if treaty documentation is knowingly fabricated or used to conceal the true recipient.
Shell Company and Lack of Commercial Substance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may recharacterize transactions, deny deductions, reallocate income, apply section 245 anti-avoidance principles, deny treaty benefits, and impose interest and penalties. Related customs, GST/HST, and provincial tax adjustments may follow.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face civil restitution, director liability for statutory remittances, injunctions, asset-preservation orders, and criminal prosecution where the structure is used for wilful evasion or fraud.
AML, Beneficial Ownership, and Suspicious Transactions

Statutory sanction: Reporting entities subject to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may face administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, transaction restrictions, and criminal charges. Laundering proceeds of crime can result in imprisonment of up to ten years, with more severe treatment for indictable offences and terrorism financing.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners may face asset restraint, forfeiture, restitution, travel restrictions in appropriate criminal proceedings, fines, and imprisonment. Corporate non-liability does not protect persons who direct or participate in laundering.
Customs, Import, and Product-Regulatory Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include reassessed duties and GST, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure or forfeiture of goods, import privileges suspension, product recalls, compliance orders, and prosecution under customs or health-product legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face fines, prosecution, personal liability for knowing customs fraud, prohibition orders, and imprisonment for serious offences.
Prohibited Distributions and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Recipients may be required to repay unlawful distributions. Creditors, trustees, tax authorities, or courts may unwind transactions, impose interest, and pursue fraudulent-preference or conveyance remedies. Directors may face statutory corporate-law liability.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil restitution, oppression or creditor claims, director disqualification, asset tracing, and criminal liability for fraud or fraudulent conveyances. Travel restrictions may arise only where ordered in applicable insolvency or criminal proceedings; they are not an automatic tax penalty.
Employment, Payroll, and Worker Misclassification

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess payroll deductions, CPP and employment insurance amounts, interest, penalties, and taxable benefits. Provincial employment and workplace-safety liabilities may arise independently.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions where statutory conditions apply and may face civil penalties or prosecution for intentional payroll fraud.
