Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; online portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (SBS-ROE)

E-government filing portal

Upon federal incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act, Corporations Canada via ISED forwards registration data to the CRA, which automatically issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN). The corporate tax account (RC) is created automatically. To add GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), or import/export (RM) program accounts, the corporation or its authorized representative files Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or registers online through Business Registration Online / My Business Account. A foreign-owned Canadian corporation may nominate a registered agent in Canada for CRA correspondence; no minimum capital is required. A non-resident corporation without Canadian incorporation must apply for a BN via Form RC1 and the Non-Resident Business Registration process, providing certified constitutional documents and a Canadian representative Authorization Form RC59.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Canadian business corporation is a separate taxable person under Part I of the Income Tax Act (ITA). It is subject to federal corporate income tax at the entity level; shareholders are separately taxed on dividends and capital gains with the dividend tax credit providing integration relief. There is no pass-through or check-the-box regime for Canadian corporations, and no federal territorial tax election; the worldwide income of a federal corporation is taxable, with a credit for foreign-source taxes.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists. Specific elections available include: (i) ETA section 156 election for closely related corporations to treat intercorporate supplies as made for nil consideration (GST/HST), via Form GST25; (ii) associated-corporation elections for allocation of the small business deduction under ITA section 125; (iii) capital cost allowance and terminal loss elections on depreciable property; (iv) income-splitting and formal valuations elections; and (v) hybrid mismatch rules under ITA sections 18.4-18.6 that apply to deny deductions in certain branch and structured arrangement cases. GST/HST groups are available under ETA section 240.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in gross worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated exports) measured over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and in the current quarter; a non-resident that solicits business in Canada is deemed a non-small-supplier and must register regardless of the threshold. Registration is mandatory within 29 days of exceeding the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act (ETA), Schedule VI, Part V, a supply of services made to a non-resident person is zero-rated where the service is not rendered in connection with real property situated in Canada, is not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, and the non-resident is not a GST/HST registrant in Canada in respect of that service. Corporate secretarial and registered-agent services supplied to offshore corporate clients, including company formation, registered office, nominee directorship administration, minute book maintenance, and statutory filing support, normally qualify for zero-rating (0% GST/HST) when the recipient is non-Canadian and the benefit of the services is realized wholly outside Canada. The supplier must retain contemporaneous evidence of the non-resident status and foreign purpose of the supply.

  • Executed written service agreement with the offshore client describing the services and Canadian/non-Canadian benefit location
  • Commercial invoice clearly marked as zero-rated exported services with reference to ETA Schedule VI Part V
  • Proof of non-resident recipient status (foreign certificate of incorporation, tax identification number, overseas registered office address, passport or utility evidence for individual owners)
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank wire/payment records demonstrating international funds inflow to the Canadian entity
  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) and Bill of Lading / airway bill only where physical documentation packages or tangible corporate kits cross the border as goods ancillary to the service
  • Deliverables log or engagement letter evidencing work product provided to the non-resident and not consumed in Canada
  • Filed GST/HST return (Form GST34) and internal schedule reconciling zero-rated supplies reported at line 103

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal electronic clearance or digital stamping. The CRA accepts standard electronic invoices and requires GST/HST registrants with more than CAD 1.5 million in annual revenue, or those whose returns are prepared by a tax professional, to file returns electronically. A Pan-Canadian electronic invoice framework based on Peppol interoperability is being developed voluntarily; no API-based live fiscal validation is yet enforced.

The CRA may reassess within the normal 4-year period, which is extended to 10+ years for misrepresentation or fraud. Where zero-rated export claims are not substantiated, the supplies are recharacterized as taxable at 5% GST/HST plus prescribed interest and penalties. Under ETA subsection 285(1), a penalty of 15% of the amount erroneously claimed as an input tax credit or refund applies. Knowingly making a false statement (ETA subsection 286(1)) is an offense punishable by a fine of up to CAD 25,000 and/or imprisonment up to 2 years, while income tax evasion under ITA section 239 carries penalties of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. The CRA also applies structured transaction and sham transaction doctrines to clawback illegitimate export zero-rating and may deny future refunds under the significant unreported/overstated penalty regime (ETA section 280).

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility, having accepted Article VIII obligations of the International Monetary Fund. There is no general foreign-exchange control law, no central-bank approval requirement for inward or outward remittances, no exchange-rate rationing, and no restriction on profit repatriation, dividend remittance, interest, royalty, or management-fee payments. Dividend payments by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder are subject to Part XIII withholding tax of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, generally reduced to 5% to 15% under the Canada-U.S. tax treaty and other applicable bilateral income tax conventions), but the underlying foreign-exchange conversion and cross-border transfer are entirely free and unrestricted. The only substantive hurdles are documentary and reporting in nature, principally: finalization of beneficial-ownership and directors' registers, filing of the CBCA annual return, obtaining a T2 corporate tax clearance or attaining compliance status with the Canada Revenue Agency for the withholding remittance, and transaction reporting to FINTRAC where a transfer equals or exceeds CAD 10,000.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory mandate, prescribed timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for the surrender of export proceeds. Export receipts derived by a Canadian federal corporation from offshore secretarial and registered-agent services are freely receivable in any convertible currency, may be retained in foreign-currency accounts at Canadian or foreign banks without restriction, and are not subject to any forced conversion into Canadian dollars. The only compliance obligations are: (i) electronic-fund-transfer reporting to FINTRAC for single or linked transactions of CAD 10,000 or more conducted within a 24-hour period, filed on an approved EFT report through the FINTRAC reporting system; (ii) accurate income recognition under the Income Tax Act for services performed in Canada; and (iii) completion of Statistics Canada balance-of-payments and direct-investment surveys under the Statistics Act, which collect aggregate cross-border payments data but do not function as a surrender or licensing requirement.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) serves as the central bank and monetary authority; prudential supervision of federally regulated banks is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); foreign exchange policy and regulatory oversight are administered by the Department of Finance Canada and the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52); anti-money laundering and terrorist financing supervision is conducted by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Low feasibility for offshore unregulated digital EMIs. Canada does not recognise the European Union EMI passport regime, and a foreign electronic-money institution cannot lawfully open a merchant or treasury account in Canada solely on the strength of an overseas EMI licence. A non-resident fintech entity wishing to service the Canadian corporate-secretarial cash flows must either register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, or partner as a licensed payment-service provider with a Schedule I bank, in which case the underlying clearing remains within the Canadian banking system. An unlicensed or passport-only offshore EMI is therefore not a compliant stand-alone clearing rail for this CA_FED business corporation; the recommended structure is a primary operating account at a Schedule I bank with a business credit-card processor (e.g., Stripe, Moneris, or Paypal business) layered as a front-end collection rail and same-day sweep to the bank account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound routing is executed as a SWIFT MT103 corporate credit from the investing shareholder's foreign bank to the Canadian Schedule I bank account of the federal corporation. The remittance instruction must carry: field 50K (Ordering Customer) with the full legal name and address of the non-resident shareholder; field 59 (Beneficiary) with the exact CBCA legal name and federal incorporation number of the CA_CORP; field 70 (Remittance Information) stating 'EQUITY SUBSCRIPTION / SHARE CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' and citing the board resolution date; and field 32A (Amount) in CAD or USD. The Canadian receiving institution will verify the beneficial-ownership register, the source-of-funds declaration, and the corporate resolution; where the transaction equals or exceeds CAD 10,000, the bank is required to submit an Electronic Funds Transfer report to FINTRAC within the statutory time frame. There is no central-bank licensing or conversion requirement; however, to evidence equity rather than indebtedness for Canadian tax purposes, the corporation must issue the share certificate, file the updated CBCA return, and record the capital contribution in the company's minute book and financial statements before the funds are disbursed for operational purposes.Outbound disbursements for trade settlements and commercial expenses are cleared through: (i) SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfers for cross-border supplier payments and intra-group settlements; (ii) commercial documentary letters of credit governed by UCP600 for goods-based trade where the Canadian corporation acts as importer or confirming bank principal; (iii) domestic electronic funds transfer through Payments Canada, including the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for real-time final settlement in Canadian dollars and the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) for batch retail payments; and (iv) correspondent U.S. dollar clearing through CHIPS, Fedwire, or the Canadian banks' U.S. branches for USD disbursements. All outbound EFTs of CAD 10,000 or more require FINTRAC record keeping and reporting; outbound transfers to sanctioned persons or jurisdictions are prohibited under the Special Economic Measures Act and the Criminal Code money-laundering provisions. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low feasibility for offshore unregulated digital EMIs. Canada does not recognise the European Union EMI passport regime, and a foreign electronic-money institution cannot lawfully open a merchant or treasury account in Canada solely on the strength of an overseas EMI licence. A non-resident fintech entity wishing to service the Canadian corporate-secretarial cash flows must either register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, or partner as a licensed payment-service provider with a Schedule I bank, in which case the underlying clearing remains within the Canadian banking system. An unlicensed or passport-only offshore EMI is therefore not a compliant stand-alone clearing rail for this CA_FED business corporation; the recommended structure is a primary operating account at a Schedule I bank with a business credit-card processor (e.g., Stripe, Moneris, or Paypal business) layered as a front-end collection rail and same-day sweep to the bank account.
