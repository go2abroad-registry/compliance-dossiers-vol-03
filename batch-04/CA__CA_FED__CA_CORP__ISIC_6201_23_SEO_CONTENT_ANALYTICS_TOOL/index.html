Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - 555 Mackenzie Avenue, Ottawa, ON K1A 0L5; digital portal: CRA 'My Business Account' via canada.ca, Business Registration Online (BRO), and GST/HST NETFILE.

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincial equivalent and obtain Articles of Incorporation. 2) Register for the federal 9-digit Business Number (BN) using CRA Business Registration Online (BRO); resident corporations use Form RC150. 3) Non-resident corporations (including foreign SaaS vendors with Canadian nexus) must file Form RC4605 'Request for a Business Number - Non-Resident', attaching foreign registration certificates and identifying an authorized representative or Canadian agent. 4) Open program accounts: RT (corporate income tax), RC (GST/HST) - mandatory when the small-supplier threshold is met or upon voluntary election, RP (payroll) if employees in Canada, and RD (customs) if importing. 5) CRA issues a BN with a two-letter program-account suffix (e.g., RC0001 for GST/HST; the GST/HST registration number is formatted as 9-digit BN + RC + 4-digit account). 6) No minimum capital, notarization, or statutory license applies to a federally incorporated MarTech analytics corporation.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election; entity classification follows the CBCA and the Income Tax Act. Key elections and regimes: (i) associated-corporations allocation agreement (Schedule 23, T2) to split the CAD 500,000 CCPC business limit; (ii) s. 216.1 election for non-residents rendering services in Canada without carrying on a business; (iii) s. 219(2) branch-tax election (non-resident corporation electing increased branch tax in lieu of s. 212 dividend withholding); (iv) voluntary GST/HST registration election under s. 240 ETA to trigger ITC recovery pre-threshold; (v) s. 177 ETA Quick Method election for eligible small suppliers; (vi) hybrid mismatch treatment: CRA denies Canada-US treaty benefits to fiscally transparent US LLCs unless taxed as corporations in the US, so the Canadian payer must apply withholding under s. 215 and dual-consolidated loss rules under s. 250.01 ITA.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (small-supplier threshold) measured on total taxable worldwide supplies before GST/HST; for a non-resident digital supplier, the 'specified supplies' threshold is CAD 30,000 per calendar quarter and CAD 30,000 over the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters (specified supplies are digital services, intangibles, and goods supplied to Canadian recipients, deemed under s. 142.1 ETA). Registration becomes mandatory on the first supply once the threshold is exceeded; the ordinary small-supplier carve-out does not shield non-resident suppliers for specified supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Canadian GST/HST registrant exporting MarTech analytics as a service may zero-rate the digital supply under s. 7(1) (services to non-residents) and s. 10 (intangibles) of Part V, Schedule VI, ETA, provided the recipient is a non-resident and the supply is not made in Canada under the s. 142.1 place-of-supply rules (IP address, billing address, bank country). The Canadian supplier charges 0% GST and remains entitled to full input tax credits (ITCs) on expenses attributable to exports. Physical goods exports require export documentation under the ETA. If a distribution platform (e.g., Paddle) acts as merchant of record, the platform may collect GST/HST on Canadian sales; the supplier must nevertheless maintain zero-rating substantiation for all non-resident sales.

  • CBSA Canadian Export Declaration (formerly Form B13A) filed electronically through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) with acknowledgement
  • Commercial Invoice (tax invoice) identifying the non-resident buyer, description, currency, and export basis
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill for physical goods; service delivery or usage report for digital SaaS exports
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or payment-gateway settlement reports from Stripe/Paddle confirming receipt of export consideration
  • Signed contract / purchase order with foreign customer, including address and contact-jurisdiction evidence
  • IP address and geolocation logs demonstrating the electronic supply was made to a recipient outside Canada (consistent with CRA GST/HST Notice 319)

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or pre-clearance API regime exists in Canada. E-invoicing is voluntary in B2B; the federal procurement framework requires PEPPOL BIS Billing 3.0-compliant e-invoices for B2G supplies (phased mandate from 2024). CRA accepts returns and remittances via GST/HST NETFILE and supports API-based data exchange through 'My Business Account'; registrants must retain conventional invoice records, e-invoice XML, and payment-gateway settlement evidence (Stripe/Paddle) for six years. Quebec imposes a separate QST filing regime and specific e-records rules, but these do not apply at the federal jurisdiction level.

Canada has no statutory exchange-control repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark functions as CRA's commercial reasonableness reference for recognizing export receivables. CRA may reassess zero-rating claims within 4 years (7 years for misrepresentation, unlimited for fraud). Non-compliant exporters face: (i) civil penalty under s. 285 ETA of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax sought to be avoided; (ii) late-filing and remittance penalties under s. 280 ETA of 10%-20% of unreported net tax plus arrears interest at CRA quarterly prescribed rates; (iii) criminal tax-evasion prosecution under s. 327 ETA with fines of 50%-200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment; (iv) retrospective clawback of ITCs, deeming of GST collected, and denial of future export zero-rating until substantiation is rehabilitated; (v) parallel income-tax exposure under s. 163(2) ITA (50% gross-negligence penalty) and s. 239 ITA (evasion fines up to 200% and imprisonment).

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member with full current-account and capital-account convertibility; the CAD is a market-determined floating currency with no capital controls, no repatriation authorisation, and no quantitative limits on software-export subscription remittances, dividends, intercompany loans, share redemptions, or inbound equity. A non-resident shareholder can receive dividends and repatriation proceeds freely; the only statutory burden is Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (25% statutory, frequently reduced by treaty, e.g., 5% withholding on dividends to qualifying corporate U.S. shareholders under the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention), with the Canada Revenue Agency requiring the appropriate NR4/annual return, treaty relief application, or waiver. The Investment Canada Act review applies only to acquisition-of-control transactions or specified enterprise-value thresholds and is unrelated to FX conversion. The only operational frictions are FINTRAC threshold reporting and Form T1135 foreign-property disclosure when specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000 in cost amount.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no exporter surrender or conversion regime for export or SaaS service proceeds: there is no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to receive, hold, or convert export receipts through the Canadian banking system. Export revenue in USD/EUR/GBP may be retained offshore, converted at any time, or held in a foreign bank account; the corporation must still report worldwide taxable income to the Canada Revenue Agency and file Form T1135 when the cost amount of specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000. The only edge-triggered reporting arises under FINTRAC: physical cross-border movement of currency or monetary instruments at or above CAD 10,000, and financial-institution EFT reporting for cross-border electronic funds transfers at or above CAD 10,000. No exporter registration, corporate identity certificate, surrender certificate, or central-bank conversion ticket is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority and, since 1 November 2024, the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) oversight supervisor for retail payment service providers; the Department of Finance Canada owns the exchange-rate policy framework for a freely floating Canadian dollar; OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) prudentially regulates the Schedule I chartered banks that execute treasury and FX transactions; FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) administers AML/CFT reporting and cross-border currency/EFT disclosure under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Canada has no exchange-control licence or permit authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating of a non-resident or offshore digital EMI for the Canadian MarTech SaaS treasury is MEDIUM for an ancillary receivables and contractor-payout augmenter, and LOW as a primary clearing rail. Legal constraints: (1) an offshore EMI is not a Schedule I bank under the Bank Act and cannot take Canadian-dollar deposits or hold the principal operating cash position without infringing the Bank Act and OSFI licensing; (2) a payment-function provider serving Canadian end users must be registered or appropriately supervised under the Retail Payment Activities Act, and an unregistered offshore EMI leaves the corporation without a defensible payments-licence path; (3) funds held with an offshore EMI are not CDIC-insured and represent an unguarded cash-concentration and counterparty risk; (4) an EMI account maintained at a foreign institution is reportable on Form T1135, and cross-border EFTs above CAD 10,000 are transparent to FINTRAC; (5) Canadian Schedule I banks will underwriting-review the EMI KYC file, home-jurisdiction regulatory licence, audit trail, and settlement flow, and routing the main SaaS revenue through an offshore EMI before dividend distribution to non-resident shareholders will trigger enhanced due diligence. Optimal blueprint: use a Schedule I chartered bank as the reference treasury and clearing anchor; retain one registered global payout provider (e.g., Wise Platform or an equivalent with a valid Canadian PSP registration) only for small foreign contractor payouts and low-value disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or treasury funding is wired as a SWIFT MT103 message by the investor or parent to the Canadian capital-escrow or operational account; field 50K identifies the ordering customer and its bank, field 59 carries the beneficiary in Canadian transit-institution-account format (Canada does not use IBAN), and field 70 or the bank-required ISO 20022 purpose code states capital subscription for common shares or intercompany loan advance for working capital; no official Canadian exchange-control purpose-code matrix exists, but the bank AML system will classify the transaction and require pre-delivery of the signed board resolution authorising the share issuance or borrowing, the investor source-of-funds proof and financial statements, and the updated CBCA share register. Where the lender is a non-resident parent, the loan must be documented at arm's length with Part XIII withholding addressed for any interest; when the parent funds in USD but the operating entity runs in CAD, the bank either spot-converts or the treasury opens a USD sub-account and executes a targeted deliverable forward, recording translation gains or losses in equity or OCI. Concurrent compliance verification: CRA BN match of the receiving entity, automatic FINTRAC EFT reporting above CAD 10,000, no Investment Canada Act filing triggered for treasury capital, and updated Form T1135 for the new foreign receivable.Settlement rails: (1) cross-border payments to suppliers, contractors, or licensors in non-CAD currency run as SWIFT MT103 messages from the Canadian clearing bank to the beneficiary bank in its domestic RTGS, with the commercial purpose in field 70 matching the underlying invoice or intercompany statement; where an enterprise buyer requires bank security, the relevant credit instrument is a standby letter of credit or bank performance guarantee issued under SWIFT MT760; where the Canadian entity is a software reseller with physical deliverables, the bank may advise or issue a commercial letter of credit (SWIFT MT700) against the licence agreement and delivery acceptance, but pure subscription income continues to use the PSP settlement rail; an NDF is contractually permissible only where the underlying non-CAD currency is itself non-convertible or restricted (e.g., certain Asian or Latin American emerging-market currencies), and then only with a supervised Canadian bank counterparty under its credit approval.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating of a non-resident or offshore digital EMI for the Canadian MarTech SaaS treasury is MEDIUM for an ancillary receivables and contractor-payout augmenter, and LOW as a primary clearing rail. Legal constraints: (1) an offshore EMI is not a Schedule I bank under the Bank Act and cannot take Canadian-dollar deposits or hold the principal operating cash position without infringing the Bank Act and OSFI licensing; (2) a payment-function provider serving Canadian end users must be registered or appropriately supervised under the Retail Payment Activities Act, and an unregistered offshore EMI leaves the corporation without a defensible payments-licence path; (3) funds held with an offshore EMI are not CDIC-insured and represent an unguarded cash-concentration and counterparty risk; (4) an EMI account maintained at a foreign institution is reportable on Form T1135, and cross-border EFTs above CAD 10,000 are transparent to FINTRAC; (5) Canadian Schedule I banks will underwriting-review the EMI KYC file, home-jurisdiction regulatory licence, audit trail, and settlement flow, and routing the main SaaS revenue through an offshore EMI before dividend distribution to non-resident shareholders will trigger enhanced due diligence. Optimal blueprint: use a Schedule I chartered bank as the reference treasury and clearing anchor; retain one registered global payout provider (e.g., Wise Platform or an equivalent with a valid Canadian PSP registration) only for small foreign contractor payouts and low-value disbursements.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 50 million gross revenue (for Master File and Local File); also, transactions with non-residents exceeding CAD 1 million require documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) but applies General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and requires economic substance for treaty benefits. CRA may challenge transactions lacking commercial substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Penalty under Income Tax Act s.239: fine up to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, or both.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes under s.227.1 if they failed to withhold or remit; civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment possible.
Shell Company

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess, impose penalties under GAAR (s.245), and deny treaty benefits; fines up to 50% of tax avoided.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for penalties and taxes if they knowingly participated; potential criminal charges for fraud.
AML (Anti-Money Laundering)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2 million for corporations; criminal penalties for non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly failed to implement compliance measures; travel bans and imprisonment for serious violations.
