Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is required under the CBCA; a federal corporation may be incorporated with zero issued capital. Shares may be issued for any lawful consideration, and where subscription funds are received the full consideration must be deposited into a corporate bank account before the shares are issued. No statutory escrow account is imposed for a private business corporation; non-cash consideration must satisfy section 49 requirements and the directors' solvency and fairness duties under section 118.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and the CRA My Business Account portal. Federal corporate income tax administration is conducted by the CRA. Provincial and territorial tax authorities separately administer certain corporate, sales, payroll, and retail taxes, including Quebec corporate tax and Quebec Sales Tax.

E-government filing portal

A corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act generally obtains a Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The corporation must register for applicable CRA program accounts, including the corporate income tax account (RC), GST/HST account (RT), payroll deductions account (RP), and import-export account (RM), as applicable. Registration may be completed through Business Registration Online, by telephone, or through the federal incorporation process where available. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, earning income from Canadian sources, importing commercial goods, or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a BN and the applicable program accounts. The CRA may require incorporation documents, registered-office information, directors and officers, beneficial ownership information, business activity details, Canadian banking and contact information, and evidence supporting the existence and Canadian activities of the entity. A Canadian BN is generally a nine-digit identifier; program accounts append a two-letter account type and four-digit reference number. GST/HST registration is required when the entity is a person making taxable supplies in Canada and exceeds the small-supplier threshold, subject to special rules for non-residents, taxi and ride-sharing operators, and other prescribed activities. A non-resident may be required to provide security when registering for GST/HST if it does not have a permanent establishment in Canada and the CRA determines security is appropriate.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general U.S.-style check-the-box election allowing a corporation to elect partnership or disregarded-entity treatment. A corporation is generally a separate taxpayer and must file a T2 corporation income tax return. Partnerships are generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes, subject to applicable anti-avoidance and allocation rules. Available statutory elections may include section 85 rollovers for eligible transfers of property to a taxable Canadian corporation, section 86 share reorganizations, subsection 88(1) wind-ups, amalgamation rules, capital dividend elections, scientific research and experimental development elections, and elections under applicable tax treaties. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions, include amounts, or otherwise neutralize deduction/non-inclusion and double-deduction outcomes; they do not provide a general entity-classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies over the relevant single-calendar-quarter or rolling-four-consecutive-calendar-quarter test for most small suppliers. The threshold generally applies to taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, and excludes specified supplies such as financial services and certain sales of capital property. Voluntary registration is permitted. HST rates vary by place of supply: 13% in Ontario, 15% in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, and Prince Edward Island, and 5% GST plus any applicable provincial sales tax in non-participating provinces. Quebec generally imposes 9.975% QST in addition to 5% GST, subject to registration and place-of-supply rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated for GST/HST where the supplier delivers or ships the goods outside Canada and satisfies the statutory export conditions. Documentation must establish the export, destination outside Canada, supplier transaction, purchaser, goods, and date of shipment. Goods supplied to a non-resident for export may qualify where the statutory delivery, possession, or export conditions are met; merely selling to a non-resident while the goods remain in Canada does not automatically qualify. Services may be zero-rated only under specific place-of-supply and non-resident rules, including services performed for a non-resident where the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada, goods situated in Canada, or another excluded Canadian connection. Export zero-rating does not apply to services physically performed in Canada where a specific exclusion applies. A registrant claiming input tax credits or GST/HST refunds must retain commercial invoices, customs and transport evidence, contracts, proof of payment, customer status, and records linking inputs to zero-rated supplies.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the Canadian supplier, purchaser, goods, value, currency, and export destination
  • Export declaration filed through the Canada Border Services Agency Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) where required, including the applicable reporting reference
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, postal record, or other third-party transport document showing shipment outside Canada
  • Proof of delivery, importation, or customs entry in the destination jurisdiction where available
  • Sales contract, purchase order, packing list, and inventory or production records connecting the exported goods to the zero-rated invoice
  • Proof of payment or other settlement evidence; an inward remittance certificate is not a general Canadian statutory requirement, although bank evidence may support audit substantiation
  • For services: written contract, evidence of the recipient's non-resident status, service-delivery records, location and nature of the service, and evidence supporting the applicable zero-rating provision

Canada generally has no nationwide real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance or digital-tax-stamping mandate for ordinary domestic bakery sales. Registered persons must issue prescribed tax information on invoices, receipts, or other commercial documentation when required by customers, and must retain books and records supporting tax collected, input tax credits, zero-rating, exports, and imports. Electronic records and invoices are acceptable if they preserve required information and remain accessible for audit. CRA GST/HST returns are generally filed electronically where electronic filing is required or selected, but invoices are not ordinarily submitted to the CRA for pre-clearance. Certain specialized regimes and provincial requirements may impose separate electronic filing or point-of-sale obligations.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange control rule requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within 180 days. Export proceeds may generally be held offshore, subject to income-tax, transfer-pricing, anti-avoidance, customs, anti-money-laundering, and financial-reporting requirements. If zero-rating is unsupported or incorrectly claimed, the CRA may reassess GST/HST, deny related input tax credits or rebates, assess interest, and require repayment of improperly claimed amounts. False statements or omissions may attract statutory penalties, including penalties under the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions and gross-negligence-type conduct. Fraudulent conduct may expose the corporation and responsible individuals to prosecution and fines or imprisonment. Reassessment periods are generally four years for a GST/HST registrant, subject to extended periods for misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful blindness, fraud, or failure to file. Directors may face personal liability for certain unremitted amounts under applicable director-liability rules. Export documentation should therefore be retained for the applicable statutory record-retention period, generally six years from the end of the relevant tax year or reporting period, subject to longer periods where litigation, objection, waiver, or audit circumstances apply.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible currency regime under the Foreign Exchange Act. Current account transactions, including profit remittances and trade settlements, face no capital controls. Capital account transactions require ministerial approval only for investments exceeding CAD 100 million per counterparty annually. Profit repatriation is unrestricted for Canadian corporations, subject solely to CRA tax filing obligations and beneficial ownership disclosure.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory mandatory surrender or conversion requirements apply to export receipts. Exporters may retain foreign currency proceeds offshore or convert to CAD at prevailing market rates without timeline mandates. All FX transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to the Bank of Canada via authorized dealers. Voluntary surrender is permitted at any time with no prescribed ratios.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% (federal general corporate income tax rate after the 38% basic rate is reduced by the 10% provincial abatement and 13% general rate reduction); provincial taxes are separate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder; the rate may be reduced under an applicable tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$50,000 – aggregate fair market value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions in a taxation year; triggers mandatory Form T106 filing and sustained contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation expectations by the CRA. Canada follows OECD Master File/Local File principles administratively, and Country-by-Country reporting is required for multinational groups meeting the CRA-prescribed consolidated revenue threshold (approximately C$1.1 billion).

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations; however, the CRA applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), transfer pricing substance-over-form analysis, and DTA anti-abuse / principal purpose tests to disregard transactions lacking real economic substance, cross-border commercial rationality, and operational presence in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act, s. 239)

Statutory sanction: Criminal fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment; civil liability includes gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understatement and interest on unpaid taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors who personally participated in or directed the scheme are liable to prosecution as principals; the corporate veil is pierced under s. 227.1 ITA for uncollected payroll/tax withholdings, and directors may face travel bans in bail conditions and restitution orders before conviction.
Shell Company / Artificial Treaty Shopping (GAAR and CBCA)

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits and re-characterize the tax structure under s. 245 of the Income Tax Act; reassessment of tax, interest, and a 50% gross negligence penalty may apply; CBCA regulators may dissolve the company for failure to carry on business.

Director personal liability: Directors authorizing a sham structure may be personally assessed for Part XIII withholding under s. 227.1 ITA, sued for breach of fiduciary duty, and, in criminal treaty-abuse cases, subjected to prosecution, fines, and imprisonment where diverting income constitutes tax evasion.
AML / Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (PCMLTFA and Criminal Code)

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties of up to C$2,000,000 per contravention for a corporation; laundering proceeds of crime under Criminal Code s. 462.31 is an indictable offence punishable by imprisonment for up to ten years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who direct, participate in, or knowingly facilitate the transaction are parties to the offence; personal fines, imprisonment, travel restrictions, and seizure of personal assets under criminal restitution proceedings may be imposed.
