Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; online registration and administration portals: CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MBA); non-resident matters are administered by the CRA International Tax Services Office (Sudbury, Ontario).

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated executive search agency establishing Canadian taxable nexus must: (1) determine whether it is deemed to carry on business in Canada under Income Tax Act s. 253 or has a permanent establishment for treaty purposes; (2) procure the statutory TIN, the 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN), by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' with the International Tax Services Office or by online application through Business Registration Online (BRO), accompanied by certified corporate documents and, where applicable, proof of extra-provincial registration; (3) open the required program accounts: RC (corporate income tax) for any T2 filing obligation, RT (GST/HST) which functions as the VAT ID, and RP (payroll withholding) if Canadian employees are hired; (4) for a non-resident registrant with no place of business in Canada, expect the Minister to require acceptable security under Excise Tax Act s. 240 before the RT account is activated; (5) obtain written confirmation of the BN/RT account and thereafter file GST/HST returns through GST/HST NETFILE.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - a federal Business Corporation is a distinct taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act; if resident (central management and control in Canada), it is taxed on worldwide income; a non-resident corporation is taxable on Canadian-source income under s. 2(3); no consolidated filing or partnership pass-through treatment is available to a corporation, and foreign entity classifications are respected only where the arrangement has real substance.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; the status of a Canadian business corporation as a taxable entity is mandatory. Available statutory elections include: (a) Income Tax Act s. 216(1) election to tax net Canadian rental or timber income instead of gross withholding; (b) ITA s. 217 election for non-resident employees to file a Canadian return and be taxed on net employment income; (c) ITA s. 85(1) elective rollover for transfers of Canadian business property into the corporation at tax-deferred values; (d) ITA s. 39(6) joint election with a non-resident disposing of taxable Canadian property; (e) the GST/HST quick-method election under the Excise Tax Act and its regulations, commonly used by headhunting and research agencies with limited input-tax complexity; (f) discretionary fiscal year-end election under ITA ss. 249.1(4) and 250.1(1); hybrid-mismatch characterization and adjustment rules in ITA ss. 18.4-18.6 are mandatory and cannot be elected out of.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - a supplier becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant when its total worldwide taxable supplies (for the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters) exceed CAD 30,000; non-resident providers of remote digital services or short-term accommodation additionally face mandatory registration when Canadian taxable revenues exceed CAD 30,000 in any 12-month period under the 2021 digital-economy framework; a non-resident headhunter billing Canadian corporate clients must register from the first day it ceases to be a small supplier, or may voluntarily register from its first taxable supply in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 20 where the supplier makes the goods available outside Canada and retains evidence of export. Services (including executive search and headhunting services): zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 23 where (a) the recipient is a non-resident corporation, (b) the service is not performed in connection with real property situated in Canada, (c) the service is not in relation to tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of supply, and (d) the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada at the time of performance; a C-Suite executive search mandate performed for a foreign employer, with all deliverables (shortlists, interview reports, offer negotiation support) supplied to the foreign board, therefore qualifies as a zero-rated exported service and the agency may recover input tax credits on costs attributable to that export.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A or the Canadian Automated Export Declaration / CAED electronic record) for any physical movement of supporting goods or materials
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing export from Canada, or equivalent service-performance evidence (contract, interview schedules, assessment reports, invoices) for headhunting services supplied to the foreign recipient
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank wire / foreign-currency conversion confirmation from a Schedule I Canadian bank evidencing receipt of the export receivable from the non-resident client

No real-time fiscal clearance regime exists in Canada: there is no CRA pre-approval API, no digital stamping, and no continuous transaction control mandate. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE) where annual taxable supplies exceed the prescribed threshold (CRA administrative threshold generally above CAD 1.5 million); invoices, whether paper or electronic, including PEPPOL-based Pan-Canadian e-invoices, must support the supply under ETA record-keeping rules (s. 286); the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing standard is voluntary for B2B, with the federal government progressively accepting e-invoices for B2G procurement.

Zero-rating claims are subject to document-based CRA audit review: if the exporter fails to retain the required evidence, the CRA will reverse the zero-rating, assess 5% GST/HST on the full consideration, and charge prescribed compounded daily interest. Reassessment windows extend to 7 years for misrepresentation or wilful neglect under ETA s. 298(6); gross-negligence penalties of 25% of the disallowed amount apply under ETA s. 280(1); false statements on GST/HST or T2 returns attract substantial penalties under ITA s. 163.2; failure to provide records attracts penalties under ETA s. 285(1); directors may be jointly and severally liable under ETA s. 323 for the corporation's unpaid GST/HST clawbacks. The CRA can also suspend refunds, invoke tax treaty information exchanges, and place the entity on a pre-payment audit list.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible across both current account and capital account categories. No exchange-control permits, repatriation taxes, or conversion quotas apply to profits, dividends, management fees, or royalties. Profit remittance is freely permitted but is subject to non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (default 25%, treaty-reduced, e.g., 5% to 15% on dividends depending on shareholding) and to recordkeeping obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), including reporting thresholds at or above CAD 10,000 for cross-border transfers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to repatriate or convert export or service-export receipts. An executive search firm may lawfully maintain CAD or foreign-currency accounts and may retain receipts in the original currency. Statistically reportable information must be retained for Statistics Canada Balance of Payments surveys, and transactional documentation must be preserved for FINTRAC wire-report and client-identification purposes where applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary and financial system authority; prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); cross-border financial reporting is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada has no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration and applies no exchange-control approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Moderate-to-High viability for secondary disbursements, vendor payments, and foreign-client collections; WISE (Wise Business) is the designated B2B rail in the target payment profile. Legal constraints: Canada does not recognise the EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) passport; Wise operates as a FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB) and holds client funds at regulated partner banks under provincial MSB/money-transmitter licences where applicable. A Wise balance is a stored-value e-money instrument, is not a CDIC-insured deposit, and cannot serve as the sole or primary treasury account for statutory filings, escrow, or director-indemnity obligations. The primary operating and retainer escrow accounts must reside at a Schedule I chartered bank; Wise may be used for intragroup, retainer, and sub-contractor flows only up to prudent liquidity caps.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Non-resident ownership and control with a layered corporate structure; beneficial owners in jurisdictions that are non-CRS-compliant or that fail to produce tax identifiers.

Defense evidence: Documentation bundle: (i) Canada Revenue Agency Business Number letter confirming federal T2 corporate tax registration; (ii) Articles of Incorporation and the up-to-date annual return retrievable from Corporations Canada (CIDRE); (iii) full ultimate beneficial ownership (UBO) declaration identifying each individual holding 10 percent or more, with notarised passport pages, proof of residential address, and FATCA/CRS self-certifications (W-8BEN-E or CRS-I); (iv) notarised or legalised board resolution appointing a Canadian-resident contact person and AML liaison; and (v) an economic-substance memorandum demonstrating client contracts, billing records, and operational decision-making in Canada.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Federal small-business rate for an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC): 9% on qualifying active business income within the CAD 500,000 business limit, subject to associated-corporation and passive-income restrictions. Provincial or territorial corporate tax is additional and depends on the permanent establishment allocation.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Part XIII withholding tax is generally 25% on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident. A treaty may reduce the rate. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership test and 15% in other treaty cases. Treaty entitlement depends on beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits provisions, anti-conduit rules, and procedural compliance.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A CCPC may qualify for a three-month balance-due date if it satisfies the statutory taxable-income and associated-corporation conditions. Instalments are due on the prescribed monthly or quarterly dates. A corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act must also file its annual return with Corporations Canada, which is separate from the T2.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which arm's-length pricing rules cease to apply. Contemporaneous documentation is required for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, subject to the statutory exception where the total value of transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents is CAD 1 million or less for the year. T106 reporting generally applies at the CAD 1 million threshold. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File filing requirement solely because a group exceeds a fixed revenue threshold. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, or the Canadian-dollar equivalent under the applicable rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone general economic-substance regulation equivalent to the United Kingdom or United Arab Emirates ESR regimes. Substance is relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse rules, beneficial-ownership requirements, transfer-pricing rules, permanent-establishment analysis, thin-capitalization provisions, foreign-affiliate rules, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and avoidance. An executive-search corporation should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making authority, recruitment records, client contracts, operating premises or credible business infrastructure, service-delivery evidence, and commercially supportable intercompany pricing.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and deliberate false reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to two years on indictment, or both, in addition to tax, interest, and civil penalties. Gross-negligence penalties may be 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they authorized, assented to, or participated in the conduct. Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted payroll source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts under statutory director-liability rules, subject to due-diligence defenses. Civil restitution, asset seizure, and travel restrictions may arise from enforcement proceedings; imprisonment is not automatic and requires the applicable criminal process.
Payroll source-deduction failure

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted deductions, employer CPP and EI amounts, interest, penalties, and possible prosecution under the Income Tax Act. Corporate penalties may apply for failure to deduct, remit, report, or provide information.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions if the corporation cannot pay, unless the statutory due-diligence defense is established. Deliberate payroll fraud may produce criminal fines and imprisonment.
GST/HST non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, failure-to-file penalties, input-tax-credit denial, and potential prosecution under the Excise Tax Act. Collected GST/HST is generally held in trust-like priority for remittance purposes.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts, subject to statutory limitations and due-diligence defenses. Fraudulent claims may result in criminal fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, deny deductions, impute income, apply transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, impose interest, and require amended withholding or information filings.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is assessed. Personal exposure may arise from knowing participation in false records, tax evasion, creditor misconduct, or unpaid source deductions and GST/HST.
Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of withholding at 25%, interest, penalties for failure to withhold or remit, denial of treaty benefits under anti-avoidance and limitation-on-benefits principles, and potential payer liability for the shortfall.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may face civil recovery for unpaid withholding amounts and criminal exposure if the documentation was knowingly false or the arrangement was fraudulent.
Shell company, sham, or nominee arrangement

Statutory sanction: Transactions may be recharacterized or denied under the Income Tax Act, GAAR, sham doctrines, beneficial-ownership rules, or anti-money-laundering legislation. Consequences may include tax reassessment, forfeiture, civil penalties, prosecution, and corporate dissolution or registration consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate may face personal civil claims, asset tracing, criminal charges, fines, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions may be imposed in connection with enforcement or bail proceedings, but are not an automatic tax penalty.
Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: The Criminal Code and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may impose substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment. A headhunting agency is not automatically a FINTRAC reporting entity solely because it receives wires, but obligations arise if it conducts a regulated business such as money services, and criminal proceeds prohibitions apply regardless of FINTRAC status.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, facilitate, or knowingly benefit from laundering may face prosecution, forfeiture, imprisonment, and restitution. Corporate separateness does not protect a director from personal criminal conduct.
Privacy and confidential recruitment-data breach

Statutory sanction: PIPEDA and applicable provincial privacy laws may result in investigations, compliance orders, damages, breach-notification obligations, and regulatory enforcement. Provincial private-sector privacy legislation may apply instead of or alongside PIPEDA depending on the province and activity.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is generally fact-dependent and may arise for knowing participation, breach of fiduciary duty, statutory offenses, or independently wrongful conduct. Criminal exposure is possible for intentional misuse of personal information under applicable law.
Corporate-record and beneficial-ownership non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The Canada Business Corporations Act may impose corporation and individual fines for non-compliance, and the corporation may face compliance orders or administrative consequences. Exact penalties depend on the specific provision and enforcement route.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be fined personally and may face prosecution for knowingly authorizing false or incomplete records. Veil piercing is exceptional, but statutory personal liability and criminal responsibility are not barred by incorporation.
Foreign-exchange and profit-repatriation failure

Statutory sanction: The payment may be treated as an unlawful distribution, a shareholder benefit, a non-deductible or recharacterized payment, or a payment subject to unremitted Part XIII withholding. Interest, penalties, reassessment, and creditor recovery may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unlawful dividends or other prohibited distributions under applicable corporate legislation, particularly where the corporation was insolvent or statutory solvency tests were ignored. Criminal liability requires additional fraudulent or knowing misconduct.
