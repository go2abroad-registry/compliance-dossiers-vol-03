Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; primary tax portal: 'My Business Account' (Mon dossier d'entreprise) and Business Registration Online (BRO) via canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; federal corporate registration is administered by Corporations Canada (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada) for CBCA incorporations.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Canadian entity obtains a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) by: (1) incorporating federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) through Corporations Canada's online Filing Centre, observing the CBCA director-residency requirements; (2) completing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' or using the CRA Business Registration Online service; (3) activating the required program accounts - RT for GST/HST, RP for Corporation Income Tax, RC for Payroll, and RZ for Import/Export; and (4) if the corporation is non-resident and not yet incorporated in Canada, filing Form RC466 'Application to Register a Non-Resident for GST/HST' and Form RC516 for non-resident account registration. A federal corporation is deemed resident in Canada for tax purposes by operation of ITA subsection 250(4), triggering an annual T2 corporate income tax filing obligation on worldwide income, and must maintain a registered Canadian agent and service address.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation (C-corporation analog; no pass-through eligibility for business income; resident corporations taxed on worldwide income, non-resident corporations taxed only on Canadian-source income, with Part XIV branch tax applicable to non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada). — A foreign-owned Canadian entity obtains a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) by: (1) incorporating federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) through Corporations Canada's online Filing Centre, observing the CBCA director-residency requirements; (2) completing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number' or using the CRA Business Registration Online service; (3) activating the required program accounts - RT for GST/HST, RP for Corporation Income Tax, RC for Payroll, and RZ for Import/Export; and (4) if the corporation is non-resident and not yet incorporated in Canada, filing Form RC466 'Application to Register a Non-Resident for GST/HST' and Form RC516 for non-resident account registration. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (C-corporation analog; no pass-through eligibility for business income; resident corporations taxed on worldwide income, non-resident corporations taxed only on Canadian-source income, with Part XIV branch tax applicable to non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada).

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box election; entity classification is determined by corporate legal form. Available statutory elections relevant to this structure include: (i) functional currency election under ITA s.261 permitting USD reporting; (ii) ITA s.216 election for non-resident owners of Canadian rental property; (iii) deemed-dividend and Capital Dividend Account elections under ITA ss.83(2) and 89(5); (iv) GST/HST Quick Method election for smaller registrants; (v) treaty-based non-resident tax waiver/Part XIV branch tax reduction elections; and (vi) targeted elections available under Canada's BEPS hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA ss.18.2-18.4) to address hybrid financial instrument and hybrid entity deductions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 per annuum measured on cumulative worldwide revenues from taxable supplies (other than financial services) over the preceding four calendar quarters, including associated-person aggregation; registration becomes mandatory immediately upon exceeding the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada are subject to the same threshold, and voluntary registration is available to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for goods applies under ETA Schedule VI section 6, requiring: (i) the supplier ships or delivers the goods to a destination outside Canada; (ii) the supplier transfers possession of the goods to a common carrier or consignee for shipment to a destination outside Canada; or (iii) the supplier transfers ownership or possession to the recipient and the goods are exported as soon as reasonably possible, with adequate documentary proof of export. For kitting and assembly services, Schedule VI section 9 may zero-rate a service performed on tangible personal property that is imported into Canada solely for the purpose of that service and exported as soon as reasonably possible after the service is rendered; however, general warehousing, storage, and handling services performed on goods situated in Canada remain subject to GST/HST. The non-resident services rule under Schedule VI section 23 is unavailable because the kitting/assembly work is performed on goods physically in Canada at the time of supply; therefore, clean customs records linking the invoice to the export transaction are mandatory for any claimed zero-rating.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (CAED/B13A acceptance report) for shipments exceeding C$2,000
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill consigned to a destination outside Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or bank foreign-currency receipt evidencing non-resident payment
  • Commercial invoice citing Incoterms 2020 export terms
  • Kitting work order, warehouse receipt, and recipient export instruction contract

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping mandate as of the current regime. GST/HST registrants are required to file returns electronically through the CRA GST/HST NETFILE system, invoices must contain the supplier's BN and prescribed disclosure data, and books and records must be retained for six years. E-invoicing is voluntary, with Pan-Canadian Peppol-aligned adoption under federal evaluation; exporters shipping goods valued above C$2,000 must submit electronic export declarations to the Canada Border Services Agency via the Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) system.

CRA may reassess within four years of the relevant reporting period, but the limitation period is unlimited where misrepresentation, neglect, or wilful default is shown; fraudulent or grossly negligent export zero-rating claims attract a 50% gross-negligence penalty under ETA s.285.1 plus prescribed-rate daily compound interest, and refunds previously paid may be clawed back in full as tax and interest. Late or inaccurate CBSA export declarations attract Administrative Monetary Penalties of up to C$25,000, and wilful evasion may lead to criminal prosecution with fines and imprisonment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member (accepted 1952) and operates a fully freely floating currency with unrestricted convertibility on both current and capital account. The wartime Foreign Exchange Control Act regime was repealed in 1951; no Bank of Canada or government approval is required for inward equity, dividend, interest, royalty, or loan remittances. Profit-repatriation friction is exclusively tax-driven: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%, commonly reduced to 0 to 15% under tax treaties, e.g., 5% on arm's-length dividends to a U.S. parent under the Canada-U.S. Treaty and 0% on arm's-length interest under Article XI); thin-capitalization limitation of 2:1 debt-to-equity under ITA s. 18(4)-(6); transfer-pricing compliance under ITA s. 247; and GST/HST zero-rating on exported goods. Cross-border investment screening under the Investment Canada Act applies only to acquisitions of control above statutory thresholds or to new investments in non-cultural businesses with a notice obligation, not to routine 3PL kitting operations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None apply. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement whatsoever. Exporters may hold export receivables indefinitely in any currency, in onshore foreign-currency accounts or offshore accounts. The only obligations are informational: reporting of cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC, and income inclusion under the accrual method for tax purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the sole central monetary authority. Canada maintains no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration; prudential regulation of chartered banks is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and financial-transaction reporting and AML supervision of money service businesses is vested in the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). The Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) designates the Canadian dollar as lawful money.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High feasibility as a supplementary rail for operational disbursements only, with material legal constraints. Airwallex (Canada) Inc. is registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC, not a Schedule I chartered bank: it can originate ACH Debits, EFTs, SWIFT MT103s, and hold pooled CAD and USD settlement balances, but it cannot take deposits, extend credit, or provide CDIC insurance. The same MSB perimeter applies to Wise, PayPal, and Stripe-linked treasury issuers under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Practical constraints: (i) the 3PL must ensure inbound funds from fintech aggregator accounts are traceable to identifiable end clients; many Tier-1 banks treat third-party aggregator settlements as higher risk and request transaction-level detail; (ii) Airwallex balances are not eligible for Canadian Payments Act settlement guarantees; (iii) the entity should keep a minimum liquidity buffer of approximately two to four weeks of disbursements in a Tier-1 insured account.

Equity contribution from a non-resident parent: remit via SWIFT MT103 (field 50K remitter; field 59 beneficiary as the CA_FED corporation name and CBCA number in field 72/77B), clearing in Canada through Lynx (Payments Canada real-time gross settlement for high values) or ACSS for batch EFT. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: High feasibility as a supplementary rail for operational disbursements only, with material legal constraints. Airwallex (Canada) Inc. is registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC, not a Schedule I chartered bank: it can originate ACH Debits, EFTs, SWIFT MT103s, and hold pooled CAD and USD settlement balances, but it cannot take deposits, extend credit, or provide CDIC insurance. The same MSB perimeter applies to Wise, PayPal, and Stripe-linked treasury issuers under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Practical constraints: (i) the 3PL must ensure inbound funds from fintech aggregator accounts are traceable to identifiable end clients; many Tier-1 banks treat third-party aggregator settlements as higher risk and request transaction-level detail; (ii) Airwallex balances are not eligible for Canadian Payments Act settlement guarantees; (iii) the entity should keep a minimum liquidity buffer of approximately two to four weeks of disbursements in a Tier-1 insured account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate (net of 10% provincial abatement and 13% federal general rate reduction); provincial/territorial income tax rates add 8%–16%, producing a commonly cited combined headline rate of 23%–31% for a federally incorporated taxable Canadian corporation carrying on business in Canada.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on any dividend paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless a reduced rate applies under an income tax treaty; the 25% rate applies to the gross amount of the dividend without deduction for the Canadian entity's losses or costs.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

The T2 return must be filed no later than 6 months after the end of the fiscal period; the final Part I tax balance-due day is 2 months after the year end for a qualifying CCPC and 3 months for all other corporations; monthly instalments are required for non-CCPC filers whenever projected Part I tax exceeds C$3,000, payable by the last day of each month of the taxation year.

Due within 6 months of the corporation's fiscal year end, aligning with the T2 return due date; the T106 must be filed for any non-arm's length non-resident counterparty when the aggregate of all reportable transaction values (gross revenue, costs paid, payments received) equals or exceeds C$1,000,000 in the fiscal year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File) is mandatory where the aggregate value of reportable transactions between the Canadian taxpayer and a single non-arm's length non-resident exceeds C$1,000,000 in the fiscal year. A Master File is also required for an MNE group whose consolidated group revenue equals or exceeds C$100,000,000; the Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) must be filed where the ultimate parent entity's consolidated group revenue equals or exceeds C$750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose the Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations; equivalent discipline arises from (i) s. 247 of the Income Tax Act requiring an arm's length result for cross-border non-arm's length transactions, (ii) s. 245 GAAR, which disallows tax benefits from transactions lacking genuine economic purpose and business substance, and (iii) the Limitation on Benefits (LOB) article of Canada's tax treaties (e.g., Art. XXIX A of the Canada-US treaty) that restricts treaty benefits to residents with active business, public-trading, or derivative-ownership profiles. A Canadian fulfillment/kitting subsidiary with leased warehouse premises, full-time operating employees, a corporate bank account, and actual third-party logistics contracts satisfies the substance test; a letterbox subsidiary without premises or employees is exposed to CRA recharacterization, denial of treaty benefits, Part XVI anti-avoidance rules, and reassessment at the foreign parent level.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Criminal Offence)

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 239(1): on indictment, a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for a term not exceeding 2 years; on summary conviction, the same fine range and imprisonment up to 6 months. The CRA also recovers all evaded taxes, statutory interest, and civil penalties, and may seize bank accounts and inventory.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who participate in or direct the evasion face criminal prosecution in their personal capacity; under s. 227.1, directors are jointly and severally liable for any unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and Part XIII withholdings, and the CRA may issue a director's liability certificate against personal assets without prior court order.
Gross Negligence and False Statements

Statutory sanction: s. 163(2) gross negligence penalty: the greater of C$100 and 50% of the understated tax; s. 163.2 penalties of up to C$100,000 for participating in a false tax shelter or misrepresentation; CRA may reassess beyond the normal 3-year limitation period to 6 years (or 10 years where negligence or fraud is involved) and compound daily arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence participate in the filing of false information are subject to the same 50% penalties personally; the CRA can pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a mere facade, impose civil restitution orders, and prohibit departure from Canada under s. 226 of the Income Tax Act where amounts are owing.
Transfer-Pricing Mispricing (Offshore Profit Shifting)

Statutory sanction: s. 247(2) reassessment adjusting revenues, deductions, and allocations to the arm's length result; s. 247(3) penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds C$5,000 and the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts; failure to file T106 adds daily penalties and extends the statutory reassessment window; the CRA may also apply the GAAR to recharacterize the sham arrangement.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or certify TP documentation that is wilfully false may be examined under oath by the CRA and added as parties to penalty assessments; under corporate veil piercing principles, directors can be held personally liable for intentional diversion of corporate revenues and for withholding obligations that arise from recharacterized distributions.
Shell Company / Absence of Economic Substance and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: GAAR (s. 245) denies the tax benefit and recharacterizes the arrangement; the CRA may reassess the entire series of transactions for up to 10 years and apply the 50% gross negligence penalty; the Limitation on Benefits article of the applicable treaty would be denied, exposing the shareholder to the full 25% Part XIII withholding tax; for a federal CBCA corporation, directors face CBCA beneficial-ownership register penalties of up to C$200,000 and imprisonment for 12 months for knowingly false information (with corporate penalties up to C$1,000,000).

Director personal liability: Directors of the shell entity are at risk of personal liability for the corporation's unremitted source deductions and taxes under s. 227.1, for recovery of management fees extracted without substance, and for civil restitution where the corporate veil is pierced; travel bans and asset freezes under ss. 226 and 225.1 ITA may be imposed when unpaid taxes exceed C$50,000 and the director is considered a flight risk.
AML and Financial-Reporting Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CBCA: knowingly providing false or misleading information in the beneficial ownership register is a hybrid offence punishable on summary conviction by a fine of up to C$200,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 12 months for an individual, with fines up to C$1,000,000 for a corporation; PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties can reach C$500,000 per violation for a corporation (and additional penalties for repeat violations), while criminal convictions for money laundering carry imprisonment and fines up to C$2,000,000 for the entity.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for AML penalties where the corporation's compliance failures result from their wilful blindness or participation; directors may be subject to criminal money-laundering charges where they instruct employees to structure transactions to evade reporting thresholds, and the corporate veil is pierced where the corporation is used as an instrument of unlawful conduct, exposing the directors' personal assets.
