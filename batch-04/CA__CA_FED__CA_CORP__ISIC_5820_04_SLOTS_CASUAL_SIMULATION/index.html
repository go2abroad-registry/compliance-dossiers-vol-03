Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Territorial_Scope: A federal Business Corporation is deemed resident in Canada under Income Tax Act subsection 250(4)(a) and is taxed at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act; no pass-through eligibility exists for a corporation. The federal general corporate rate is 15% (38% statutory rate less 10% provincial abatement less 13% general rate reduction); a Canadian-controlled private corporation may access the 9% small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income. A resident corporation is taxed on worldwide income while a non-resident-owned corporation carrying on business through a Canadian permanent establishment is taxed only on Canadian-source income. Foreign revenue from the digital gaming business is included in Canadian taxable income with a foreign tax credit available under section 126 of the Income Tax Act.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box entity classification election. Available elections: (i) subsection 89(11) election among associated corporations to allocate the small business limit; (ii) section 216 election permitting a non-resident to file a return and pay tax on Canadian rental or timber income; (iii) subsection 115.2 election for a non-resident corporation to file a simplified Canadian income tax return under an income tax treaty; (iv) subsection 128.1(4) election for a corporation migrating from Canada on a deemed disposition basis; (v) for GST/HST purposes, the simplified non-resident digital-service supplier registration election under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act and the closely-related corporation election under section 156 (intercorporate supplies at nil consideration); (vi) hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.4 through 18.6 of the Income Tax Act are mandatory recharacterization provisions that operate without taxpayer election and do not create a Canadian analogue to an entity classification check-the-box regime.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under both current account and capital account in the IMF Articles of Agreement sense. Canada imposes no exchange controls restricting convertibility, dividend repatriation, interest payments, licence royalties, or capital disinvestment. Corporate profits may be remitted abroad without prior regulatory approval, subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (typically 25% under domestic law, reducible by tax treaty) and the general compliance requirements of the Income Tax Act. The Investment Canada Act may apply as a review mechanism for non-resident control of Canadian businesses, but this is not a currency control.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export proceeds surrender rule. Exporters and service providers, including software and social-casino license suppliers, are not required to convert foreign currency receipts into CAD within any prescribed timeline, nor are they required to remit export proceeds to any designated account. All foreign currency earnings may be retained, held in foreign-denominated accounts, or deployed as treasury policy dictates, provided FINTRAC reporting thresholds (including large transaction reports and cross-border currency reporting) are satisfied.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (the central bank) retains monetary policy authority; foreign exchange oversight is distributed via the Department of Finance Canada, while the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers anti-money laundering and commercial transaction reporting. Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign exchange control administration; the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act govern the national monetary framework.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility with probative legal constraints. Non-Canadian fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) may operate for Canadian corporate clients only if the provider is properly registered with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business (MSB), which is required for foreign money services businesses directing services at Canada. Alternatively, a fintech may obtain an E-Money Institution licence from another jurisdiction, but that licence does not replace Canadian MSB registration. Funds held with unregulated foreign EMIs are not covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC); if the EMI is not an MSB, opening and funding the account creates reverse money laundering and counterparty sanctions exposure. For social-casino distribution, the acceptance of USD payouts via payment facilitators such as Stripe, Adyen, or digital bank accounts may be allowed, provided the EMI has a compliant anti-money laundering (AML) program that treats the mobile gaming revenue as a consumer digital-payments flow. Legally prudent architects will use an EMI only as a secondary collection rail, not as a primary repository of shareholder capital.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity injections must be routed through the Canadian banking system with a compliant wire transfer protocol. A standard SWIFT MT103/MT202 from a foreign bank to the Canadian bank is customary; the remitter must include in field 70 the precise corporate-purpose description, e.g., 'Capital contribution for issued shares' or 'Paid-in capital under Business Corporations Act'; field 57 must contain the beneficiary bank's BIC (e.g., ROYCCAT2 or TDOMCATTTOR), and the beneficiary account should be a CAD current account or an escrow account legally identified as a capital-escrow deposit. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility with probative legal constraints. Non-Canadian fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) may operate for Canadian corporate clients only if the provider is properly registered with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business (MSB), which is required for foreign money services businesses directing services at Canada. Alternatively, a fintech may obtain an E-Money Institution licence from another jurisdiction, but that licence does not replace Canadian MSB registration. Funds held with unregulated foreign EMIs are not covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC); if the EMI is not an MSB, opening and funding the account creates reverse money laundering and counterparty sanctions exposure. For social-casino distribution, the acceptance of USD payouts via payment facilitators such as Stripe, Adyen, or digital bank accounts may be allowed, provided the EMI has a compliant anti-money laundering (AML) program that treats the mobile gaming revenue as a consumer digital-payments flow. Legally prudent architects will use an EMI only as a secondary collection rail, not as a primary repository of shareholder capital.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is mandated when the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1 million in a fiscal year; Master File (Form RC7204) and Local File (Form RC7205) are mandated for MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 1 billion, with the Local File additionally conditioned on the Canadian entity's gross revenue exceeding CAD 10 million and/or reportable transactions exceeding CAD 1 million

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a Caribbean/Gulf-style ESR statute; instead, the CRA applies s.245 GAAR, s.247(2) transfer-pricing recharacterization, the beneficial ownership doctrine, and the OECD DEMPE functional analysis to attack substance-deficient structures. A Canadian social casino holding IP must demonstrate real employees, decision-making, IP development, enhancement, maintenance, protection, and exploitation (DEMPE) functions, and bear commensurate financial risk; otherwise revenue can be reallocated to the non-resident parent and recharacterized as a deemed dividend subject to 25% Part XIII withholding

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: 10% penalty on the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 1 million or 10% of gross revenue; full reassessment and recharacterization of the adjustment as a deemed dividend or deemed interest; compound daily interest; Form T106 penalties up to CAD 25,000 plus CAD 24,000 gross-negligence penalty

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who sign false or incomplete T2/T106 returns face the 50% gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) and criminal liability under s.239(1) for knowingly making false statements; CRA may enter personal assets and impose travel bans pending criminal tax proceedings
Unremitted Part XIII / Withholding Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of the full unremitted amount plus compound daily interest; late-payment penalty of 5% plus 1% per month; potential s.239 criminal prosecution with fines of 50%-200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years

Director personal liability: Under s.227.1, directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII and source-withholding amounts even after resignation; the CRA may pursue personal bank accounts, garnish wages, and prohibit cross-border travel without a court release
GAAR / Shell Company Anti-Avoidance

Statutory sanction: s.245 GAAR denial of the tax benefit, recharacterization of transactions, reassessment at full statutory rates, and s.163(2) gross negligence penalties of 50% of the avoided tax; denial of treaty relief for lack of beneficial ownership

Director personal liability: Corporate veil piercing where the entity is a mere conduit: directors are personally assessed under s.227.1 for withholding, and knowingly signing conduit arrangements creates exposure to tax evasion prosecution and personal asset seizure
Criminal Tax Evasion / False Declarations

Statutory sanction: Under s.239(1), conviction on indictment: fine between 50% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; summary conviction: imprisonment up to 2 years less a day; the CRA + RCMP joint enforcement units routinely target gaming-adjacent digital businesses

Director personal liability: Personal criminal liability is imposed on signing officers; the sentencing court may issue a travel ban, restitution order, and forfeiture of proceeds; professional disqualification and reputational death are direct collateral consequences
AML / FINTRAC and Payment-Rails Abuse

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for AML violations under the PCMLTFA; CRA and FINTRAC coordinated audits may disallow expenses funneled through unreported AML-treated flows and impose gross negligence penalties

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly enable laundering of virtual credits face criminal money-laundering charges (Criminal Code s.462.31), imprisonment up to 10 years on indictment, travel bans, and forfeiture of personal and corporate assets
Director Payroll / Personal Benefit Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Unremitted source-withholding reassessments plus interest; 5% late-filing penalties; s.163(2) gross negligence penalty of 50% where the omission is made knowingly

Director personal liability: s.227.1 makes each director jointly and severally liable for the entire unremitted payroll amount; personal bankruptcy does not discharge the liability; the CRA can issue a Requisition to pay and freeze personal assets
