Generally permissible for CBCA registration only: the registered office must be a physical street address in a Canadian province or territory where the corporation carries on business, and must be attended during normal business hours for service of process. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer for federal income tax and GST/HST. For GST/HST it is a 'registrant' once taxable supplies exceed the small supplier threshold; no check-the-box pass-through is permitted under the Income Tax Act.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Canada. Available elections relevant to this structure include: (i) GST/HST section 156 election for closely related corporations to treat taxable supplies as made for nil consideration; (ii) election under section 211 of the ETA for the filing of combined returns for GST/HST members of a qualifying group; (iii) the branch or subsidiary election under ETA section 178.4 permitting a non-resident registrant to file separate returns for Canadian branches; (iv) treaty-based transfer pricing and permanent establishment attributions under Canada's income tax conventions. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (sections 18.2–18.4 Income Tax Act) limit certain deduction benefits but are not 'election' mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.05%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — cumulative worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated financial services) in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and in any single calendar quarter trigger mandatory GST/HST registration for a non-resident carrying on business in Canada (ETA section 240; 'small supplier' threshold at section 148).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, a supply of a service is zero-rated (0% GST/HST) where the service is supplied to a non-resident person and the service is: (i) an international freight forwarding service in respect of goods, including air cargo charter brokerage, air waybill management and cargo consolidation services, provided the goods are exported from Canada; (ii) a supply of a transportation service (air or other) of goods from Canada to a destination outside Canada; (iii) an international freight service supplied to a non-resident regime under section 23 of the ETA (supplies made to non-residents) provided the service is not in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed, and the service is not 'in respect of' movable property used or consumed primarily in Canada. To support zero-rating, the registrant must maintain evidence that the recipient is a non-resident, that the service is physically performed in relation to export cargo, and that the consideration is received from outside Canada. Intermediary services in the charter-broker chain (acting as agent for the non-resident charterer or for the non-resident air carrier) will be zero-rated only where the invoice is issued to, and paid by, the non-resident principal.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Customs Export Reporting via the Canadian Automated Export Declaration — CAED or equivalent electronic filing with Canada Border Services Agency)
  • Air Waybill (AWB) and/or Master Air Waybill evidencing carriage from Canada to a foreign destination
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or equivalent multimodal transport document
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank-generated SWIFT / Fedwire credit advice evidencing foreign currency remittance from the non-resident counterparty)
  • Non-resident status evidence (foreign certificate of incorporation, tax residence certificate, or statutory declaration)

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval of invoices. Mandatory electronic filing applies to GST/HST registrants with annual revenue exceeding CAD 1.5 million (GST/HST NETFILE or EDI). Registered domestic businesses above CAD 5 million in annual sales are required to issue e-invoices to their customers via EDI or XML data interchange; the CRA accepts machine-readable records and requires audit-ready digital data. The Pan-Canadian e-invoice framework (adopted from European PEPPOL standard) is available voluntarily. A non-resident registrant must retain invoice records in English or French and provide them to the CRA upon demand.

The ETA authorizes the CRA to assess an unregistered or mis-registered exporter for uncollected GST/HST on services wrongly zero-rated, plus interest under ETA section 280.1 and a failure to file penalty under section 280 if returns are outstanding. Where a registrant negligently or wilfully furnishes false export substantiation, the CRA may impose a gross negligence penalty of 25% of the amount assessed under ETA subsection 285.1(1) (in addition to the base tax assessment), or a similar false statement penalty under subsection 327(2) if the claim relates to an input tax credit or refund. The CRA may also deny net input tax credits (ITCs) claimed on expenditures attributable to the export supply and claw back any previously refunded GST/HST on a Schedule A determination. The assessment limitation period extends from four years to any later reassessment where misrepresentation, fraud, or a waiver has been executed under subsection 298(4). The agency applies an administrative 180-day benchmark for verifying foreign exchange repatriation against export proceeds in air cargo and freight forwarding supply chains; failure to evidence repatriation within 180 days triggers a focused transfer pricing and zero-rating audit, and under Division E of the ETA the exporter may be ordered to deliver security for unpaid taxes. For an international air cargo charter broker, the CRA scrutinizes inter-company invoicing between the Canadian broker and the offshore charterer or air carrier; a favorable outcome requires contemporaneous documentary confirmation of the non-resident status, a fully executed charter party contract, and standard bank SWIFT wires matching the exact invoiced currency and amounts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely, and there are no exchange control restrictions on foreign currency purchase, sale, or transfer. Profit remittance and dividend repatriation are unrestricted provided Canadian-source earnings are reported and, where applicable, subject to withholding tax under Income Tax Act subsection 212(2), reduced by applicable tax treaty (e.g., Canada-Peru, India, etc.). The Income Tax Act under Part XIII applies a 25% statutory withholding on dividends paid to non-residents, but treaties typically reduce this to 5%-15%. Capital gains remittance for non-residents requires a compliance certificate under section 116 of the Income Tax Act. No prior approval for capital flows is required from the Bank of Canada or OSFI, though large cash transactions (CAD 10,000 or more) and global electronic funds transfers (EFTs) of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline for export proceeds in Canada. A resident business may retain export earnings in any foreign currency and may hold funds in offshore accounts, provided such accounts are accurately disclosed for income tax purposes and do not constitute a controlled foreign affiliate triggering attribution rules under Income Tax Act sections 91-95. All export receipts must be recorded in Canadian dollar equivalents for tax filing (using the daily exchange rate or the average annual rate published by the Bank of Canada where applicable). No mandatory conversion ratio applies; export proceeds can be used directly for international trade settlements, but anti-money laundering (PCMLTFA) reporting obligations apply to EFTs exceeding CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Department of Finance Canada; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for cross-border monetary reporting, while the Bank of Canada is the central monetary authority overseeing financial stability and payment clearing systems.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-High for ancillary and subsidiary treasury functions, but Low as a primary or sole cross-border liquidity rail. Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise Business, Revolut Business, and Payoneer can on-board Canadian federal corporations, provided the EMI is registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC or operates through a Canadian Schedule I bank sponsor. Legal constraints include: (i) EMIs are not chartered banks under the Bank Act and do not hold deposit-taking insurance from CDIC; (ii) funds held with EMIs may be classified as unsecured liabilities and are not guaranteed by the Canadian government; (iii) the $10,000 EFT reporting threshold under the PCMLTFA applies to all domestic and cross-border wire transfers through EMIs; (iv) certain EMIs may restrict the holding or conversion of CAD/USD settlement due to their own licensing limitations; (v) for escrow arrangements involving aircraft charter deposits, a Tier 1 Canadian bank trust account is preferred over EMI to satisfy counterparty due diligence requirements. For operational liquidity, an EMI can be used for low-value disbursements, but capital escrow and high-value trade settlements must route through Tier 1 banking rails.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: Medium-High for ancillary and subsidiary treasury functions, but Low as a primary or sole cross-border liquidity rail. Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise Business, Revolut Business, and Payoneer can on-board Canadian federal corporations, provided the EMI is registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC or operates through a Canadian Schedule I bank sponsor. Legal constraints include: (i) EMIs are not chartered banks under the Bank Act and do not hold deposit-taking insurance from CDIC; (ii) funds held with EMIs may be classified as unsecured liabilities and are not guaranteed by the Canadian government; (iii) the $10,000 EFT reporting threshold under the PCMLTFA applies to all domestic and cross-border wire transfers through EMIs; (iv) certain EMIs may restrict the holding or conversion of CAD/USD settlement due to their own licensing limitations; (v) for escrow arrangements involving aircraft charter deposits, a Tier 1 Canadian bank trust account is preferred over EMI to satisfy counterparty due diligence requirements. For operational liquidity, an EMI can be used for low-value disbursements, but capital escrow and high-value trade settlements must route through Tier 1 banking rails.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate; combined federal-provincial general rate ranges from 23% to 31% depending on province and interprovincial allocation. For Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs), the federal Small Business Deduction reduces the rate to 9% on active business income up to CAD 500,000 (subject to the business limit reduction at taxable capital and passive income thresholds).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, imposed under subsection 212(2) of the Income Tax Act. The Canada–US Income Tax Convention reduces the rate to 5% on dividends paid to a US corporation that beneficially owns at least 10% of the voting shares of the Canadian payer; otherwise 15%. Most other Canadian DTAs provide 5% for substantial shareholdings (usually at least 10% voting power) and 15% for portfolio dividends. Dividends to treaty countries may also be subject to Canadian non-resident surtax if the non-resident corporation does not have a permanent establishment in Canada.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end (e.g., a December 31 year-end is due by June 30 of the following calendar year). A final tax payment is due within two months after fiscal year-end for most corporations, or three months for a CCPC claiming the SBD and whose taxable income did not exceed a specified threshold in the preceding year. Instalment payments of estimated tax are generally due monthly (or quarterly for CCPCs in some cases).

Deadline: · Frequency:

Due on the same date as the T2 return, generally six months after the fiscal year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory T106 information return filing threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (aggregate fair market value of all non-arm's length transactions with non-residents in the fiscal year). Formal contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (equivalent to a Local File) has no fixed statutory minimum threshold; however, under subsection 247(3) the CRA may assess a penalty of 10% of the adjusted amount (minimum CAD 5,000; maximum CAD 500,000) where the taxpayer fails to produce documentation demonstrating an arm's length result no later than six months after the CRA's written request. For large taxpayers (total gross revenue above CAD 90 million), the CRA expects a Master File; for smaller taxpayers, a Canadian Local File is recommended for major transactions. A Berry Ratio or TNMM analysis is routinely used by air cargo brokerages given their intermediation function.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime equivalent to the UAE/Cayman; however, economic substance requirements arise from statutory GAAR (subsection 245(3) of the Income Tax Act), the beneficial ownership requirement for treaty access, the foreign affiliate exempt surplus/FAPI rules, and the CRA's anti-avoidance jurisprudence. A Canadian corporation that is a 'letterbox' with no office, no employees, no Canadian decision-making, or that merely routes charter brokerage commissions through Canada without commercial substance may be reassessed under GAAR or have its transactions disregarded. The CRA considers functional analysis (personnel, assets, risk assumption) in transfer pricing; for an air cargo broker claiming arm's length commissions, the Canadian entity must perform actual negotiation, risk management, operational oversight, and invoicing functions. For foreign affiliate dividends under subsection 113(1), the offshore affiliate must carry on an active business in a treaty country and derive substantially all income from active business operations; otherwise FAPI inclusion applies to the Canadian parent.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act, section 239; Criminal Code, section 380)

Statutory sanction: Indictable offence: a fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to five years. Summary conviction: a fine up to CAD 25,000 and/or imprisonment up to one year. Civil penalties include the gross negligence penalty of 50% of the misreported tax, late-filing penalties, and compounded daily interest. The CRA may also seek a court order prohibiting the person from managing a corporation or obtaining licenses.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions or GST/HST under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act and section 323 of the Excise Tax Act. For corporate tax fraud, the corporate veil may be pierced under section 380 of the Criminal Code, resulting in orders for restitution of unpaid taxes, interest, and penalties plus the costs of prosecution. A director who knowingly participates in the evasion may be personally subject to fines and imprisonment, as well as a Canadian criminal record; travel bans (asset forfeiture orders under the Criminal Code) and passport surrenders can be imposed. Courts have also issued injunctions freezing personal accounts.
False Statements / Misrepresentation (ITA sections 162, 163(2), 239(1)(a); Criminal Code section 380(1)(a))

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax, plus interest calculated under section 161. A repeated failure to file information returns can attract penalties of up to CAD 12,000; false statements in information returns can attract a penalty of CAD 1,000 per statement. Criminal prosecution for tax evasion (taxpayer) is punishable by imprisonment up to five years and a fine not exceeding 200% of the amount evaded.

Director personal liability: Directors and signing officers of the corporation who sign the T2 return can be assessed personally for negligence penalties if they fail to exercise due diligence; in the event of fraud-based misrepresentations, the Corporate Veil is pierced and restitution is payable from personal assets. Attorney General may seek a travel ban or prohibit an officer from board service for a fixed term in serious cases.
Transfer Pricing Penalty (ITA section 247(3))

Statutory sanction: Penalty equal to 10% of the net adjustment amount (minimum CAD 5,000; maximum CAD 500,000), in addition to reassessment and interest. The CRA may also recharacterize a transaction under subsection 247(2), deny deductions, or assess a deemed dividend under subsection 214(3).

Director personal liability: Directors are generally not personally liable for transfer pricing penalties where they act in good faith. If the CRA establishes fraud, sham, or a deliberate arrangement to deceive, the directors who designed or benefitted from the scheme may be jointly liable for the penalty as initiators of the avoidance transaction; they may face separate criminal prosecution and be ordered to pay restitution.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance (GAAR section 245; treaty abuse provisions)

Statutory sanction: GAAR denies the tax benefit (subsection 245(2)) and imposes an additional tax of 25% of the denied benefit (subsection 245(6)), together with reassessment and interest. The CRA may treat a purported arrangement as truly a sham and reassess the Canadian entity as carrying on a business in Canada. Penalties for false returns and negligence can also apply. In egregious cases, a criminal investigation for aiding tax evasion may ensue.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held jointly liable with the corporation for the tax benefit denied under GAAR if they personally participated in the scheme and the corporate veil is pierced; courts have awarded personal restitution and issued asset freezes. If criminal fraud is alleged, bail conditions may include travel bans.
Anti-Money Laundering Violation (PCMLTFA; Criminal Code section 462.31)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 for a corporation and CAD 500,000 for an individual per violation. A criminal conviction for laundering proceeds of crime carries a maximum of 10 years' imprisonment. Property derived from laundering is subject to forfeiture under the Criminal Code. FINTRAC may publish notices of violation and the transportation regulator may revoke the air cargo broker license.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable for AML penalties if they knowingly, or with negligence, failed to implement the required policies. Under section 462.31(2), individual officers who direct, authorize, or participate in the laundering are liable to imprisonment, fine, or both. Senior officers may face travel bans and disqualification from holding an office or directorship; personal assets may be frozen under restraint orders; a criminal record may bar entry to many jurisdictions.
Dividend Withholding / Part XIII Non-Compliance (ITA sections 212, 215, 227)

Statutory sanction: The payer corporation is liable for the unremitted tax under section 215(1), plus daily compounded interest under section 161(1), penalties under section 227(9) or 227(10), and possible criminal prosecution. The CRA may assess the non-resident recipient directly and the payer remains jointly liable.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the corporation's failure to withhold and remit Part XIII tax to the extent of unremitted amounts, under subsection 227.1(1), unless they exercised the degree of care, diligence, and skill required under the circumstances. Liability extends to officers and other persons who have authority over the withholding function, and the CRA may recover by way of garnishment and execution.
Foreign Affiliate / T1134-T1135 Non-Disclosure

Statutory sanction: T1135 late or failure penalty: CAD 25 per day up to CAD 2,500, but a gross negligence claim can lead to a penalty of CAD 12,000 per failure; T1134 penalty: CAD 100 per day up to CAD 2,400. In addition, the CRA may disregard the exempt surplus designation for favourable dividend treatment, reject foreign tax credit claims, and reassess FAPI as income with interest. The failure can also trigger a criminal investigation for false statements.

Director personal liability: Officers who knowingly fail to file the information returns may be individually liable under section 162(7) to the extent of the penalty if they directed or knew of the failure; senior officers who signed a false statement under section 239 of the ITA can be personally charged. For civil recovery, a court may order restitution from directors who benefited from the avoided reporting.
GST/HST Registration and Remittance Violation (Excise Tax Act, sections 240, 276, 280, 323)

Statutory sanction: The CRA assesses uncollected GST/HST, plus penalty of 6% of the unremitted amount under section 280(1) (minimum CAD 100) if the return is late more than two days and interest on the unpaid balance; repeated failures may attract a penalty of 10%. Director liability applies under section 323; criminal prosecution is possible for intentional evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable for the corporation's unremitted GST/HST under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act, including amounts collected and not remitted, interest, and penalties. The CRA can issue a director liability assessment and levies execution, seize personal property, or impose a travel ban in criminal cases.
