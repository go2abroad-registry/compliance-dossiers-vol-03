Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; online portals: 'My Business Account' (myBA) and 'Business Registration Online' (BRO) at canada.ca.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Obtain a federal or provincial incorporation number from Corporations Canada (CBCA) or the applicable provincial registry. Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) by using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1, simultaneously registering for the corporate income tax account (RC), the GST/HST account (RT), and payroll (RP) or import/export (RM) programs as needed. Step 3: For a non-resident corporation without a permanent establishment in Canada, submit the dedicated non-resident GST/HST registration (Form GST52) if taxable supplies in Canada exceed the small supplier threshold, or elect to self-register voluntarily. Step 4: Provide supporting corporate documents (certificate of incorporation, articles, by-laws), evidence of Canadian representative or service address, and the ultimate beneficial ownership schedule; the CRA issues a BN confirmation in writing or through the business portal.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - A Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act; no pass-through election exists. Resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations are taxed only on Canadian-source employment, business, and capital gains. The federal general corporate income tax rate is 15% (subject to provincial tax rates for allocation to the province of permanent establishment).

Available election forms

No US-style 'check-the-box' regime in Canada. Available statutory elections include: (i) subsection 85(1) rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of property to a taxable Canadian corporation; (ii) subsection 88(1) winding-up election for wholly-owned subsidiaries; (iii) subsection 216(1) election for non-residents filing annual returns on net rental income; (iv) section 156 of the Excise Tax Act (GST/HST seamless closely-related corporation election); and (v) various provincial capital tax / mining tax elections. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (section 18.4 et seq.) apply automatically by deeming provisions and do not provide an opt-in/opt-out election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 cumulative taxable supplies in Canada over any four consecutive calendar quarters (including online sales of goods to Canadian residents by non-resident suppliers). A person making less than CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies is a 'small supplier' and may refrain from registering, except where certain zero-rated supplies are involved or voluntary registration is chosen.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods (including musical instruments and studio audio gear) that are exported by the supplier from Canada are zero-rated under section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act. Zero-rating requires evidence that legal ownership or possession has passed in Canada and that the goods are shipped directly outside Canada (by the supplier or the purchaser's foreign agent). Related services, such as freight, handling, or arranging, supplied to a non-resident in respect of exported goods may also be zero-rated under other provisions of Schedule VI. A GST/HST-registered exporter may claim input tax credits (ITCs) for tax paid on inputs and apply for cash refunds through periodic GST/HST returns.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or CBSA electronic export declaration via Canadian Export Reporting System / GSTR)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (air) showing foreign destination and consignee
  • Commercial invoice describing items, quantities, sale price, and export terms
  • Packing list
  • Evidence of payment and foreign exchange repatriation, including Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank/FX acknowledgment confirming receipt of export proceeds in CAD or convertible currency

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping system. Electronic GST/HST returns via NETFILE/TELEFILE and corporate T2 returns via EFILE are mandatory for entities with annual revenue exceeding CAD 1 million. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Service (launched May 2024, Peppol-based) is voluntary and does not trigger tax clearance obligations.

The CRA and CBSA may audit export transactions and require documented proof of physical export, including shipment tracking, customs clearance records, and financial evidence. Unsupported zero-rating claims are reassessed at the applicable GST/HST rate (5% federal or 13%-15% harmonized) plus interest and penalties. Gross negligence penalties under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act amount to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the tax evaded; willful evasion may lead to criminal prosecution under section 327 of the ETA. For income tax purposes, subsection 163(2) imposes penalties of 25% (gross negligence) or 50% (misrepresentation) of any unreported export income. The CRA may also claw back input tax credits and deny refunds where export documentation is incomplete or fraudulent.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible and freely floating under IMF Article VIII. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are liberalized without surrender, licensing, repatriation, or profit-remittance approval requirements. The only material remittance frictions are fiscal: Part XIII Canadian withholding tax of up to 25% on cross-border dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees, generally reduced to 5% to 15% under the Canada–US and Canada–UK income tax treaties, and a Part XIV branch tax of 25% on a Canadian branch's excess taxable amount, subject to statutory exemptions and treaty relief. E-commerce retail receivables from the sale of musical instruments and studio audio gear are ordinary business income and may be freely converted into CAD.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement exists in Canada. There is no mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds, no repatriation timeline, and no designated foreign-currency account rule. An ISIC 4791 exporter of musical instruments and studio audio gear may retain 100% of foreign-currency receipts in CAD or USD operating accounts at any Schedule I Canadian bank or that bank's US or foreign affiliate. The only compliance obligation is informational: wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC as international electronic funds transfers, and the financial institution files the report under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); formally responsible for monetary policy and currency issuance. The historical exchange-control regime administered by the Foreign Exchange Control Board was repealed in 1952, and Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII status since 1951. There is no ministerial exchange-control approval authority. Banking prudential supervision is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while cross-border financial transaction monitoring is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Viable only as a settlement adjunct, not as a primary treasury anchor. Canada does not recognize the European 'electronic money institution' (EMI) licence. Foreign EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, and Revolut Business may operate lawfully in Canada as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, providing cross-border payment and e-money services without holding a Bank Act banking charter. They cannot accept Canadian deposits in the manner of a bank, are not members of Payments Canada, and do not benefit from Canadian Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) protection; balances are segregated pre-funded pools. For an ISIC 4791 online retailer targeting Stripe and PayPal rails, the compliant structure is: Schedule I bank accounts for liquidity concentration, tax, payroll, and CAPEX; EMI accounts limited to small-value vendor disbursements in exotic corridors and to avoid USD–CAD conversion drag.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% (Part I federal general corporate income tax rate, after 10% provincial abatement and 13% general tax reduction from the 38% basic rate; Canadian-controlled private corporations on active business income benefit from a 9% federal rate up to the CAD 500,000 small business deduction limit)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% (Part XIII Income Tax Act). Treaty rates under the Canada-US DTA: 5% for a US corporation owning at least 10% of the voting stock of the Canadian payer; 15% for other US shareholders. Certain portfolio dividends paid to arm's-length foreign companies may benefit from a 10% or 15% treaty-reduced rate depending on the specific DTA.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

The T2 return must be filed within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. For a CCPC with eligible business income and taxable capital of not more than CAD 15 million, the balance-due date is three months after the tax year-end; all other corporations must pay any resulting balance within two months after the fiscal year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: There is no explicit statutory dollar threshold for preparing a Transfer Pricing Local File in Canada; any non-arm's-length cross-border transaction with a non-resident must be contemporaneously documented under s.247(4) of the Income Tax Act. A Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) and Master File obligation is triggered once the multinational group's consolidated annual revenues equal or exceed CAD 750 million; the CRA's TP penalty is tied to the adjustment in excess of the greater of CAD 50,000 or 10% of the arm's-length amount.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate OECD-style ESR legal framework, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) in s.245 denies tax benefits from transactions that are artificial, lack business purpose, or are sham arrangements. The concept of economic substance is also embedded in the s.125 Small Business Deduction test: income must be from an 'active business carried on primarily in Canada', requiring meaningful premises, employees, and real commercial activity. For offshore 'shell' arrangements, the CRA will disregard treaty benefits if the arrangement is motivated by tax avoidance and the non-resident is a hybrid or has no economic substance. The Canada-US DTA and BEPS minimum standards apply.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False or Misleading Statements

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) equal to 50% of the additional tax payable; criminal conviction for evasion under s.239 produces a fine of not less than 100% and not more than 200% of the evaded tax, plus imprisonment for up to five years. Restitution orders, interest, and compounding civil penalties also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in, assent to, or fail to oppose the fraudulent filing are jointly and severally liable with the corporation under s.227.1 and under common law 'piercing the corporate veil' principles. Directors face personal monetary judgments, asset freezing orders, travel restraint orders, and imprisonment up to five years. CRA may also seek director restitution from the business's assets or personal property.
Transfer Pricing / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess taxable income to the arm's-length amount. The s.247(2) transfer pricing penalty is 10% of the gross upward adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the greater of CAD 50,000 or 10% of the arm's-length amount; a minimum record penalty of CAD 1,000 per month for up to 12 months may also apply for missing documentation. Withholding taxes on the unreported payments may be reassessed and charged at 25% Part XIII, plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorise artificial transfer pricing are exposed to personal tax liability for Part XIII shortfall under s.227.1 and to fraudulent trading claims. In criminal cases, directors and officers can be fined and imprisoned for up to five years; CRA can pierce the veil to recover the adjusted tax and penalties from directors who received or issued intercompany benefits.
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance / Part XIII Defaults

Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the full unremitted amount, plus interest and penalties under s.227. The CRA can assess a director's lien against corporate and personal assets; failure to withhold is treated as a separate liability from the underlying tax. A 10% penalty accumulates on the unremitted amount (20% for repeat defaults within three years) in addition to the tax itself.

Director personal liability: Corporate directors are personally liable for unremitted Part XIII amounts under s.227.1(1), including penalties and interest. Courts will uplift the corporate veil where directors caused the corporation to pay non-residents while failing to withhold; director bank accounts may be garnished and a travel ban may be imposed in serious proceeds-of-crime cases. Imprisonment up to five years applies for willful evasion of withholding obligations.
AML / FINTRAC Reporting Breaches (Shell Money Laundering)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act up to CAD 1 million per first violation and CAD 5 million for each subsequent violation; criminal offences carry fines and imprisonment up to five years. Violations may also trigger CRA audit and the withdrawal of small business deduction benefits.

Director personal liability: Officers, directors, and compliance officers who knowingly direct or acquiesce in AML violations are personally liable for the AMPs and can be banned from acting as a director for up to 10 years. In money laundering prosecutions, directors may receive sentences of imprisonment up to 10 years; asset forfeiture orders and travel bans are commonly imposed.
