Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — official portal: canada.ca/revenue-agency; Business Registration Online (BRO): canada.ca/business-registration-gst-hst

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a Business Number (BN) by completing Form RC1, requesting both the BN and the applicable program accounts. The filing can be made electronically through Business Registration Online (BRO), by mail to the nearest CRA tax centre, or in person by an authorized representative. The BN is the statutory TIN for Canadian tax purposes. The GST/HST program account (RT prefix) is established on the same application. Registration is mandatory within 30 days after the entity ceases to be a small supplier, defined generally as exceeding CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies in a calendar quarter or in the preceding four calendar quarters. Non-resident factoring companies whose only Canadian supplies are exempt financial services are not required to register for GST/HST, but a BN is still required for other federal program accounts, customs, payroll, or income tax compliance. An authorized non-resident may also appoint a Canadian agent or representative to manage filings.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or equivalent provincial act is a separate legal taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act. Canada does not permit pass-through tax treatment for corporations. Resident corporations are taxed on worldwide taxable income; non-resident corporations are taxed only on Canadian-source income attributable to a permanent establishment in Canada or on specified Canadian property under Part XIII withholding.

Available election forms

Canada does not have a US-style check-the-box election. Available statutory elections relevant to tax registration and indirect tax status include: (i) the election under section 240(3) of the Excise Tax Act to register voluntarily as a non-small-supplier for GST/HST; (ii) the election under section 216 of the Income Tax Act for non-residents to file a Part I return on any taxable Canadian-source rental income; and (iii) treaty-based relief elections available through treaty-residency certification. No hybrid mismatch election is available to reclassify a corporation as a pass-through entity for Canadian federal income tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — a person makes a taxable supply and must register if worldwide taxable supplies (other than exempt supplies, including most financial services) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the aggregate over the four previous calendar quarters. Exempt financial supplies such as accounts-receivable factoring are not included in the threshold computation.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule IX to the Excise Tax Act, exported tangible goods and certain services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated. For a factoring or trade-finance service supplied to a non-resident client, the supply is zero-rated under Part VII of Schedule IX if the service is provided to a person that is a non-resident, is not an investment plan or certain excluded recipients, and is not acquired for consumption, use, or supply principally in Canada. The supplier must retain contemporaneous evidence that the recipient was a non-resident and that the service was not consumed in Canada. Domestic factoring of receivables from Canadian account debtors is an exempt financial service, not a taxable supply, and therefore does not attract GST/HST.

  • Customs Export Declaration filed in the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or equivalent commercial export evidence for services
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or other shipping document proving physical export when zero-rating goods is claimed
  • Inward remittance certificate, SWIFT wire confirmation, or bank statement evidencing receipt of settlement in Canadian currency
  • A signed contract, purchase order, or service agreement identifying the non-resident recipient and the foreign supply

Canada has no mandatory real-time electronic invoice clearance, fiscal stamping, or government pre-approval requirement for invoices. CRA accepts electronic invoices and records provided they are complete, legible, and retained in a reliable format. Large registrants generally must file GST/HST returns electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or EDI. For exempt factoring services, simplified recordkeeping is permitted, but all invoices, contracts, and payment instructions must be retained for audit purposes.

CRA may deny the zero-rating treatment upon audit if the supplier fails to demonstrate that the goods were exported or that the services were supplied to a non-resident for use abroad. The tax may be reassessed as taxable at the applicable GST/HST rate with interest. Penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act can reach 100% for gross negligence; fraudulent claims expose the taxpayer to prosecution, fines, and indefinite reassessment periods. The standard four-year reassessment period does not apply where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default is established. Additionally, improper input tax credits claimed on an exempt or zero-rated supply base may be fully recovered with interest and penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian Dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account under Canada's IMF Article VIII obligations and the Currency Act. No exchange-control approvals, conversion quotas, or central-bank pre-screening apply to capital inflows, cross-border receivable purchases, or profit remittances. Reverse-factoring purchase price remittances and dividend distributions are subject solely to Income Tax Act Part XIII withholding (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced) and general payment integrity rules, not FX vetting.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no mandatory surrender obligation, repatriation timeline, or minimum conversion ratio for export receipts. Cross-border receivables may be settled and held in any freely convertible currency in domestic or foreign accounts with OSFI-authorized banks. For tax purposes, receivable and receivable-related balances must be translated into CAD under Income Tax Act s. 261, and unrealized foreign-exchange gains are subject to deemed-accrual rules for specified foreign-currency debt obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential supervision of banks is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and AML/ATF oversight to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no separate Foreign Exchange Administration; exchange control functions are absorbed by the federal monetary framework and the Currency Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low. Offshore digital e-money institutions (e.g., UK FCA EMIs or EU passporting EMIs) are not recognized as banks in Canada and cannot independently hold client funds or offer clearing access. Such an entity would require either FINTRAC registration as a Foreign Money Services Business and/or an authorized foreign bank licence under the Bank Act, each practically cumbersome and economically non-viable as the primary treasury rail. At most, an EMI may serve as a supplemental collection sub-channel with settlement ultimately swept to a Tier-1 Canadian bank account.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal Part I net rate for a Canadian-resident corporation (38% base less 10% provincial abatement less 13% general rate reduction).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on any dividend paid or credited to a non-resident under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, unless the applicable tax treaty reduces that rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate annual cross-border transactions with a non-arm's-length non-resident related party; exceeding this threshold triggers contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation, Form T106 filing, and, where the group is required to prepare OECD-aligned Country-by-Country reporting, a Master File and Local File requirement.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime as in certain offshore jurisdictions, but the CRA enforces substance through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245) and common-law substance-over-form principles. For trade-supply-chain factoring, the Canadian entity must demonstrate real operational substance: active decision-making, invoicing, collection, credit-risk management, Canadian bank account control, and physical or personnel presence. The OECD Principal Purpose Test, implemented through Canada's tax treaties and the Multilateral Instrument, may deny treaty benefits where a factoring or financing structure has the principal purpose of obtaining treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion — Off-Book Factoring Fees and Fictitious Bad Debts

Statutory sanction: Imprisonment up to 2 years and a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded under s.239(1) of the Income Tax Act; civil reassessment for unreported income plus 50% gross negligence penalty under s.163(2); daily compounded interest and freezing of bank accounts by the CRA.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns, authorize concealment, or fail to remit source deductions may be jointly and severally liable under s.227.1. Criminal conviction can result in restitution orders, a director ban under the CBCA, and travel restrictions or foreign-asset seizure orders.
Shell Company — Lack of Economic Substance in Cross-Border Factoring

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under the GAAR denying the intended tax benefit; denial of treaty benefits under the MLI Principal Purpose Test; transfer-pricing adjustment under s.247(2); 10% TP penalty under s.247(3); 50% gross negligence penalty; Part XIII withholding tax may be imposed on deemed dividends.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly establish or operate a shell with no substance may be held jointly and severally liable for unpaid taxes and withholdings under s.227.1; civil fraud remedies can pierce the corporate veil; asset preservation orders and travel bans may be issued when the directors attempt to relocate overseas.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Registration and Unlicensed Trade Factoring

Statutory sanction: Monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; criminal prosecution of corporations and officers with imprisonment up to 5 years; FINTRAC may impose compliance orders; CRA may assess penalties for unreported international electronic funds transfers.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may be prosecuted personally for willful blindness or failure to take reasonable care to prevent AML violations; restitution and forfeiture orders may be made; travel bans and arrest warrants can issue where the directors are outside Canada and the offense is punishable by imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation of Factoring Discount Rates and Profit Allocation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment to adjust taxable income under s.247(2); a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the adjustment (net of certain tolerance) under s.247(3); additional penalty of CAD 2,500 per failure to provide documents; interest and gross-negligence penalties if documents are deliberately falsified; Part XIII withholding may be imposed on any recharacterized distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in transfer-pricing manipulations and false reporting can be joint and severally liable for the tax amounts and penalties under s.227.1; wilful evasion may result in imprisonment, corporate-veil piercing, and orders requiring repatriation of foreign assets; travel bans and seizure of passports are available to Canadian authorities during tax fraud investigations.
