Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); Business Registration Online and My Business Account portal

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity conducting or intending to conduct business in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) with the relevant program accounts (corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and import/export) by filing RC1 'Request for a Business Number' online via Business Registration Online, or by completing the paper RC1 with supporting constitutive documents. A non-resident corporation without a permanent establishment in Canada may nonetheless require an income tax account if it files Canadian Part I tax returns, and a GST/HST account if it makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier. The CRA issues a BN immediately upon online registration; third-party authorization is obtained through Form RC59 or online rep authorization.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply is zero-rated for GST/HST purposes where the supplier exports tangible personal property or makes a supply to a non-resident who receives it and the supply is categorised under Division V of the Excise Tax Act; relevant cross-border legal services may be zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident and the service is not performed in connection with Canadian real property or goods situated in Canada. The supplier must obtain and preserve evidence establishing the export within a reasonable commercial period.

  • Inward remittance certificate, SWIFT MT103 or equivalent banking evidence of payment from the non-resident

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully liberalized and freely convertible currency regime under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52). There are no exchange control restrictions administered under the Canadian dollar (CAD). The Income Tax Act (Canada), R.S.C. 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.) (the 'Act') imposes only domestic tax compliance measures on outbound/inbound fund movements; neither the Bank of Canada nor the Minister of Finance administers a general exchange control approval system for capital account transactions. Under Part XIII of the Act, non-resident recipients of dividends, interest, rents, royalties, and management fees are subject to a statutory 25% withholding tax, subject to treaty reduction (e.g., Canada-US Treaty, Canada-UK Treaty). Profit remittance hurdles are strictly fiscal rather than currency-based: the remitting Canadian corporation must produce a valid Form T2061 (Certificate of Compliance) or a Form T106 information return in respect of transactions with non-resident related parties where applicable, plus evidence of remittance of the Part XIII tax to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) via Form R105 or electronic remittance voucher. The CRA may assess a 10% penalty on unreported T106 transactions and a daily late-filing penalty of $100 per day (minimum $1,000, maximum $24,000) for failure to file the certificate of compliance (subsection 219(4)). Capital account transactions are unrestricted; Canada reports voluntarily to the IMF under Article VIII, maintaining convertibility for current and capital account items.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory export proceeds surrender rule, no repatriation timeline, and no statutory conversion ratio. Exporters are freely permitted to hold, invest, or repatriate foreign currency receivables without Bank of Canada approval. The only applicable compliance obligation is tax-oriented: under section 39 of the Act, unrealized foreign exchange gains or losses on income account must be reported on an annual accrual basis; under the functional currency reporting regime (Part II of the Income Tax Regulations, sections 2611 to 2617), a Canadian taxpayer may elect to compute Canadian dollar equivalence using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate or a spot rate that is reasonable in the circumstances. For customs valuation purposes, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) requires value conversion at the rate in force on the date of export documentation under the Customs Act, but no surrender or conversion ratio applies. There is no 'FX intervention' or 'non-resident deposit' restriction; the Currencies, Foreign Exchange and Bank Notes Act and the Bank of Canada Act impose no surrender obligation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW for core IOLTA/trust settlement and litigation disbursement functions; HIGH only for ancillary payment processing and FX conversion. Legal constraints: Canadian law firms and litigation chambers acting in a fiduciary capacity as trustees of client funds are subject to provincial law society rules (e.g., Law Society of Ontario By-Law 9, Part III; Law Society of British Columbia Trust Assurance Rules), which require trust funds to be maintained in Canada at a 'bank or financial institution' authorized under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46) or a credit union. Offshore digital EMI licenses (e.g., UK FCA EMI, Lithuanian ECB EMI, Singapore MPI) are not recognized as 'authorized foreign bank' or 'deposit-taking institution' under the Bank Act, and trust funds deposited with an unlicensed EMI do not qualify as trust property under provincial solicitor's trust account rules. A digital EMI cannot hold Canadian client trust funds, issue IOLTA-compliant cheques, or provide deposit insurance equivalent to the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). However, an EMI may be used as a non-trust ancillary payment rail for receiving settlement funds from foreign arbitration counterparties, converting currencies, or paying foreign disbursements (e.g., expert witness fees, arbitral institution fees at ICC/LCIA/AAA-ICDR), provided the EMI account is not designated as a Canadian trust account and all client trust monies remain in a Canadian chartered bank account. Additional constraint: under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), FINTRAC reporting and KYC obligations cannot be delegated to an offshore EMI; the Canadian law firm remains the reporting entity for suspicious transaction reports (STRs) and large virtual currency transaction reports (LVCTRs).

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound routing is fully liberalized. Recommended compliant wire transfer protocol: (1) funder (foreign parent, judgment creditor, or litigation funder) initiates an SWIFT MT103 from its home jurisdiction using purpose code 'GADE' (General Capital Inflow) or, for payment of legal fees, 'LINC' (Legal fees) or 'C002' (repayment of loans) under the IMF BPM6 classification; Canadian banks do not use the US Federal Reserve purpose codes, but FINTRAC's electronic funds transfer reporting rules require the bank to include the originator name, address, account number, and beneficiary details; (2) CAD settlement clears via Payments Canada Real-Time Rail (RTR) or Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) — LVTS covers transfers above CAD 25,000 with final settlement at the Bank of Canada; (3) The receiving Canadian bank (Tier 1) credits the IOLTA trust sub-account only when the law society trust ledger matches, and credits the operating account for capital/subscription; (4) Compliance verification: under PCMLTFA, the bank must report international electronic funds transfers (EFT) of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC within 5 business days via the EFT report (Form EFT); the litigation entity may also be required to file a T106 information return if the inbound remittance is from a non-arm's length non-resident. Capital subscription (share capital) is not taxable, but the CRA requires either a T2 corporate tax return reporting the capital structure or a Schedule 50 'Share Capital' reconciliation. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: LOW for core IOLTA/trust settlement and litigation disbursement functions; HIGH only for ancillary payment processing and FX conversion. Legal constraints: Canadian law firms and litigation chambers acting in a fiduciary capacity as trustees of client funds are subject to provincial law society rules (e.g., Law Society of Ontario By-Law 9, Part III; Law Society of British Columbia Trust Assurance Rules), which require trust funds to be maintained in Canada at a 'bank or financial institution' authorized under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46) or a credit union. Offshore digital EMI licenses (e.g., UK FCA EMI, Lithuanian ECB EMI, Singapore MPI) are not recognized as 'authorized foreign bank' or 'deposit-taking institution' under the Bank Act, and trust funds deposited with an unlicensed EMI do not qualify as trust property under provincial solicitor's trust account rules. A digital EMI cannot hold Canadian client trust funds, issue IOLTA-compliant cheques, or provide deposit insurance equivalent to the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). However, an EMI may be used as a non-trust ancillary payment rail for receiving settlement funds from foreign arbitration counterparties, converting currencies, or paying foreign disbursements (e.g., expert witness fees, arbitral institution fees at ICC/LCIA/AAA-ICDR), provided the EMI account is not designated as a Canadian trust account and all client trust monies remain in a Canadian chartered bank account. Additional constraint: under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), FINTRAC reporting and KYC obligations cannot be delegated to an offshore EMI; the Canadian law firm remains the reporting entity for suspicious transaction reports (STRs) and large virtual currency transaction reports (LVCTRs).

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal basic corporate income tax rate of 15% (ITA s. 123(1)), net of the 10% federal-provincial abatement and the 13% General Rate Reduction under s. 123.4. Combined federal-provincial composite rate for a federal corporation with a permanent establishment in a province ranges from approximately 26.5% to 31% depending on the province of allocation.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to non-residents (ITA s. 212(2)).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within 6 months after the end of the corporation's fiscal period, and a fiscal period cannot exceed 53 weeks (s. 249.1 ITA). The final balance-due remittance is due 2 months after fiscal year-end, or 3 months for qualifying CCPCs with prior-year access to the small-business limit.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File) is required under s. 247(4) when the aggregate fair market value of any category of cross-border transactions (purchases, sales, rents, royalties, interest, management fees, loans) with a particular non-arm's-length non-resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. A T630 Local File schedule is attached to the T2 return at that threshold; a T631 Master File is mandatory where the multinational group's annual consolidated revenue equals or exceeds CAD 50,000,000; a Country-by-Country Report (T626) is required where group consolidated revenue reaches CAD 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations statute. The functional equivalents are: (i) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR, s. 245), which disallows a tax benefit where transactions lack bona fide business purpose and economic substance; (ii) the common-law 'central management and control' test for corporate residency, used to collapse shell-company structures; (iii) thin-capitalization limits (s. 18(4)) restricting related-party debt to a 1.5:1 debt-to-equity ratio; and (iv) the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL, ss. 18.2 and 18.21), effective for large groups, capping net interest deductions at 30% of tax EBITDA with a CAD 10M de minimis floor. A federal law corporation with no employees, physical premises, or active board oversight in Canada fails the commercial-substance fact pattern and will be denied treaty dividend rates and GAAR protection.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (False Statements in T2)

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 239(1): imprisonment up to 2 years (summary) or 5 years (indictable), plus a fine of 50%-200% of the tax sought to be evaded. CRA concurrently applies the s. 163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax, with compounded daily interest at the prescribed rate.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 for unremitted corporate withholdings; courts will pierce the veil where the corporation is the alter ego funding the evasion. Exposure includes civil restitution, asset freezing (s. 462.34 restraint orders), passport surrender and court-ordered international travel bans as bail or sentencing conditions.
Abusive Treaty Shopping / Shell Company (GAAR and Sham)

Statutory sanction: GAAR (s. 245) denies the treaty benefit; reassessment at the statutory 25% Part XIII rate; the new 25% GAAR penalty applies to abusive transactions entered into after June 13, 2023. The CRA and courts apply the beneficial-ownership and limitation-of-benefits analysis of each DTA to void the shelter.

Director personal liability: Directors who directed the sham dividend flow face Part XIII withholding liability under s. 227 agent-liability rules, civil liability under CBCA s. 118 for knowingly signing false financial statements, and personal prosecution under s. 239 where the sham amounts to false pretense.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: s. 247(2) reallocation of consideration to arm's-length values; s. 247(3) penalty of 10% of the net adjustment exceeding the greatest of (i) 10% of gross transaction value, (ii) 10% of property cost, (iii) 10% of proceeds, or (iv) CAD 5,000,000; the burden of proof shifts to the taxpayer for all contested adjustments when documentation is not produced.

Director personal liability: The s. 247(3) penalty is corporate-only, but directors who certify or prepare false transfer-pricing documentation become exposed to s. 163(2) penalties and to director-veil-piercing in civil misrepresentation proceedings initiated by CRA for recovery of reassessed amounts.
AML / PCMLTFA Compliance Breach (IOLTA Trust and Cross-Border Wire)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) up to CAD 500,000 per contravention for a corporation and CAD 100,000 for an individual; escalation to compliance orders, suspension of the IOLTA trust account, and criminal prosecution under Criminal Code s. 462.31 for laundering (imprisonment up to 10 years) and under the PCMLTFA for knowingly providing false reports.

Director personal liability: Directors overseeing the FINTRAC compliance program bear personal AMP exposure, face criminal indictment for knowing facilitation, suffer professional disbarment by the provincial law society, and risk restraint orders over personal and trust assets under s. 462.34(1).
Unremitted Statutory Withholdings (Payroll and Part XIII)

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-remit penalty of 10% of the amount not remitted; unpaid balances bear prescribed-rate compound interest and constitute a crown-priority charge under the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act; CRA can issue third-party demand letters under s. 160(3) to the non-resident recipient of transferred funds.

Director personal liability: Directors are automatically jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 for all unremitted source deductions and Part XIII withholdings, including interest, with no fault requirement—the sole statutory defense is having exercised due diligence; CRA routinely enforces by garnishing directors' personal bank accounts and refusing issuance of tax clearances until liability is discharged.
