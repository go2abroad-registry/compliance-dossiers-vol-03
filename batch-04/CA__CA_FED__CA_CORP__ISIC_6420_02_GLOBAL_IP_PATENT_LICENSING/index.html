Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Corporations Canada accepts a virtual registered office address for a federal corporation, provided that a physical street address in Canada is designated, process can be served at that address, and the province of the registered office is stated in the Articles of Incorporation. For an Intellectual Property HoldCo engaged in cross-border patent and trademark sub-licensing, a purely virtual presence carries an elevated risk of administrative and tax challenge: the Canada Revenue Agency may reassess under subsection 247(2) of the Income Tax Act (transfer pricing) or apply the general anti-avoidance rule if the entity lacks commercial substance, and treaty benefits may be denied under the principal-purpose test. Accordingly, a law-firm or corporate-service-provider registered office is compliant for incorporation purposes, but a demonstrable local commercial substance (board meeting venue, administrative office, books-and-records premises, and resident decision-makers for the IP licensing business) is strongly recommended for the intended active IP licensing model.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/account-information/business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities carrying on business in Canada or establishing a Canadian permanent establishment must obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The BN is the statutory TIN for federal corporate tax, GST/HST, payroll, and customs purposes. Application is made online through Business Registration Online (BRO), on paper via Form RC1, or by calling the CRA Business Window. Non-resident applicants may be required to provide constitutional corporate documents, director information, beneficial ownership details, and a completed Canada Revenue Agency form designating an authorized representative. Registration for a GST/HST account is mandatory once taxable Canadian supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, and a CARM importer/exporter account is required for any customs-related activity.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election analogous to the U.S. system. Entity classification is determined by corporate law and the Income Tax Act; a Canadian business corporation is automatically taxed as a corporation. For hybrid mismatch contexts, no elective reclassification is available; instead, the Income Tax Act through sections 18.2 and 18.3 denies deductions for hybrid mismatch arrangements, and Part XIII non-resident withholding applies at source to cross-border royalty payments unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies in a trailing four-quarter period (small-supplier threshold); non-residents making taxable supplies in Canada must register for GST/HST once the threshold is met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under the Excise Tax Act applies to goods and services exported in the course of commercial activity. For intangible property such as a patent or trademark licence, the supply to a non-resident not registered for GST/HST may be zero-rated when the licence is for use outside Canada and the supplier maintains evidence establishing the non-resident status of the recipient and the foreign use or exploitation of the intellectual property.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Customs Export Declaration B13 or CAED, where physical goods accompany the transaction)
  • Bill of Lading (or equivalent transport document evidencing that goods left Canada)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank-confirmed evidence of foreign currency receipt, or equivalent remittance and banking records)

Canada does not impose a mandatory federal real-time e-invoicing or digital fiscal clearance regime. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA GST/HST NETFILE or an approved certified service provider when required. Electronic invoicing and records are acceptable, but the CRA requires an unalterable, complete audit trail; no digital stamp or continuous transaction clearance is required at the federal level.

CRA may reassess and claw back zero-rated treatment when export substantiation is inadequate, requiring additional GST/HST at 5% or the applicable provincial HST plus interest. Penalties include gross-negligence penalties for false statements or omissions, fines under the Excise Tax Act, and criminal sanctions for fraudulent refund or zero-rating claims. Audit records, including the outward transfer of property, remittance evidence, and the 180-day foreign receipt evidence, must be retained for six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada scores maximum convertibility: no capital-account restrictions, no current-account convertibility controls, and full IMF Article VIII status (accepted 1994). CAD is a free-floating currency; cross-border payments are unrestricted. Regulatory hurdles to profit remittance are tax-only: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax of 25% generally applies to royalties paid by a Canadian resident payer (s. 212(1)(b) ITA), reduced by treaty, subject to CRA Form NR301/NR302. Additional compliance anchors include thin-capitalisation (s. 18(4) ITA, maximum 1.5:1 debt-to-equity on interest deductibility), anti-surplus-stripping rules (s. 212.1/246.1 ITA), and Canadian transfer-pricing documentation for intercompany royalty rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada has no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters may maintain free foreign-currency accounts. Mandatory reporting is FINTRAC-driven: electronic funds transfers (inbound or outbound) of CAD 10,000 or more within a 24-hour period must be reported under the PCMLTFA; large cash transaction reports apply at CAD 10,000+. No Bank of Canada repatriation or surrender certification is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Residual FX-control authority resides with the Minister of Finance under the Currency Act, but Canada has maintained a fully liberalised exchange-control regime since the repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951. The Bank of Canada issues CAD, manages monetary policy, and acts as fiscal agent; it does not approve or restrict individual remittances.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low as a primary or sole banking relationship; Medium as an ancillary FX/payment aggregator. Under s. 545 Bank Act, only banks and prudentially recognised entities may accept deposits or carry on banking business in Canada; a foreign EMI cannot open an operational account independently unless registered as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and licensed under provincial payment-service statutes (e.g., Ontario Required Payments Act, 2024). EMIs generally lack Canadian cheque-clearing and LVTS participation, so CAD clearing requires a sponsoring Schedule I bank. IP HoldCos should therefore use a Schedule I tier-1 bank as principal, with EMI rails limited to multi-currency receivables in non-CAD jurisdictions.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder-loan funding for the IP HoldCo is wired via SWIFT MT103 to the Schedule I operating account. The remittance description must carry a specific Canadian purpose-of-payment descriptor such as 'CA: Capital Subscription — Common Shares No-Par Value' or 'CA: Shareholder Loan — Non-Interest Bearing'. Under PCMLTFA, the receiving bank must obtain written source-of-funds confirmation from the sending bank/beneficiary owner; a board resolution, share-subscription agreement, and officers' certificate (confirming non-resident ownership percentages) should be pre-filed. No Bank of Canada approval or escrow release is required; however, if the injection is structured as an intercompany loan, disciplined documentation of principal, interest rate, and repayment plan must exist to avoid reclassification as equity for thin-capitalisation or withholding purposes.Outbound remittances for sub-licensee disbursements or upstream royalties are cleared primarily by SWIFT MT103 (SWIFT field 70: 'Royalty Payment pursuant to License Agreement dated ...') with invoice matching. For commercial trade settlements where goods or collateralised services accompany the IP, an irrevocable confirmed Letter of Credit (SWIFT MT700) provides payment assurance; the final funding leg is executed by MT103 against presented transport/documentary evidence. Canadian payers remitting royalties to non-residents must comply with Part XIII withholding: file CRA Form NR4, remit 25% (or treaty-reduced rate) electronically by the 15th of the following month, and obtain an NR301 certificate before paying at a reduced rate. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. Provincial and territorial corporate income tax generally applies in addition; the combined general rate commonly ranges from approximately 23% to 31%, depending on province or territory. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may receive a 9% federal small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, but IP-holding and non-resident-controlled structures require separate eligibility analysis.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty rates are 5% where the recipient is a qualifying corporate shareholder holding the required ownership percentage, 10% or 15% in other qualifying cases, and 15% for many portfolio dividends. The applicable treaty must be reviewed for ownership, limitation-on-benefits, anti-conduit, beneficial-ownership, and principal-purpose requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within 6 months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due 2 months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally pay the balance within 3 months after year-end if the statutory conditions are satisfied. Instalments are generally monthly, or quarterly for qualifying corporations.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold before contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required under Income Tax Act section 247. A taxpayer must make reasonable efforts to determine and use arm's-length terms for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and maintain contemporaneous documentation by the return-filing deadline. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to an ultimate parent or surrogate entity in a multinational enterprise group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, translated and administered under Canadian rules. T106 reporting generally uses a CAD 1 million aggregate threshold for reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions, subject to statutory exclusions.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance remains relevant under the arm's-length principle, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty anti-abuse rules, the principal-purpose test, the general anti-avoidance rule, sham doctrine, series-of-transactions principles, and judicial business-purpose analysis. An IP holding company should demonstrate genuine control and management of IP risks, qualified personnel or appropriately supervised outsourced personnel, decision-making capacity, development or acquisition records, enforcement and licensing functions, DEMPE-related functions, adequate capitalization, commercial agreements, and consistent board and accounting records. Legal ownership alone does not justify retaining substantial royalty profit where relevant functions and risks are performed elsewhere.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, and possible criminal fines. On conviction for tax evasion, the Income Tax Act may impose a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 2 years on summary conviction or up to 5 years on indictment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct. The corporation is normally liable for its own tax debts, but directors may be personally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under the director-liability provisions, subject to a due-diligence defence. Criminal conviction can produce restitution orders and restrictions arising from bail or court orders; a general automatic tax-related travel ban does not apply.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny or reduce deductions, impose withholding consequences, and apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory materiality conditions are met, including the applicable gross-revenue and CAD 5 million thresholds. Recharacterization, interest, and treaty-corresponding-adjustment disputes may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims by the corporation, shareholders, creditors, or insolvency representatives for knowing approval of unlawful or misleading arrangements. Personal tax liability does not ordinarily arise solely from a transfer-pricing adjustment, absent statutory director liability, fraud, knowing participation, or separate personal receipt of diverted funds.
Improper Treaty Relief and Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer may be assessed for unwithheld Part XIII tax, arrears interest, penalties, and information-return failures. Treaty relief may be denied where the recipient is a conduit, nominee, non-beneficial owner, or arrangement participant whose principal purpose was obtaining the treaty benefit.

Director personal liability: Directors may become personally liable for unremitted source deductions if statutory director-liability conditions are satisfied and reasonable diligence was not exercised. Deliberate withholding fraud may also create criminal exposure.
Shell Company and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Potential denial of treaty benefits, transfer-pricing adjustments, GAAR application, withholding at statutory rates, reassessment of deductions, penalties, and interest. A shell company is not automatically unlawful, but false records, evasion, money laundering, or abusive treaty arrangements may be criminal.

Director personal liability: Directors may face claims for breach of fiduciary duty, oppression, fraudulent conveyance, or misrepresentation. Veil piercing is exceptional and generally requires misuse of the corporation, fraud, or an improper purpose; incorporation alone does not create personal liability.
Unreported Foreign Affiliates and Offshore Assets

Statutory sanction: Late or missing T1134 and T1135 penalties can accrue daily and may be increased for gross negligence or repeated failure. Unreported foreign income may be reassessed with interest and gross-negligence penalties; deliberate concealment may result in criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for source-deduction or GST/HST debts and criminal exposure where they knowingly directed concealment. Ordinary corporate income-tax penalties remain primarily corporate liabilities unless fraud or statutory liability is established.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties may be imposed for compliance failures, and criminal offences may result in substantial fines and imprisonment. Sanctions violations can produce asset freezes, seizure, prosecution, and penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners who authorize, facilitate, or knowingly participate may face criminal prosecution, imprisonment, fines, forfeiture, and director disqualification consequences. A general automatic travel ban is not imposed, but bail, forfeiture, arrest, or immigration consequences may restrict travel.
Corporate Records, Beneficial Ownership, and Books-and-Records Failure

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, tax reassessments, denied deductions, adverse evidentiary inferences, civil fines, and potential offences may apply. CRA may impose penalties for inadequate records and inability to substantiate reported amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally fined or prosecuted for statutory corporate-record breaches and may face civil liability for negligent oversight. Personal liability is fact-dependent and is not automatically created by a records deficiency.
Thin Capitalization and Excessive Interest

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions may be denied under the thin-capitalization rules, with possible deemed dividend treatment and Part XIII withholding consequences. Transfer-pricing and interest-limitation rules may apply in addition to thin capitalization.

Director personal liability: Directors generally have no personal liability for the resulting corporate tax adjustment solely because the financing was excessive. Personal liability may arise for unpaid withholding amounts, fraudulent conduct, or knowing participation in unlawful distributions.
Unlawful Distributions and Insolvency-Duty Breaches

Statutory sanction: Recipients may be required to repay unlawful distributions; directors may face statutory indemnity and contribution claims, creditor remedies, oppression remedies, or insolvency claims. Tax and withholding assessments remain payable with interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for certain unpaid source deductions, GST/HST, wages, and unlawful distributions, subject to statutory defences. Civil restitution and contribution orders are possible; imprisonment generally requires separate criminal conduct.
