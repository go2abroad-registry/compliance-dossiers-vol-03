Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the CBCA. Incorporators may subscribe for shares of any par or no-par value for nominal consideration; no escrow account or capital deposit is required. Full consideration must be paid or promised before shares are issued.

Funding & unlock path: Obtain fire-safety inspection and clearance certificate under the applicable provincial Fire Code (NFPA 101 life safety and NFPA 13 sprinkler design where triggered); structural load inspection and engineering certification for mezzanine and multi-tier racking systems under the applicable Building Code Act; workplace clearance under the applicable provincial Occupational Health and Safety Act (hazard identification, machine guarding, WHMIS signage, first-aid kits); and mandatory workers' compensation board coverage for all warehouse, fulfilment and administrative employees.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the CBCA. Incorporators may subscribe for shares of any par or no-par value for nominal consideration; no escrow account or capital deposit is required. Full consideration must be paid or promised before shares are issued.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; online portal: My Business Account (CRA), with GST/HST NETFILE; business registration through Business Registration Online (BRO)

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities must first incorporate a federal or provincial Canadian corporation (or complete extra-provincial registration), then obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST program account (RT). Registration is effected through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts). A non-resident corporation is required to register for GST/HST if it carries on business in Canada or otherwise makes taxable supplies exceeding the small-supplier threshold. The BN is used for corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, import/export, and other program accounts.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — Foreign entities must first incorporate a federal or provincial Canadian corporation (or complete extra-provincial registration), then obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST program account (RT). Registration is effected through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts). A non-resident corporation is required to register for GST/HST if it carries on business in Canada or otherwise makes taxable supplies exceeding the small-supplier threshold. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box or hybrid mismatch election is available: a Business Corporation is automatically a separate taxable entity under the Income Tax Act. Relevant filing elections include the election for closely related corporations to file a combined GST/HST return (Form GST25), election of a GST/HST fiscal year or filing frequency, and any applicable federal or provincial corporate allocation/waiver mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies over four consecutive calendar quarters, or more than CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter; registration is required once the threshold is exceeded. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada may be required to register even if below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for subscription-box goods exported from Canada applies when tangible personal property is supplied for export to a customer outside Canada and the supplier either exports the goods directly or delivers them to a common carrier engaged to export them. The supplier must retain documentary proof that the goods were shipped from Canada to a foreign destination, including destination on the bill of lading/waybill and the commercial invoice. Related services may also be zero-rated where supplied to a non-resident and not performed in Canada, or where they otherwise qualify under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act.

  • Customs export documentation (CBSA Form B13A or equivalent Canada Customs export record for the shipment)
  • Commercial invoice describing goods, value, parties, terms, and export destination
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill issued by a common carrier showing shipment from Canada to the foreign customer
  • Proof of export / carrier delivery confirmation (POD, customs stamp, or electronic tracking record)
  • Payment evidence applicable to the foreign customer (bank statement, SWIFT/remittance advice, or card settlement record)

No real-time national electronic invoicing or fiscal clearance mandate is currently imposed by the CRA. GST/HST registrants must supply invoices and sales receipts containing prescribed information (supplier name and BN, date, description of supply, tax charged or zero-rated status) and maintain adequate books and records. Electronically kept records must be made available in readable format to the CRA on request; no third-party clearance or API digital stamping is required.

CRA will reassess zero-rated export claims if documentation is incomplete or inconsistent; reversal of zero-rating subjects the supply to GST/HST at the applicable rate (5% federal GST, or HST rate if goods are not actually exported), plus interest. Civil penalties under Excise Tax Act section 285.1 may reach the greater of CAD 500 and 25% of the net tax in issue, with higher exposure for knowing misstatement or gross negligence. The normal four-year reassessment window may be extended where misrepresentation is found, and fraudulent export-claim conduct can attract criminal prosecution and loss of relief programs.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible and Canada is compliant with Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement; there is no legal distinction between current-account and capital-account convertibility, and no exchange-control licensing exists. Dividends, management fees, royalties, and loan repayments to non-residents may be remitted freely without Central Bank approval, subject only to CRS/FATCA reporting and corporate law solvency tests. The Investment Canada Act requires a simple notification for the establishment of any new Canadian business by a non-Canadian, and a competitive review only for large control acquisitions of Canadian entities that meet prescribed financial thresholds, which are irrelevant for a subscription-box service corporation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. CAD or foreign-currency export proceeds may be held in domestic or offshore accounts at the corporation's discretion. Export documentation (commercial invoices, waybills, and bank credit advice) is retained solely for income-tax and GST/HST verification, and zero-rated export treatment under the Excise Tax Act applies to the physical subscription-box supply, enabling input tax credit recovery.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange administration is coordinated with the Department of Finance Canada, while anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing oversight of money services businesses and financial institutions is exercised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium-High viability with structural constraints. Canada does not issue a distinct electronic money institution (EMI) banking licence; non-resident digital payment providers must register as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and, when providing retail payment services to Canadians, must register as Retail Payment Activity Providers (RPAPs) with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act. Offshore EMIs such as Wise Business and Payoneer may be used for cross-border payables and receivables through their Canadian Schedule I bank partner rails, but they cannot accept conventional deposits and are not substitutes for a primary corporate operating account at a domestic bank.

No Bank of Canada exchange-control approval is required; the receiving corporation must file the Investment Canada Act establishment notification within 30 days of commencing business, complete Statistics Canada direct-investment survey reporting when thresholds are reached, and maintain supporting agreements, board resolutions, and bank credit advices for FINTRAC and CRA audit purposes. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal Part I headline rate 38%; after 10% provincial abatement and 13% general rate reduction, net federal rate 15%; CCPC small business deduction reduces federal rate to 9% on active business income up to C$500,000.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders, subject to reduction by an applicable comprehensive income tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: mandatory for Canadian taxpayers with annual cross-border related-party transactions exceeding C$1 million in aggregate. Master File: mandatory when the multinational group's annual gross revenue exceeds C$50 million or where cross-border related-party transactions exceed the C$1 million per-party threshold and the group is above the Master File size threshold. Country-by-Country Report: mandatory when consolidated group revenue exceeds C$850 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the Caribbean Economic Substance Regulations or EU ESR directives. However, the CRA requires real economic substance for intercompany transactions under s.247 and the GAAR in s.245. Transactions and structures that exist only to shift profits away from the Canadian subscription-box business, such as a non-resident IP shell with no employees, assets, or functions, will be disregarded, recharacterized, or adjusted to reflect the arm's length allocation of profit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Understatement of Revenue

Statutory sanction: Reassessment; gross negligence penalty under ITA s.163(2) of 50% of the understatement; criminal prosecution under ITA s.239(1) with a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for up to 2 years; court-ordered restitution and publication of conviction.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in, direct, or authorize revenue understatement may be held personally liable in civil fraud; CRA may pursue directors' personal bank accounts and assets; after indictment, travel restrictions/bans can be imposed, and conviction may lead to imprisonment.
Unremitted Payroll and GST/HST Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: CRA assesses unremitted GST/HST under ETA s.323 and source deductions under ITA s.227.1; interest and penalties compound daily; CRA may issue garnishment on receivables (Stripe, payment processors) and register a security interest against all property of the corporation.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted trust amounts regardless of corporate intent; personal bank accounts and real estate may be seized; travel bans and denial of tax-clearance certificates (e.g., for immigration or government contracts) apply while balances are outstanding.
Transfer Pricing Abuse / Unjustified Intercompany Royalties

Statutory sanction: Adjustment under ITA s.247(2) to the arm's length result; transfer pricing penalty under s.247(3) equal to 10% of the understatement/overstatement if the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts to determine arm's length values; interest and gross negligence penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who execute sham royalty arrangements without commercial reality may be jointly liable for fraudulent or undervalued dispositions under s.160 and provincial fraudulent conveyance legislation; the corporate veil can be pierced where the entity is a mere conduit, and directors may face travel restrictions during criminal investigation.
Non-Resident Shell Company and Dividend Stripping

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize transactions under anti-avoidance rules; deemed dividends under ITA s.84; Part XIII withholding of 25% on deemed dividend treatment; GAAR under s.245; transaction penalties and interest; refusal to grant treaty relief if the non-resident shell is not the beneficial owner.

Director personal liability: Shareholders and directors may be jointly liable under s.160 for property transferred to the shell without fair consideration; personal liability extends to the amount by which value was depressed; the CRA can seize assets of the non-resident shell's beneficial owners; if criminal intent is shown, imprisonment and travel bans apply.
False T106 Information / Incomplete Transfer Pricing Disclosure

Statutory sanction: T106 late-filing/failure penalty: C$2,500 plus C$100/day, capped at C$25,000; false statements penalty under s.163(2); failure to provide documents permits CRA to make a reasonable assumption in assessing, and interest applies to any deficiency.

Director personal liability: A director who certifies or knowingly directs false T106 filings may face criminal liability for false statements; personal liability may arise under s.227.1 where withheld amounts were part of the erroneous filings; a travel ban or criminal prosecution can follow where false filings are part of a tax evasion scheme.
