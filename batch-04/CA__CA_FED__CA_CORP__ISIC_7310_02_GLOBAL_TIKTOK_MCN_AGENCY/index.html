Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); federal registration and filing portals: Business Registration Online (BRO), CRA My Business Account, Represent a Client, and GST/HST NETFILE (canada.ca/en/revenue-agency).

E-government filing portal

A federal Business Corporation must first obtain an incorporation certificate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) through Corporations Canada, after which the CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with separate program accounts (e.g., RM0001 for GST/HST, RP0001 for payroll, RD0001 for imports/exports). Non-resident entities without Canadian incorporation obtain a BN by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) by mail or fax, or through an authorized accounting representative under Form RC59; GST/HST registration is mandatory where the non-resident carries on business in Canada and is not a small supplier, and voluntary registration is available to claim input tax credits. A foreign entity with no Canadian permanent establishment is generally not required to hold an income tax account unless it has Canadian-source taxable income subject to Part I, Part XIII, or Part XIV tax.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian-resident business corporations pay federal Part I income tax on worldwide income at a base rate of 38%, reduced by the federal abatement and general rate reduction to 15% federal, plus provincial/territorial corporate tax; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada pay Part I tax on Canadian-source business income plus a 25% Part XIV branch tax on unremitted Canadian business profits; no federal pass-through classification exists for an Inc./Corp., and the Tax_Exempt_Zone designation does not apply to a standard CBCA corporation).

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; corporate classification is statutorily determined. Relevant elections for a cross-border TikTok MCN agency include: (i) functional-currency election under Income Tax Act (ITA) s. 261 for USD-denominated platform revenue; (ii) ITA s. 216(4) election for a non-resident to file net rental income and s. 217 election for deemed-resident income; (iii) ITA s. 88(1.1)/(1.2) winding-up and amalgamation continuation elections; (iv) ITA s. 256(9) election on corporate association status affecting Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) treatment; and (v) taxpayer alignment with the hybrid-mismatch rules in ITA ss. 18.2-18.3 where a US LLC or other hybrid entity is interposed in the creator-payment chain.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total taxable supplies over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA small-supplier threshold); registration within 30 days of exceeding the threshold is mandatory. A non-resident supplier of digital and distance services into Canada must register under the CRA e-commerce framework once the CAD 30,000 threshold is surpassed, and an unregistered non-resident supplying services or intangibles to a Canadian GST/HST registrant triggers reverse-charge self-assessment by the Canadian recipient under ETA Division V irrespective of the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods physically exported from Canada are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act once export is evidenced by customs and shipping documentation. Services supplied to a non-resident person - including creator incubation, MCN talent-representation fees, and ad-operations services billed to offshore TikTok entities or global advertisers - qualify for the 0% GST/HST exported-services rate unless the service (a) relates to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada, (b) is rendered to an individual physically present in Canada, or (c) constitutes advertising directed at the Canadian market. The Canadian MCN entity must therefore segregate Canadian-market campaign management from foreign-market services and maintain a substantially-all (90%) outside-Canada use and benefit analysis; zero-rating preserves full input tax credit recovery on agency operating expenses.

  • CBSA G14 Export Declaration transmitted through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED) or GFT portal, or B13A for postal exports
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill issued by the carrier
  • Commercial invoice plus service agreement or TikTok Creator Payout statement evidencing the non-resident recipient and foreign market scope
  • Bank credit advice and foreign-exchange settlement record evidencing receipt of offshore consideration (Canadian functional equivalent of an Inward Remittance Certificate)

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, no mandatory API clearance, and no digital stamping regime in Canada; the CRA requires prescribed invoice content only (ETA s. 221: supplier identity, Business Number, date, consideration, and tax), and electronic invoicing is permitted. Voluntary PEPPOL BIS 3.0 e-invoicing is promoted through the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Foundation for business-to-government adoption; registrants with taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million per year must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or GST ACCESS and remit through CRA My Payment.

Canada imposes no codified foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 180-day parameter is adopted as the working benchmark for CRA collectability and commercial-substance review of exported receivables. CRA and the Canada Border Services Agency jointly audit zero-rating claims; absent contemporaneous export evidence, supplies are re-characterized as taxable at 5% GST (or applicable HST in participating provinces) and assessed with arrears interest plus the ETA s. 285(1) gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the overstated refund claim, subject to a CAD 100 minimum. Deliberate fabrication of export documentation constitutes tax fraud under ITA s. 239 and Criminal Code s. 380, exposing the taxpayer to monetary penalties of up to 200% of the evaded tax, fines, and imprisonment for up to five years; the normal GST/HST reassessment period is four years, extended indefinitely where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII obligations; Canada maintains an entirely open capital account, imposes no exchange controls, no prior approval requirements for inbound or outbound fund transfers, and no restrictions on dividend, interest, royalty, or capital repatriation to non-residents; the only financial impediments are Part XIII withholding tax (statutory or treaty-reduced) on certain outbound cross-border payments, general AML/KYC screening, and sanctions screening; no balance-of-payments licensing exists for current or capital account transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no minimum conversion ratio; exporters and cross-border service providers, including MCN agencies, may retain 100% of foreign currency receipts in domestic or offshore foreign-currency accounts indefinitely; compliance is limited to CAD 100,000 aggregate foreign-property reporting via Form T1135 for offshore-held funds and income-stream alignment under FATCA/CRS information reporting.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central bank; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers AML/ATF obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, while the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises federally regulated banks; the Foreign Exchange Administration function in the Chinese/SAFE sense does not exist in Canada, and economic sanctions are administered by Global Affairs Canada under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate on general taxable income, before provincial or territorial corporate income tax. The combined rate generally ranges from approximately 23% to 31%, depending on province, territory, corporation type, and income classification.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to non-residents, subject to an applicable income tax treaty. Common treaty reductions include 5% where the recipient company satisfies the required ownership threshold and 15% for other qualifying corporate or individual recipients; exact rates depend on the recipient's residence, ownership, limitation-on-benefits rules, and treaty entitlement.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of each fiscal tax year. Any remaining balance is generally due two months after year-end. A Canadian-controlled private corporation meeting the statutory conditions may generally pay its balance within three months after year-end without arrears interest on the eligible balance. Monthly instalments are generally due on the last day of each month.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold that eliminates the contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation requirement for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. T106 reporting generally applies where the relevant aggregate non-arm's-length non-resident transaction threshold exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and CRA instructions. Canada does not generally impose a separate OECD-style Master File or Local File filing requirement by name. Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR systems. Economic substance remains relevant under the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty beneficial-ownership and treaty-entitlement analysis, foreign-affiliate and foreign-accrual-property-income rules, debt characterization, and the principal-purpose analysis in applicable treaties. A Canadian creator agency should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making, contracts, operational records, bank controls, service delivery evidence, and risk management consistent with the functions and remuneration claimed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction for wilful tax evasion may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years, in addition to tax, interest, and civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal liability where they participated in or authorized the conduct. Corporate status does not shield personal offenders. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction, but courts may impose release, bail, or other orders where legally justified.
Gross-negligence false statement or omission

Statutory sanction: A civil penalty is generally 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit attributable to the false statement or omission, plus tax and arrears interest. Criminal prosecution may apply where the conduct is wilful.

Director personal liability: Directors may be exposed where they personally made, directed, or knowingly approved the false filing. Civil assessment is ordinarily against the corporation, but director liability may arise under specific statutory provisions or for personal misconduct.
Failure to remit payroll or Part XIII withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted amount, penalties, and arrears interest. Part XIII failures may produce late-remittance penalties and failure-to-file penalties. Payroll source deductions are trust amounts and are subject to heightened enforcement.

Director personal liability: Under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act, directors may become jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions after statutory collection conditions, including the limitation period and CRA enforcement requirements, are satisfied. Similar director-liability rules can apply to GST/HST amounts.
GST/HST non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessments may include unremitted GST/HST, denial of input tax credits, penalties, and compound daily interest. Gross negligence or fraud may result in enhanced penalties and prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory personal liability for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts. Personal liability is not automatic for every corporate tax debt and depends on the applicable provision and statutory conditions.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may make a transfer-pricing adjustment under section 247, impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, recharacterize payments, and assess interest. Contemporaneous-documentation failures increase penalty exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal exposure for knowing participation in fraud, false filings, or breach of fiduciary duties. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessments generally remain corporate liabilities unless a separate personal-liability rule applies.
Artificial treaty access and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the 25% domestic withholding rate, assess the payer for the shortfall, impose interest and penalties, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule where its requirements are met.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly provide false residency or beneficial-ownership information may face civil penalties or criminal prosecution. Directors may also face source-deduction liability where the corporation failed to withhold.
Shell company and lack of economic substance

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include denial of deductions or treaty benefits, transfer-pricing adjustments, GAAR reassessment, residence disputes, withholding-tax exposure, penalties, interest, and reputational or banking consequences.

Director personal liability: Corporate veil piercing is exceptional under Canadian law and is not automatic merely because a company lacks substance. Directors may nevertheless be personally liable for statutory remittances, fraud, knowing participation, fiduciary breaches, or other independent wrongdoing.
Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime conduct

Statutory sanction: Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime offenses under the Criminal Code can carry severe fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment, with maximum imprisonment depending on the offense and circumstances, including up to 10 or 14 years for certain offenses.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, facilitate, possess, or knowingly benefit from laundering may be prosecuted personally. Courts may order forfeiture and impose release or bail conditions; travel restrictions can arise from criminal proceedings but are not an automatic corporate-tax penalty.
AML and sanctions control failure

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties and compliance measures may apply to reporting entities. Sanctions violations may trigger substantial administrative penalties and criminal prosecution under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may face personal consequences for directing or knowingly permitting unlawful transactions. Liability depends on the applicable AML or sanctions statute and the individual's conduct.
Payroll misclassification and creator withholding

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be reassessed for payroll deductions, CPP, EI, income tax, interest, penalties, and information-return failures. Employment standards, workers' compensation, and provincial tax liabilities may also arise.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for certain unremitted payroll source deductions and may face personal exposure for intentional fraud or obstruction. Classification must be determined from the actual relationship, not contract labels alone.
