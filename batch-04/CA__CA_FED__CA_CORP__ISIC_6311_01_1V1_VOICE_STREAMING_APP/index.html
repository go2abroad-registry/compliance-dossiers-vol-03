Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - GST/HST registration via Business Registration Online (BRO) portal

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST program account (RT) and corporation income tax program account (RC) by filing Form RC1, Request for a Business Number, through CRA's Business Registration Online portal, by mail, or by fax. For non-resident corporations, a distinct Non-Resident Corporate Tax Account registration may be required; supporting documents include the certificate of incorporation or equivalent foreign registration evidence, registered office address, principal place of business, names of directors, and authorized representative with power of attorney. The BN is issued immediately upon online registration subject to validation; account registration must be completed before filing returns or charging GST/HST.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not adopt US-style check-the-box classification. Available elections include elections under Income Tax Act s.85 for non-recognition transfers of property to a corporation, s.216 for non-resident taxpayers to file a Canadian income tax return on rental or timber income, s.256(7) for associated corporation status, and s.18.4 hybrid mismatch elections where applicable. GST/HST elections include the election to have a closely related corporation supply services without consideration under s.273, and the election for a foreign-supplier non-resident to have a Canadian agent act as solicitor under s.246. Hybrid mismatch rules in ss.18.4 and 18.5 restrict certain deduction elections for tax mismatch outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 annual worldwide gross revenues from taxable supplies made in Canada (small supplier threshold); mandatory registration applies to non-residents who carry on business in Canada through a permanent establishment, or who supply specified digital goods, services, or intangibles to Canadian-resident non-registered consumers where the CRA deems a Canadian nexus exists.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the goods are exported by the supplier or on its behalf. Services supplied to non-residents, including voice streaming and digital application services, are zero-rated when the supply is made to a non-resident that is not registered for GST/HST, the service is not performed in Canada, or the service is in respect of intangible property that is not used in Canada. The supplier must maintain documentary evidence that the recipient is a non-resident and that the supply is made from a Canadian establishment in the ordinary course of business. The zero-rating applies only if the recipient is outside Canada for GST/HST purposes and the supply is not included in the definition of 'supply made in Canada' under s.143.2.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Automated Export Declaration (CAED) or Form B13A as applicable)
  • Commercial invoice / sales contract describing the electronic service supplied
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, or carrier's receipt (for physical components, if any; for services, a performance completion certificate or log)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank transfer evidence confirming receipt of foreign currency payment
  • Non-resident status evidence (certificate of residency, foreign entity incorporation document, or contractual representation)
  • GST/HST zero-rated export substantiation worksheet (CRA Form 178, if required)

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based tax authority clearance for invoices. Electronic invoices are legally valid and accepted by CRA if they are issued in a readable format and contain prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act. There is no mandatory Continuous Transaction Control or centralized clearance system as of the current tax policy; however, taxpayers must retain complete electronic records and provide them to CRA on demand. Certain digital platform operators are subject to registration and reporting obligations under CRA's agent-of-the-ministry rules.

CRA actively audits zero-rated export claims, requiring the exporter to substantiate the actual export and receipt of payment. Failure to meet the documentary evidence burden results in denying the zero-rating, reassessment of GST/HST at the full applicable rate (e.g., 5% GST or 13% combined HST), clawback of input tax credits, and statutory interest. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include: a 5% late-payment/omission penalty plus 10% on any balance owing where a taxpayer has made a false statement or omission in a return; a further penalty of 5% on gross tax payable if the false statement is made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence, with a possible 10% penalty on the amount of tax sought to be evaded; and for fraudulent claims, civil penalties of up to CAD 25,000 plus imprisonment for up to five years under criminal evasion provisions. CRA may also assess non-resident suppliers with a 5% non-registration penalty for failure to register if required.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully liberalised capital account with no exchange controls; the Canadian dollar is freely convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. Profit remittance is not subject to prior approval; the only meaningful hurdle is the Part XIII withholding tax of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, reducible under applicable tax treaties, e.g., 5% dividends for qualifying U.S. or other treaty parent shareholders). All foreign exchange transactions are effected at market rates through domestic chartered banks.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender timeline or mandatory conversion ratio applies to export receipts in Canada. Exporters may retain foreign currency in domestic or offshore accounts at discretion, subject only to standard documentary and AML/ATF record-keeping obligations under the PCMLTFA and normal revenue recognition under the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in conjunction with OSFI as prudential regulator and FINTRAC as AML/ATF intelligence unit

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally receive the 9% federal small business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit. Provincial or territorial corporate income tax is additional and depends on the permanent establishment locations.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes are 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership and holding-period conditions. The applicable treaty must be checked for ownership, limitation, and anti-abuse requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation meeting the statutory conditions may generally have three months to pay the balance. Installments are due monthly or, if eligible, quarterly.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold that eliminates the contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation requirement under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. A Canadian taxpayer must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms and prepare documentation by the return-filing deadline; the CRA may request it. T106 reporting is generally triggered when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1 million. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 1 billion in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not impose a separate general OECD-style local-file or master-file threshold in lieu of its section 247 documentation regime.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic substance regulation equivalent to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions' ESR regime. Substance remains relevant under section 245 of the Income Tax Act general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, section 247 transfer pricing, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and common-law sham or abuse doctrines. A voice-streaming platform should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making, technology, risk control, contractual authority, data, and expenditure evidence consistent with the profits allocated to Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to the greater of CAD 500 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and criminal tax-evasion prosecution. Conviction can result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to five years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, director-liability assessments for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, and civil recovery where statutory conditions are met. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise through criminal process, bail, enforcement, or immigration consequences.
Failure to Remit Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed the unremitted amount, interest, and penalties. A failure-to-deduct penalty may generally be 10% of the required amount, increasing to 20% for repeated failures where the statutory conditions apply. Gross-negligence or knowingly participating conduct may produce additional penalties and prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions, subject to the statutory due-diligence defence and limitation periods. Criminal exposure can arise for deliberate non-remittance or false reporting.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or adjust deductions, increase income, impute an arm's-length amount, assess interest, and impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory threshold and documentation conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil exposure for knowing participation in fraudulent records or breach of fiduciary duties. Corporate separateness generally remains effective, but it does not protect individuals from criminal, tax-administration, or director-remittance liability.
Unfiled T106, T1134, T1135, or Country-by-Country Information

Statutory sanction: Fixed and daily penalties may apply depending on the form and duration of non-compliance. Failure to provide requested transfer-pricing records may suspend the ordinary reassessment limitation period and support transfer-pricing penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed for knowingly participating in false information or obstruction. Ordinary administrative penalties generally attach to the corporation, but director liability can arise under specific statutory provisions.
GST/HST and Digital Platform Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of uncollected GST/HST, interest, late-filing and other administrative penalties, denial of unsupported input tax credits, and potential gross-negligence or prosecution consequences for deliberate conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST in accordance with the Excise Tax Act, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules.
Shell Company, Sham Residence, or Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, full 25% withholding, reassessment under the general anti-avoidance rule, interest, penalties, and possible application of anti-conduit and beneficial-ownership principles.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face civil claims, tax penalties, criminal exposure for fraudulent arrangements, and regulatory consequences. A corporate veil is not a defence to personal fraud, sham transactions, or knowing participation.
Thin Capitalization, Hybrid Financing, and Interest Stripping

Statutory sanction: Denial or restriction of interest and related amounts under thin-capitalization, excessive-interest, hybrid-mismatch, and anti-avoidance rules; possible recharacterization, withholding adjustments, interest, and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil and criminal exposure where records are knowingly falsified or the arrangement is fraudulent. Ordinary tax adjustments generally remain corporate liabilities.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code, forfeiture, and sanctions-related penalties. Corporate penalties may be substantial and fact-dependent.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal charges, fines, imprisonment, asset forfeiture, prohibition orders, and regulatory disqualification. Travel bans or passport restrictions may arise from criminal proceedings but are not automatic.
Books, Records, and Revenue Recognition Failure

Statutory sanction: Requirement to produce records, reassessment using available information, denial of deductions or credits, penalties for failure to keep adequate records, interest, and possible prosecution for obstruction or false books.

Director personal liability: Directors may face liability for obstruction, destruction of evidence, fraud, or knowing authorization of false records. Bankruptcy and insolvency proceedings may create additional recovery risks.
Payroll and Worker Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Retroactive source deductions, CPP and EI assessments, interest, penalties, payroll information-return penalties, and employment-law claims. Misclassification may also affect GST/HST and provincial obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted payroll deductions and may face personal exposure for intentional payroll fraud.
