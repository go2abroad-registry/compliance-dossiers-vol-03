Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary commercial and capital transactions. There is generally no approval requirement for foreign-currency borrowing, equity funding, dividends, royalties, loan repayment, acquisition payments, or repatriation of after-tax profits. Banks may require source-of-funds evidence, beneficial-ownership information, transaction purpose, sanctions screening, tax documentation, and corporate authorization. Non-resident ownership is generally permitted. Outbound payments remain subject to ordinary tax withholding, transfer-pricing, thin-capitalization, shareholder-benefit, sanctions, AML, customs, and contractual controls. Canadian residents must report specified foreign property on Form T1135 where applicable; large corporations may have cross-border information-reporting obligations on Form T106 and related tax filings.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general deadline requiring exporters to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars. An NVOCC or freight-forwarding corporation may retain legitimate foreign-currency balances, subject to its bank's account terms, AML monitoring, accurate books and records, revenue recognition, foreign-exchange accounting, GST/HST treatment, and applicable customs and transportation records. Export proceeds must be reported for Canadian income-tax purposes in accordance with Canadian tax-accounting rules, with foreign-currency amounts translated using an accepted and consistently applied methodology.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general exchange-control administration or capital-account surrender regime. Financial institutions and payment intermediaries are also subject to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), federal prudential supervision by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and applicable provincial securities and derivatives regulation.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary payment operations and Low to Medium as a primary Canadian treasury bank. Canada does not use a single European-style electronic-money-institution license. A Canadian payment service provider conducting in-scope payment functions may require registration with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act and must comply with operational-risk, safeguarding, reporting, and related requirements when the regime applies. A foreign EMI may be unable to provide Canadian deposit-taking or full banking services without appropriate Canadian authorization and may use a Canadian sponsor bank or correspondent. Offshore EMI balances are not Canadian deposits, may not benefit from Canadian deposit insurance, and can face enhanced AML, sanctions, source-of-funds, foreign-account, data, settlement-finality, and account-closure risks. The corporation should retain a regulated Canadian bank as the primary operating, payroll, tax, and liquidity account provider.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a Canadian corporate bank account by SWIFT wire or another bank-supported international transfer. Use a payment narrative identifying the Canadian corporation, investor, legal nature of the payment, and transaction reference, such as paid-up share capital or shareholder loan. Canada has no universal statutory foreign-exchange purpose-code system for ordinary corporate wires; the sending and receiving banks may nevertheless require internal purpose classifications, source-of-funds details, beneficial-owner information, and supporting documents. Provide the executed share-subscription agreement or loan agreement, board and shareholder resolutions, capitalization table, investor KYC, originating-bank evidence, and expected use of proceeds. Record the receipt in the corporation's books, reconcile it to the share issuance or debt ledger, and ensure corporate-law filings, tax reporting, withholding analysis, transfer-pricing documentation, and thin-capitalization analysis are completed. Do not route capital through employee or director personal accounts. For large international electronic transfers, financial institutions may have FINTRAC reporting and recordkeeping obligations; these are bank obligations and do not eliminate the corporation's documentation duty.Use bank-controlled SWIFT customer credit transfers, commonly represented by MT103 in legacy messaging or the applicable ISO 20022 customer-payment message, for carrier invoices, port charges, freight agents, insurance, customs-related costs, and other documented trade expenses. Each payment should identify the beneficiary, invoice, shipment or bill-of-lading reference, vessel or port where relevant, currency, ultimate payer, and commercial purpose. Use a commercial letter of credit, preferably an irrevocable documentary credit subject to UCP 600, where supplier risk, jurisdictional risk, or documentary control warrants it. Supporting documents may include commercial invoice, bill of lading, packing or cargo documentation, insurance certificate, certificate of origin, customs documentation, and inspection certificate. Apply sanctions, export-control, anti-boycott, anti-bribery, and counterparty screening before release. Use separate customer-receipt and corporate-operating ledgers where the corporation receives funds for onward settlement. ACH or EFT is suitable for domestic Canadian payments; wires or bank-supported correspondent transfers are generally preferable for urgent or high-value international trade payments. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for goods transactions; CAD 500,000 for services, interest, royalties, and other transactions (per year, per related party). If thresholds exceeded, a complete Transfer Pricing Documentation (Local File and Master File) must be prepared and filed within 6 months of the fiscal year-end.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the UAE, but the CRA applies the 'general anti-avoidance rule' (GAAR) and substance-over-form doctrines. For treaty benefits, the 'beneficial ownership' requirement and the 'limitation on benefits' (LOB) provisions in treaties (e.g., Canada-US) require substantive business presence and economic activity. A shell company with no real operations may be denied treaty relief and subject to reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act, s. 238, 239)

Statutory sanction: Criminal penalties: fine up to 200% of tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to 5 years; civil penalties: 50% of tax evaded (gross negligence penalty) plus interest; CRA may also impose a 10% penalty for repeated failure to report income

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate tax (including withholding tax) under s. 227.1 of the Income Tax Act if they fail to exercise due diligence; civil restitution, travel bans (via CRA's 'tax evasion' list), and imprisonment up to 5 years for criminal conviction
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may disregard the entity under GAAR, reassess income to the beneficial owner, impose penalties (up to 50% of tax avoided), and deny treaty benefits; in severe cases, criminal charges for tax evasion may apply

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for taxes owed if the corporation is a sham; they may face civil penalties and potential criminal prosecution for aiding in tax evasion
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty: 10% of the net adjustment (if the adjustment exceeds the greater of CAD 5,000 or 10% of gross revenue); interest on reassessed tax; potential double taxation if not corrected

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in the transfer pricing mispricing; CRA can pursue directors for unpaid corporate taxes and penalties
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of the amount not withheld (if not remitted on time); interest on the unpaid amount; the payer is liable for the full amount of tax that should have been withheld

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for unremitted withholding tax under s. 227.1; they may be subject to civil collection actions, travel bans, and in extreme cases, criminal prosecution
