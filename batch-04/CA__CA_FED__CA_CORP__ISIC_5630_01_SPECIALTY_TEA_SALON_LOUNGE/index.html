Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA); primary portals are Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account, Represent a Client, and the CRA GST/HST online services. Corporate income tax is administered through the CRA using the T2 Corporation Income Tax Return. Provincial and territorial tax authorities separately administer certain corporate income taxes, retail sales taxes, employer accounts, and licensing requirements.

E-government filing portal

A corporation carrying on business in Canada generally obtains a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or Form RC1, Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts. The corporation then registers applicable program accounts, including the GST/HST account (RT), payroll deductions account (RP), import-export account (RM), and corporation income tax account (RC), as required. A non-resident corporation may obtain a BN and GST/HST account through the CRA non-resident registration process, Form RC1, or an authorized representative. The CRA may require incorporation documents, constitutional documents, registered office information, directors, authorized representatives, Canadian business activities, turnover information, banking evidence, and proof of identity. The corporation must separately obtain a federal or provincial corporate registration and any applicable provincial tax account. A BN is not itself a provincial corporation number, a GST/HST registration number, or a personal tax identification number. A non-resident corporation that carries on business in Canada may also have a Canadian income tax filing obligation and may be required to file a T2 return even where treaty relief is claimed.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for corporations. A Canadian corporation is ordinarily taxed as a separate legal entity and files a T2 return. Partnerships and certain joint ventures are generally fiscally transparent, subject to their legal and factual characterization. A corporation may qualify for specific statutory regimes or elections, including small business deduction treatment for a Canadian-controlled private corporation, scientific research and experimental development treatment, capital dividend account elections, subsection 85 rollover elections, section 86 reorganizations, amalgamation and wind-up provisions, and GST/HST elections such as elections concerning closely related corporations, taxable supplies of real property, and joint ventures where statutory conditions are satisfied. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions or impose inclusion where arrangements produce deduction/non-inclusion or duplicate deduction outcomes involving foreign jurisdictions. These rules are not an elective entity-classification regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding supplies of financial services and certain other excluded amounts, measured under the small-supplier rules. The threshold generally applies over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. A supplier exceeding the threshold in a single calendar quarter generally must register from the effective date specified by the GST/HST rules; a supplier exceeding the threshold over four consecutive quarters generally must register after the applicable grace period. Voluntary registration is permitted in many cases. The applicable consumer-facing rate may be 5% GST or a combined HST rate, generally 13% or 15% depending on the participating province. Provincial sales tax registration thresholds and rules are separate.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST/HST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or transfer outside Canada and sufficient evidence that the goods were exported. Goods supplied to a non-resident in Canada are not automatically zero-rated merely because the purchaser is non-resident. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated only where the applicable place-of-supply and non-resident conditions are met; numerous exclusions apply, including services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, and certain personal, legal, financial, and performance services. Restaurant, lounge, catering, admission, accommodation, and other services physically performed or consumed in Canada will generally remain taxable and cannot be zero-rated solely because the customer is foreign. A GST/HST registrant may claim input tax credits for eligible GST/HST incurred in making zero-rated supplies, subject to documentary support, statutory timing, and registration requirements.

  • Commercial sales invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, price, currency, and terms
  • Export declaration filed through the Canada Border Services Agency Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS), where an export declaration is required
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or other carrier documentation showing shipment from Canada to a destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and shipping records identifying the exported goods
  • Proof of delivery or importation in the destination jurisdiction, where available
  • Purchase order, contract, customer correspondence, and evidence that the goods were supplied for export
  • Customs broker records and destination-country import documentation, where available
  • Payment evidence, including bank records or payment-processor records, where relevant
  • For exported services: written contract, customer residency evidence, service deliverables, service-location records, invoices, and evidence that the service satisfies the applicable GST/HST zero-rating provision
  • GST/HST invoices and accounting records supporting the zero-rated treatment and related input tax credits

Canada has no general nationwide real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance mandate and no universal digital tax-stamping requirement for ordinary business-to-business or business-to-consumer invoices. Registered suppliers must issue invoices or receipts containing information required to support GST/HST accounting and customer input tax credits, including supplier identity and GST/HST registration number where applicable, date, description, consideration, tax amount or applicable rate, and prescribed credit-document information. Electronic records and invoices are generally acceptable if reliable, accessible, readable, and retained for the statutory period. Electronic filing is mandatory for certain taxpayers and may be required or effectively necessary for particular CRA programs. Export declarations are administered through the Canada Border Services Agency, principally through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS); customs reporting is distinct from GST/HST invoice clearance. A specialty tea lounge must also assess provincial point-of-sale, cash-register, record-retention, and payment-reporting requirements.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange repatriation requirement or universal statutory deadline requiring export proceeds to be brought into Canada within 180 days. Zero-rating must nevertheless be supported by contemporaneous documentary evidence and the supplier bears the burden of establishing entitlement. The CRA may reassess GST/HST, deny input tax credits or refunds, charge arrears interest, and impose applicable failure-to-file, false-statement, gross-negligence, or other penalties. Gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the understated net tax or overstated rebate or credit attributable to the conduct. Fraudulent or wilfully false records may result in prosecution under the Excise Tax Act, the Income Tax Act, the Customs Act, or the Criminal Code, including fines and imprisonment. False or materially incomplete customs declarations may also trigger Canada Border Services Agency Administrative Monetary Penalty System sanctions, seizure, reassessment of duties and taxes, and criminal enforcement. A reassessment can recover improperly zero-rated output tax and related credits for the applicable audit and reassessment period, subject to statutory limitation rules and extended periods for misrepresentation or fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible currency under a floating exchange rate regime. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible for both current account transactions (trade in goods and services, dividends, interest, royalties) and capital account transactions (direct investment, portfolio flows, repatriation of capital). There are no foreign exchange controls, no requirement for Central Bank approval for profit remittance, and no restrictions on the conversion of CAD into foreign currency. However, businesses must comply with anti-money laundering (AML) record-keeping and reporting obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) administered by FINTRAC. Dividends and branch remittances to non-residents are subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax (typically 25% under Part XIII, subject to treaty reduction) which is administered by the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), not the central bank. For a specialty tea salon lounge with a Canadian federal corporation exporting tea products or paying cross-border royalties, the practical verification requirements are driven by banking counterparties' own KYC policies and Canada's beneficial ownership transparency regime (Corporations Canada beneficial ownership register), not by exchange control permits.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement in Canada to surrender export proceeds to the Bank of Canada or any government authority. Exporters are free to hold proceeds in foreign currency bank accounts in Canada or abroad, to convert them to CAD at commercial bank rates, or to use them for payments. The Bank of Canada does not impose any mandatory conversion ratio or timeline for export receipts. The only applicable obligations are accounting and tax-related: exporters must report income in Canadian dollars for CRA tax purposes using the Bank of Canada annual average exchange rate or the rate prevailing on the transaction date, maintain supporting documentation (export invoices, bills of lading, customs declarations via CBSA, and proof of receipt of funds), and comply with record retention rules (generally six years). Additionally, interbank settlement for CAD must ultimately be cleared through Payments Canada (the Lynx high-value payment system for large-value CAD wire transfers), but no surrender or conversion mandate applies at the transaction level.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada / Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) / Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium/high feasibility with legal constraints. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Revolut Business, Payoneer, or Airwallex may provide multi-currency IBAN-equivalent accounts or local CAD settlement accounts. Such providers are legally permitted to service Canadian businesses only if the Canadian entity is assessed against FINTRAC AML obligations at the Canadian level; the offshore EMI is not a Canadian-chartered bank and does not provide CDIC deposit insurance on CAD deposits. Legal constraints include: (i) the foreign EMI must not hold itself out as a Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II bank; (ii) the Canadian entity must still file FINTRAC reports independently for large virtual currency or EFT transactions if applicable; (iii) CAD clearing for an EMI's local rail is typically executed through a sponsoring Canadian bank, which adds an extra intermediary and delayed finality versus Lynx direct participant status; (iv) business bank account naming and directors' signing authority must be fully compliant. Rating: MEDIUM-HIGH for cross-border receipts/disbursements and FX conversion efficiency, but LOW for underlying legal status/regulatory protection if the EMI fails. Accordingly, the principal CAD treasury account should be at a Tier 1 Canadian bank; EMI rails may be used as secondary inbound/outbound payment accelerators for small-value trade transactions.

Defense evidence: Defend with: (i) full disclosure of the PEP status and the precise political office held or held within the last five years; (ii) evidence of legitimate source of funds/wealth (employment contract, tax returns, audited financial statements, asset sale agreements); (iii) Canadian legal counsel opinion confirming the PEP status does not undermine the transaction's legitimacy; (iv) explicit senior management approval record from the bank where required; (v) detailed use-of-funds statement showing that the PEP's investment is passive, non-control-oriented, and unrelated to state-owned entities; and (vi) consent to enhanced ongoing monitoring and periodic re-verification every 6 months.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For a federal Canadian corporation receiving inbound capital contribution from a foreign parent or non-resident shareholder: (i) use SWIFT MT103 wire transfer from the foreign corporate bank account to the Canadian corporation's Tier 1 CAD settlement account, with memo referencing 'Shareholder loan / Paid-up capital contribution' and the Corporation Canada corporate registry number; (ii) standard SWIFT purpose code should be '1100' (Foreign Direct Investment / Equity Capital) where the remitter is a shareholder; for a loan, use code '3100' (Financial Loan / Intercompany) or the receiving bank's prescribed B2/B3/B4 purpose sub-code; (iii) the Canadian receiving bank will require proof of the corporate existence and may ask for a copy of the CBCA filed Articles of Amendment/Annual Return showing authorized share capital or a shareholder resolution approving the loan; (iv) where inbound funds exceed CAD 10,000, the bank must comply with FINTRAC EFT reporting; a Large Cash Transaction Report is not applicable because it is a wire, but the bank will include the transaction in its FINTRAC suspicious transaction monitoring; (v) for amounts over CAD 50,000, the bank may request an opening balance sheet and source-of-funds declaration to satisfy its regulatory capital adequacy and AML/KYC obligations; (vi) Verify that the corporate name on the receiving account exactly matches the CBCA certificate and that the bank has a completed Form BR/BC (Business Banking KYC) on file; (vii) For dividends, ensure that a director's resolution declaring the dividend and a CRA T2/T5 filing plan is documented. CAD clearing finality is provided by Payments Canada Lynx during operating hours; large-value wires should be initiated before 3:00 PM Eastern for same-day value; otherwise, settlement occurs next Lynx business day.For outbound trade payments (import of high-end loose-leaf tea, ceramics, brewing equipment): (i) use SWIFT MT103 for direct correspondent-bank transfer from the Canadian corporate account to the foreign beneficiary's bank, ensuring the beneficiary bank is identified by a valid BIC (ISO 9362) and the beneficiary account by IBAN or national account number; (ii) where the amount exceeds approximately CAD 75,000 and the foreign beneficiary is in a country with limited correspondent relationships or where letter-of-credit (LC) terms are required by the supplier, use an irrevocable commercial standby Documentary Letter of Credit (DC) issued by the Canadian Tier 1 bank through a correspondent bank in the supplier's country; for the specialty tea trade, a Documentary Collection (CAD — Cash Against Documents) is a low-cost alternative to LC where trust exists; (iii) supply chain financing alternatives (bank payment obligation, supplier advance) are available from the Canadian bank if annual import volume exceeds CAD 1M; (iv) all outbound wires must carry the UN/CEFACT purpose code appropriate to goods purchase (typically '1231' for goods, or '9300' for services), plus the Canada Border Services Agency import entry number for customs reconciliation; (v) the disbursement must be supported by: commercial invoice, packing list, transport document (bill of lading/air waybill), and proof of the underlying trade; (vi) for regular recurring supplier payments, banks allow pre-registered beneficiary templates subject to dual-approval (maker-checker) so that disbursements are cleared without additional documents for each wire; (vii) all outbound wires in CAD clear via Lynx and are settled irrevocably in central bank money; foreign-currency CAD wires use the bank's nostro account and are settled in the foreign currency at the bank's internal FX desk with customer rate marked up; (viii) For high-value import payments exceeding CAD 100,000, the bank may require a copy of the supplier contract, and non-resident supplier's bank details must be verified via SWIFT KYC Registry (KRI) or direct confirmation to prevent fraud.Foreign Exchange Forward Contract (non-deliverable not required; CAD is fully deliverable) — a plain vanilla FX forward with a Tier 1 Canadian bank, typically available for maturities up to 12 months, requiring a signed ISDA Master Agreement or a master FX confirmation, with credit support annex if unsecured limits are exceeded.Canadian Dollar FX Swap (foreign currency vs CAD spot-forward swap) — permissible for treasury liquidity management, allowing the Canadian corporation to swap CAD received from sales into a foreign currency (e.g., CNY, JPY, TWD) for supplier settlement at a future date.Cross-Currency Swap (CCS) — permissible where the Canadian federal corporation has a long-term foreign-currency intercompany loan or foreign capital injection; available under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement with bilateral credit support; used to convert foreign-currency debt service into fixed CAD obligations.Over-the-counter Foreign Exchange Option (European-style call or put on USD/CAD or CAD/CNY, CAD/JPY, CAD/TWD) — permissible for hedging forecasted tea import costs, with premium paid upfront; not a prohibited retail forex product because the user is a commercial end-user treasury, and the transaction is executed under bilateral ISDA documentation with a qualified Canadian or global bank counterparty.Non-Deliverable Forward (NDF) — not generally needed for CAD because the Canadian dollar is fully deliverable, but permissible as an overlay instrument where the counterparty bank quotes a benchmark (e.g., offshore offshore CAD) or where the Canadian entity has an operational subsidiary in a restricted-currency jurisdiction such as Nigeria, India, or mainland China requiring USD-based settlement of local-currency exposure; in Canada's regulatory environment, NDFs are permissible for corporates with documented genuine underlying commercial exposure under bilateral master agreements.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium/high feasibility with legal constraints. Foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Revolut Business, Payoneer, or Airwallex may provide multi-currency IBAN-equivalent accounts or local CAD settlement accounts. Such providers are legally permitted to service Canadian businesses only if the Canadian entity is assessed against FINTRAC AML obligations at the Canadian level; the offshore EMI is not a Canadian-chartered bank and does not provide CDIC deposit insurance on CAD deposits. Legal constraints include: (i) the foreign EMI must not hold itself out as a Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II bank; (ii) the Canadian entity must still file FINTRAC reports independently for large virtual currency or EFT transactions if applicable; (iii) CAD clearing for an EMI's local rail is typically executed through a sponsoring Canadian bank, which adds an extra intermediary and delayed finality versus Lynx direct participant status; (iv) business bank account naming and directors' signing authority must be fully compliant. Rating: MEDIUM-HIGH for cross-border receipts/disbursements and FX conversion efficiency, but LOW for underlying legal status/regulatory protection if the EMI fails. Accordingly, the principal CAD treasury account should be at a Tier 1 Canadian bank; EMI rails may be used as secondary inbound/outbound payment accelerators for small-value trade transactions.
