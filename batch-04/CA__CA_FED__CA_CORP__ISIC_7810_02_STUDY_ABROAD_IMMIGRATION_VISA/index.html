Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html (GST/HST account registration and corporation TIN issuance via Business Registration Online / My Business Account)

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) as its corporate TIN, and if engaged in taxable supplies in Canada, register for GST/HST through CRA's Business Registration Online portal. The steps are: (1) confirm legal entity documentation and foreign registration; (2) obtain Canadian director identification if required; (3) file initial corporate income tax return and register for a BN using CRA Form RC1 or through an authorized service provider; (4) obtain a GST/HST account if making taxable supplies in Canada and not a small supplier; (5) maintain a permanent establishment in Canada for GST/HST and corporate tax purposes, or rely on treaty-eligible business presence rules. Non-resident corporations without Canadian resident directors must also file Form RC4245.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - A Canadian Business Corporation is treated as a separate taxable entity subject to federal and provincial income tax, with no default pass-through treatment. For GST/HST purposes, each corporation is a separate registrant where applicable.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a general check-the-box entity classification regime similar to the U.S. Elections available under the Income Tax Act include: (a) section 216 election for non-residents to file net taxable income on Canadian real property or resource business income; (b) subsection 216.1 election for non-resident employers; (c) joint election under subsection 150(2) for related Canadian subsidiaries; (d) GST/HST combined registration option for closely related corporations under Division V.1 of the Excise Tax Act (group registration); and (e) specified anti-hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.2 and 18.21 that disallow deductions for hybrid mismatches unless an election applies.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

General threshold: $30,000 CAD in worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies in Canada over a single quarter or trailing four consecutive calendar quarters. Non-resident persons carrying on a business in Canada with taxable supplies above this threshold must register; below threshold, optional registration available. Provincial HST rates (Ontario 13%, Quebec 14.975%, etc.) impose a harmonized provincial component where applicable.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods exported from Canada and services performed for non-residents where the supplier charges no GST/HST on the condition that the goods are exported or the service is performed entirely outside Canada. Tangible personal property exported under CRA administrative policy (zero-rated under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act) requires documentary proof of export within a reasonable time (normally 60 days of the invoice). Services provided to non-residents (zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI) must relate to real property outside Canada, or be performed wholly outside Canada, or be supply of advisory, consulting, or professional services to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and not carrying on business in Canada. For immigration / study abroad consulting services, zero-rating is available when the recipient is outside Canada, the performance of the service is outside Canada, or the service is directly in connection with real property located outside Canada.

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping for electronic invoices. However, GST/HST registrants must issue prescribed invoices (Form GST/HST 34 for GST/HST) for taxable supplies exceeding $30, and file GST/HST returns electronically if annual gross revenue exceeds $1.5 million CAD. The CRA has introduced digital reporting for certain GST/HST rebate programs, and the E-invoicing framework is being developed under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard (PCEI) using the Peppol network, but issuance remains voluntary as of the current reporting period. Registrants must retain e-invoice data and supporting records in machine-readable format for six years.

CRA audits export zero-rating claims under section 296 of the Excise Tax Act and can reassess, impose penalties under subsection 285(1) (up to $25,000 for false statements), and assess gross negligence penalties up to 25% of the amount claimed if a person knowingly makes a false statement. Where zero-rated export supplies are later redetermined to be taxable, the registrant is liable for the uncollected GST/HST plus arrears interest calculated from the original due date. Criminal penalties for fraudulent refund claims, including jail terms under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, apply to intentional misrepresentation. For goods sold in bond, failure to provide export proof within 60 days triggers GST/HST application and recovery of any input tax credits claimed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility for the Canadian dollar (CAD), which floats freely under a pure floating exchange rate regime. There is no general foreign exchange control law, no prior approval requirement for capital inflows or outflows, and no profit remittance restriction for non-resident investors. Cross-border monetary transactions of CAD 10,000 or more and wire delivery instructions are subject to FINTRAC reporting obligations. Profit and dividend remittance is subject only to normal tax withholding and corporate insolvency solvency tests.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no statutory surrender obligation, no forced conversion ratio, and no mandatory repatriation timeline on export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained in any permitted currency, used directly for working capital, or converted through any licensed financial intermediary. Standard anti-money laundering obligations apply as set out under PCMLTFA.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange oversight is exercised under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Moderate feasibility with material compliance constraints. Canada does not maintain a standalone 'electronic money institution' (EMI) licensing category; UK or EU EMIs operating on a cross-border basis may offer CAD settlement only through regulated Canadian partner banks. Any non-bank payment service provider that holds client funds, executes payment transactions, or provides money remittance must register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) once fully in force. Offshore digital EMIs without a registered Canadian MSB authorization may not legally open clearing accounts in their own name; they must execute local-liquidity arrangements via a Schedule I bank sponsor. Enhanced due diligence is required because client funds originate from multiple non-immigrant investor remittance corridors.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a 9% federal small-business rate on eligible active business income within the CAD 500,000 federal business limit, subject to associated-corporation, passive-income, capital, and other restrictions. Provincial or territorial corporate income tax is additional.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% withholding on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to a reduced rate under an applicable income tax treaty. Common treaty outcomes are 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold and 15% in other qualifying cases, but the applicable treaty must be checked individually.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due no later than 6 months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due 2 months after year-end. A qualifying CCPC may generally pay its balance by the end of the third month after year-end if the statutory conditions are satisfied. Instalments are due monthly, with quarterly instalments available to eligible corporations meeting the applicable criteria.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is generally required where the aggregate value of non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000 for the year, subject to statutory exclusions and detailed rules. A T106 reporting obligation may apply independently. Canada generally does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File requirement on every Canadian corporation. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, or to certain Canadian constituent entities required to file under the applicable rules. A Master File may nevertheless be required or exchanged under applicable international reporting and information-exchange rules.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant through the Income Tax Act's transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty residence and principal-purpose or anti-abuse concepts where applicable, permanent-establishment analysis, beneficial-ownership requirements, thin-capitalization rules, debt-versus-equity characterization, and corporate residence. The Canadian company should maintain real Canadian decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, records, banking controls, contractual authority, and commercial risk consistent with its claimed functions. An entity established only to invoice foreign customers or receive commissions may face deduction denial, treaty relief denial, reassessment, or recharacterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and deliberate false reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, repayment of tax benefits, interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, and possible criminal conviction. Tax evasion under the Income Tax Act may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorized, assented to, or acquiesced in misconduct may face criminal prosecution, restitution, fines, and imprisonment. Corporate status does not protect a person from personal liability for their own fraud or crime. Ordinary tax debt is not automatically a director debt.
Unremitted payroll deductions, GST/HST, and source withholdings

Statutory sanction: Assessment against the corporation for tax, interest, and penalties; withholding failures may attract penalties generally ranging from 10% to 20% depending on the failure and repetition. GST/HST and payroll trust offences may also result in criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under Income Tax Act section 227.1 and Excise Tax Act section 323 for unremitted payroll and GST/HST amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules. Resignation does not automatically eliminate liability for the period of directorship.
Improper treaty withholding and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the under-withheld amount, interest, and penalties. CRA may deny treaty relief, reassess the recipient or payer, and apply anti-avoidance or treaty-abuse rules.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers may face director-source-deduction liability where the under-withholding is within the statutory director-liability provisions, and personal liability for fraud, wilful misconduct, or knowing participation in a sham arrangement.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under the arm's-length principle, deny deductions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions apply, and impose information-return penalties for incomplete or inaccurate T106 reporting.

Director personal liability: Directors generally are not personally liable for a good-faith transfer-pricing adjustment merely because it is disputed, but may face liability for knowingly false records, fraud, source deductions, or tax evasion.
Shell company, sham management, or insufficient Canadian substance

Statutory sanction: Potential denial of treaty benefits, recharacterization of income, permanent-establishment or residence reassessment, denial of deductions, application of the general anti-avoidance rule, and additional tax, interest, and penalties. The incorporation itself is not unlawful; the risk arises from false characterization or abusive tax arrangements.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal civil or criminal exposure if they knowingly participated in sham transactions, fraud, or false filings. Corporate veil piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent; Canadian law does not impose automatic personal liability solely because a corporation lacks substance.
Unauthorized immigration representation and misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Unauthorized paid representatives may be barred from representation and the business may face regulatory, contractual, consumer-protection, and criminal consequences. Immigration misrepresentation can result in refusal, inadmissibility, removal, and a 5-year bar in applicable cases. Criminal offences under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act may carry substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to 5 years for certain offences.

Director personal liability: Individuals who personally advise, submit, procure, or direct fraudulent applications may be prosecuted independently of the corporation. Directors may face fines, imprisonment, restitution, professional or licensing prohibition, and civil claims from clients.
Anti-money laundering and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties may reach hundreds of thousands of dollars depending on the violation and reporting entity. Criminal proceeds-of-crime and money-laundering offences may result in forfeiture, significant fines, and imprisonment, potentially up to 10 years for indictable money-laundering offences under the applicable law.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted where they directed, authorized, or knowingly facilitated the conduct. Personal exposure includes criminal fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, restitution, and civil claims; corporate insolvency does not eliminate proceeds-of-crime confiscation.
Client-money, escrow, and trust-account misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential breach of contract, fiduciary duty, consumer-protection law, provincial licensing requirements, trust law, and criminal fraud or theft provisions. Consequences may include repayment, punitive damages, licence loss, injunctions, fines, and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and persons controlling the account may face personal restitution, fiduciary-duty claims, constructive trust orders, asset-freezing orders, criminal prosecution, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions may arise from criminal bail, immigration, or enforcement processes but are not an automatic corporate-tax penalty.
Corporate records and beneficial-ownership non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law fines, compliance orders, loss of corporate status or good standing, tax reassessment, enhanced AML scrutiny, and possible criminal or regulatory prosecution for forged or deceptive records.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for statutory offences, false certifications, obstruction, or fraud. Civil veil piercing is exceptional but may occur where the corporation is used as an instrument of fraud or the legal personality is abused.
Late filing, obstruction, or destruction of records

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, daily information-return penalties, reassessment based on available information, gross-negligence penalties, prosecution for obstruction, and adverse evidentiary inferences.

Director personal liability: Directors may be ordered to produce records and may face personal fines or prosecution for obstruction, false statements, or destruction of evidence. Imprisonment is possible for criminal obstruction or tax offences; a general automatic travel ban does not apply solely because a return is late.
