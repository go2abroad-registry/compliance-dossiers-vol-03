Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) via its official portal at www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html

E-government filing portal

Canadian federal business corporations receive a Business Number (BN) automatically upon incorporation through the federal Articles of Incorporation filing process with the CRA. The BN functions as the primary statutory tax identification number for federal income tax, GST/HST, and other obligations. Non-resident entities operating in Canada must first obtain a BN by registering as a foreign corporation before any tax filings or registrations can proceed.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — Canadian federal business corporations receive a Business Number (BN) automatically upon incorporation through the federal Articles of Incorporation filing process with the CRA. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box elections exist under Canadian law. All corporations are taxed at the entity level pursuant to the Income Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch and transfer pricing rules are governed by the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and specific anti-avoidance provisions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD in annual taxable supplies made in Canada

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST is zero-rated on exports of goods and services to non-residents outside Canada provided the supply is made to a non-resident and the goods are exported from Canada with supporting documentation.

  • Customs Export Declaration (if required), Bill of Lading or Air Waybill, Inward Remittance Certificate or proof of payment to non-resident
  • Proof of export such as shipping documents confirming goods left Canada

GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically through the CRA My Business Account portal. No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping is mandated; however, electronic filing is compulsory and digital records must be maintained for audit purposes.

Claiming zero-rating without valid export evidence may result in GST reassessment, interest charges, and penalties of up to 200 percent of the tax involved under the Excise Tax Act, with retrospective clawback of zero-rated amounts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility for both current account and capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar is a freely floating, freely convertible currency; no exchange control law restricts the purchase, sale, holding, or repatriation of CAD or foreign currency. Profit remittances (dividends and branch profits) are subject only to corporate solvency and surplus tests under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) and to Canadian withholding tax (statutory 25%, reduced to 5% under the Canada-US and other applicable income tax treaties where the beneficial owner holds at least 10% of voting shares). A federal business number (BN) and GST/HST registration must be active before dividends are wired to non-resident shareholders.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for export receipts in Canada. Exporters may retain and deploy overseas receivables in any currency. Fiscal obligations arise only under the Income Tax Act for foreign exchange gain/loss recognition upon conversion, and under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) where cash or monetary instruments exceeding CAD 10,000 entering or leaving Canada must be reported to CBSA/FINTRAC via a Cross-Border Currency and Monetary Instruments Report (CDAR).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), exercising monetary authority, with day-to-day FX regulatory oversight delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision and to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML compliance. Canada does not operate a foreign exchange control board or permit-issuing exchange control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Airwallex, and Stripe Treasury are viable as cross-border collection and payables rails for the B2B MRO e-commerce operation, but cannot serve as the primary treasury or capital accommodation account. Legal constraints: any non-bank payment service provider serving Canadian clients must either register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regulatory framework or operate under a sponsored arrangement with a Schedule I bank; the Canadian entity must still maintain a domestic deposit account with an OSFI-regulated financial institution to support CRA remittances, GST/HST filings, payroll, and CBCA solvency-compliant dividend wires.

Defense evidence: CBCA Significant Control Registry extract, certified passport and secondary ID for each UBO, 3-month bank statements evidencing source of funds, corporate organizational chart, notarized declaration of ownership, and a written business narrative confirming the non-resident UBO's role in the MRO supply chain.
High cross-border payment velocity and ticket fragmentation relative to expected e-commerce revenue profile

Root cause: High cross-border payment velocity and ticket fragmentation relative to expected e-commerce revenue profile

Defense evidence: Platform settlement reports (Stripe, Shopify Payments, or Braintree) reconciled to bank statements, export invoices with consistent ISIC 4791/NAICS 423840 descriptions, customs declarations, carrier manifests, and a documented transaction monitoring policy showing daily reconciliation and FINTRAC reporting thresholds.
Initial capital injection from a foreign related party exceeding declared minimum capital and lacking documentary trail

Root cause: Initial capital injection from a foreign related party exceeding declared minimum capital and lacking documentary trail

Defense evidence: Notarized share subscription agreement, corporate resolution authorizing the capital call, source-of-funds letter from the investor's bank, prior-year audited financial statements of the investor, and remittance instruction clearly coded as 'equity subscription – no goods or services rendered' to prevent misclassification as trade proceeds.
Sanctions or PEP screening hits on overseas MRO suppliers, logistics partners, or customer jurisdictions

Root cause: Sanctions or PEP screening hits on overseas MRO suppliers, logistics partners, or customer jurisdictions

Defense evidence: OSFI-compliant sanctions screening policy, completed OFAC/DFATD/United Nations screening logs for each counterparty, Export Development Canada (EDC) advisory confirmation, corporate indemnity letter, and a legal opinion from Canadian trade counsel confirming no prohibited dealings under the Special Economic Measures Act and the Freezing Assets of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act.
Discrepancy between beneficial ownership register and corporate filings at the federal registry

Root cause: Discrepancy between beneficial ownership register and corporate filings at the federal registry

Defense evidence: Certified copy of the CBCA annual return, updated Articles of Amendment (if any), the corporate minute book, and a side-by-side reconciliation memo identifying each shareholder of record versus each individual with significant control.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident shareholders originate SWIFT MT103 (cover via MT202) wires denominated in CAD or USD with remittance information 'Share capital subscription / Equity contribution' and the entity's registered business number (BN) in the beneficiary reference field. The account details comprise the 7-digit account number, 5-digit transit/branch number, 3-digit institution number, and the bank's SWIFT BIC. No central bank exchange control approval is required; the receiving Schedule I bank will perform a standard incoming wire review and may request a board resolution and share subscription agreement for wires exceeding CAD 150,000. FINTRAC cross-border reporting is bank-filed for wire transfers, but the company must retain the source-of-funds evidence for 5 years. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal Part I general corporate rate; combined with provincial corporate tax (ranging roughly 8% to 16%) the all-in headline rate is approximately 23% to 31% depending on permanent establishment allocation

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless reduced by an income tax treaty.

Non-residents claim credits only in respect of Canadian-source income; foreign tax credit claims are limited where a foreign withholding tax is borne by the Canadian branch and must be supported by foreign tax proof. Late filing penalty under ITA s.162(1) is 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per complete month of late filing, up to 12 months; a second or repeated failure is 10% plus 2% per complete month to 20 months. CRA also charges daily compound arrears interest on unpaid amounts; gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2) can be 50% of the understated tax; failure to file prescribed information returns can yield fixed daily penalties.
T2 Schedule 17 (Provincial/Territorial Allocation) Allocates taxable income among provinces and territories based on gross revenue and payroll attributable to each permanent establishment; establishes provincial tax and provincial credits. A non-resident corporation without a PE in any province is not subject to provincial income tax and completes no allocation; if a PE exists in one or more provinces, complete the allocation formula using the PE-specific gross revenue and payroll. Late filing penalty under ITA s.162(1) is 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per complete month of late filing, up to 12 months; a second or repeated failure is 10% plus 2% per complete month to 20 months. CRA also charges daily compound arrears interest on unpaid amounts; gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2) can be 50% of the understated tax; failure to file prescribed information returns can yield fixed daily penalties.
T2 Schedule 88 / instalment reconciliation schedule Reconciles instalments paid during the year, federal/provincial tax payable, refunds, and amounts applied to next year's liability. Non-residents must isolate Part XIII withheld amounts from Part I instalment payments; balances due are paid electronically to CRA by the applicable corporate payment deadline. Late filing penalty under ITA s.162(1) is 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per complete month of late filing, up to 12 months; a second or repeated failure is 10% plus 2% per complete month to 20 months. CRA also charges daily compound arrears interest on unpaid amounts; gross-negligence penalties under s.163(2) can be 50% of the understated tax; failure to file prescribed information returns can yield fixed daily penalties.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate fair-market-value threshold for filing T106 (information return on non-arm's length cross-border transactions with non-residents). The Income Tax Act does not prescribe a fixed calendar filing threshold for a formal Local File or Master File; however, contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation must be prepared for intercompany transactions and produced within 3 months of a CRA demand. Where a transfer-pricing adjustment is material and the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts or maintain qualifying documentation, ITA s.247(3) imposes a penalty of 10% of the net adjustment.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone 'Economic Substance Regulations' regime as seen in offshore financial centres; however, GAAR under ITA s.245, residence based on central management and control, agency-PE doctrine, and treaty Limitation-on-Benefits/beneficial-ownership tests are applied to strip away sham or artificial structures. A Canadian-resident corporation must have real employees, assets, decision-making powers, fulfilment activity, and risk-bearing in Canada commensurate with its MRO catalogue e-commerce revenue; a bare shell with title-holding functions is liable to income reattribution and treaty denial.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful False Statements (ITA s.239(1))

Statutory sanction: On indictment, a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; on summary conviction, the same percentage fine and imprisonment up to 2 years; civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) of 50% of the understated tax is also assessable.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and any person who authorized or acquiesced in the false return are personally liable to prosecution and penal assessment. The corporate veil can be pierced where the corporation is a façade or sham; personal assets can be seized, travel restrictions may be imposed, and imprisonment is available.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Documentation Failure (ITA s.247)

Statutory sanction: CRA can adjust taxable income and disallow deductions; a 10% penalty applies to the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the taxpayer's documentation and reasonable-efforts standard are deficient. Interest accrues from the reassessment period; coordinated federal/provincial PE reallocations may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically assessed solely for transfer-pricing adjustments, but directors who sign false returns, participate in documentation fraud, or cause the corporation to be used as a transfer-pricing vehicle can face s.239 criminal liability, director withholding assessments, and civil recovery.
Unremitted Part XIII Withholding and Sham Treaty Structure (ITA s.227(1))

Statutory sanction: The corporate payer is liable for the entire unremitted Part XIII tax plus interest and penalties; if tax was withheld but not remitted, an additional 10% penalty applies. Treaty benefits may be denied, leaving foreign shareholders subject to full 25% statutory withholding.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII amounts if they approved the distribution and the corporation fails to pay. Officers who construct a sham treaty chain may face criminal tax evasion charges, personal monetary penalties, and extradition/travel constraints.
AML/CFT Deficiencies and Proceeds of Crime Abuse (PCMLTFA; Criminal Code s.462.31)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC can impose administrative monetary penalties of up to C$1,000,000 per violation for serious non-compliance; criminal money-laundering carries imprisonment up to 10 years and fines on indictment. CRA can also apply penalty assessments for false documentation generated in laundering schemes.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers who authorize or knowingly permit inadequate AML controls can be personally assessed under FINTRAC penalty practice. Principals abusing the corporate veil to conceal ownership or move proceeds can be personally prosecuted, have assets forfeited, and be subject to travel bans and imprisonment.
Failure to File T2 and Prescribed Information Returns (ITA s.162, s.238)

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties under s.162(1) range from 5% plus 1% per month to 12 months, and 10% plus 2% per month to 20 months for repeat failures. Information-return penalties under s.162(7)/162(10) are C$100 per day up to maximums; s.238 provides fines and imprisonment for intentional continued failure or false filing.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to file after CRA demand, or who knowingly sign false information returns, risk personal criminal liability and director liability assessments for corporate tax and unremitted source deductions; civil collection proceedings may target personal assets.
