Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal tax administration exercised via the CRA GST/HST portal, My Business Account (MyBA), and Business Registration Online (BRO); customs and export control administration by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the CARM (CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management) platform; in Quebec, Revenu Québec separately administers the QST and provincial corporate tax under a CRA-Revenu Québec harmonized collection agreement.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian federal/provincial business corporation obtains its statutory tax identity in four steps: (1) incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincial equivalent and obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN), the single corporate tax identifier, through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1; (2) open program accounts under the BN with two-letter suffixes — RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll if employees; the GST/HST account is mandatory once the corporation ceases to be a small supplier (taxable supplies in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in the current calendar quarter or the four preceding calendar quarters) or if the entity is a listed financial institution; (3) for a virtual card issuer, complete the ETA Schedule V classification analysis — card issuance, network authorization, clearing and settlement are exempt financial services, whereas separately contracted data-analytics or software-as-a-service may be taxable, affecting registration thresholds and input tax credit (ITC) entitlement; (4) parallel non-tax licensing — FINTRAC money-services-business registration or Bank of Canada Retail Payment Activities Act registration, card-scheme membership and sponsor-bank agreements — must be completed concurrently with CRA registration. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a BN and register for GST/HST through the CRA non-resident registration desk (RC1/RC4611 or MyBA), appoint a Canadian agent for service, and file under Part I of the Income Tax Act on Canadian-source income.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian business corporation is taxed as a separate person under Part I of the Income Tax Act on worldwide income; no pass-through or check-the-box eligibility exists in Canadian law. Territorial scope: resident corporations pay federal tax on worldwide income with foreign tax credits and exempt-surplus rules; non-resident corporations pay on Canadian-source business income attributable to a permanent establishment, on Canadian-source capital gains, and are subject to Part XIII withholding on certain outbound payments. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian business corporation is taxed as a separate person under Part I of the Income Tax Act on worldwide income; no pass-through or check-the-box eligibility exists in Canadian law. Territorial scope: resident corporations pay federal tax on worldwide income with foreign tax credits and exempt-surplus rules; non-resident corporations pay on Canadian-source business income attributable to a permanent establishment, on Canadian-source capital gains, and are subject to Part XIII withholding on certain outbound payments. Tax-exempt zones: none at the federal level. Provincial allocation: taxable income is allocated among provinces using the provincial apportionment formula; a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may claim the small business deduction (federal 9% on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income, subject to the business limit allocation among associated corporations).

Available election forms

Canada does not recognize U.S.-style check-the-box elections; entity classification follows the Income Tax Act. Available statutory elections include: (i) ITA s. 85 rollover elections for tax-deferred incorporation and asset transfers; (ii) ITA s. 125 associated-corporation elections allocating the small-business deduction; (iii) hybrid mismatch elections under ITA ss. 18.2 and 18.21, including the designated co-ordinated election for hybrid entities and branches; (iv) ETA s. 156(2) direct-designation election between registrants; (v) ETA s. 167(1) election for the supply of a business as a single supply; (vi) ETA s. 211(1) closely-related-corporation election for supplies between affiliated financial institutions; (vii) ETA s. 225.2(5) joint election for a listed financial institution to use the Special Attribution Method (SAM) for GST/HST ITC apportionment; and (viii) ETA s. 22(4) election to treat a qualifying non-resident as resident for GST/HST rebate purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small-supplier threshold computed on the value of taxable supplies made in Canada during the current calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters (ETA s. 148); exempt supplies (financial services) are excluded from the threshold. Registration is mandatory without threshold for a listed financial institution (bank, insurer, credit union, trust, or a person whose principal business includes lending, financial instrument trading or the provision of certain financial services) and for any person supplying taxable services in Canada; a corporate virtual card issuer whose principal business is the provision of financial services will generally be a listed financial institution and must be registered for GST/HST, with ITCs apportioned under the Special Attribution Method (ETA s. 225.2).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V when sold for export and exported as soon after the supply as is reasonable in the circumstances, with proof of export retained. Services: zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 where the recipient is a non-resident, the service is not performed in connection with real property in Canada or goods situated in Canada at the time of performance, the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada, and the service is not used in Canada. Financial services: card issuance, virtual card program management, payment clearing and settlement, and network authorization are exempt (not zero-rated) under Schedule V, Part VII (ss. 1 and 8); Canadian case law treats merchant discount and interchange fees as consideration for an exempt financial service. Export zero-rating of a financial service applies only under the narrow ETA Schedule VI, Part VII, s. 23 rule where the service is supplied to a non-resident and relates to a financial instrument that is not a Canadian financial instrument (e.g., a non-resident client's foreign card program). Separately contracted data-analytics or software subscriptions supplied to non-residents without a Canadian connection may qualify for 0% GST under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7. The issuer may claim ITCs only to the extent that its taxable (non-exempt) supplies are made, and non-registered non-resident clients may claim the unregistered non-resident GST/HST rebate (ETA s. 252). Although Canada imposes no statutory FX repatriation deadline, the 180-day benchmark is used in CRA transfer-pricing and export-substantiation practice; settlement evidence within 180 days should be retained.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B3 Commercial Accounting Declaration via CBSA CARM for goods exports) — generally not required for pure virtual card services
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill (for any physical corporate cards or hardware exported)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or equivalent FX inflow evidence demonstrating non-resident payment settlement
  • Signed international service agreement naming the non-resident counterparty and governing-law clause
  • GST/HST invoice annotated 'zero-rated — supply to non-resident' or 'exempt financial service' with the non-resident's business identity evidence (W-8BEN-E, VAT registration certificate or equivalent)
  • Service-fulfilment evidence for the non-resident card program: card program host records, Mastercard/Visa sponsor-bank agreements, transaction logs, and access logs showing consumption outside Canada

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital pre-authorization of invoices exists in Canada. The CRA requires every registrant to issue a sales invoice containing the prescribed content of s. 3 of the GST/HST Regulations: supplier name and BN, recipient name and address (where consideration exceeds CAD 30 or the supply is exempt), date, description, total consideration, tax rate and tax amount, and, for zero-rated supplies, a statement that the supply is zero-rated. Electronic invoices are accepted if the prescribed content is complete and records are retained for six years (ETA s. 286) in Canada or made accessible from Canada. The Pan-Canadian Peppol e-invoicing network is voluntary (federal budget 2023 commitment with CRA-certified access points); no legislative mandate for B2B e-invoicing or digital tax clearance applies to the card-services sector.

CRA audit risk is elevated for fintech card issuers claiming zero-rating or non-resident rebates. GST/HST assessments and reassessments may be issued within four years of the filing due date, with no limitation period where a person has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud (ETA s. 296). Key penalties: ETA s. 280 false statements — the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax or net tax understated, applicable in cases of knowledge or gross negligence; ETA s. 281 counselling false statements; ETA s. 280.1 daily compound interest on unpaid amounts; Customs Act penalties for false export declarations; and Income Tax Act s. 163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated federal tax where an export scheme manufactures deductible losses. Where zero-rating is unsupported, CRA retrospectively recharacterizes the supply as taxable or exempt, denies ITCs, and applies interest and penalties; deliberate schemes are referred under Criminal Code s. 380 (tax fraud). The transfer-pricing penalty (ITA s. 247(3)) also applies if card-program integration fees or technology service fees charged to foreign affiliates are not arm's length.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted convertibility on both current and capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar is a freely floating currency under IMF Article VIII status. There are no exchange controls, no prior approval requirements for foreign investment, and no restrictions on profit remittance, dividend payments, interest, or royalties. Non-resident investors may face Investment Canada Act net-benefit review for controlling acquisitions of Canadian businesses and Part XIII withholding tax on outbound remittances, but no currency convertibility barriers apply. Required tax clearances under the Income Tax Act section 116 may apply to certain dispositions, not to routine profit repatriation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no timeline for repatriation of export receipts. Exporters may freely retain foreign currency accounts at Canadian Schedule I or Schedule II banks. Balance-of-payments and merchandise trade reporting requirements may apply under the Statistics Act, but these are statistical in nature and do not create convertibility or surrender obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in conjunction with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for banking supervision and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering monitoring; Canada does not maintain a single dedicated foreign-exchange administration body. Exchange rate policy is the joint responsibility of the Minister of Finance and the Bank of Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High with legal constraints. Canada does not have a discrete 'electronic money institution' licence comparable to the European EMI regime. A cross-border digital wallet or virtual-card issuer may operate as a registered Money Services Business (MSB) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and as a payment service provider registered with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act. Deposit-taking activity is reserved for institutions authorized under the Bank Act, so the issuer must partner with a Canadian Schedule I bank as sponsor and settlement agent. Visa and Mastercard virtual card programs require a principal membership or sponsorship from an existing Member/Principal. Non-resident EMI structures are viable only through a Canadian-registered entity with a Canadian compliance presence, a FINTRAC MSB registration, and a bank-sponsored settlement account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections must be routed by SWIFT MT103 or MT202 to a Canadian Schedule I bank treasury account using the beneficiary legal entity's escrow or operational account. Instructions should cite the equity contribution purpose; acceptable correspondence reference may include 'Share capital contribution', 'Equity subscription under CA/ISIC/6619 Program', and restated subscription agreement details. The bank must verify that the sender is a permitted source of funds under its KYC policy; the receiving federal corporation must provide a director's resolution approving the issue of shares, an updated capitalization table, and an Investment Canada Act notification or net-benefit clearance letter where the transaction is reviewable. FINTRAC reporting thresholds apply to electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more in a single transaction or series, without imposing any currency conversion constraint.Outbound trade disbursements are executed via Payments Canada Lynx for high-value Canadian-dollar clearing or via SWIFT MT103 for cross-border foreign-currency payments. Commercial letters of credit are issued through Canadian bank trade desks for import/export settlements. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-High with legal constraints. Canada does not have a discrete 'electronic money institution' licence comparable to the European EMI regime. A cross-border digital wallet or virtual-card issuer may operate as a registered Money Services Business (MSB) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and as a payment service provider registered with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act. Deposit-taking activity is reserved for institutions authorized under the Bank Act, so the issuer must partner with a Canadian Schedule I bank as sponsor and settlement agent. Visa and Mastercard virtual card programs require a principal membership or sponsorship from an existing Member/Principal. Non-resident EMI structures are viable only through a Canadian-registered entity with a Canadian compliance presence, a FINTRAC MSB registration, and a bank-sponsored settlement account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair market value of non-arm’s-length transactions with a single non-resident during the taxation year for Form T106 and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation purposes. A full Local File is expected for transactions above this threshold. Canadian multinational groups with gross revenue exceeding CAD 100 million are expected to maintain a Master File covering worldwide intercompany arrangements, allocation policies, and supply chain substance.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulation statute. Nevertheless, the general anti-avoidance rule under section 245, the sham doctrine, and permanent establishment concepts require that a federal corporation carrying on a virtual card issuance business demonstrate real operational substance. Effective substance factors include local board decisions, Canadian employees with authority over card programme risk, payment network contracts executed, treasury account ownership, and Canadian IT infrastructure. Treaty benefits and Part XIII reductions can be denied where the Canadian entity is a hollow conduit or where the card issuing functions are wholly directed abroad contrary to the OECD transfer pricing guidance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Underreporting

Statutory sanction: Full reassessment with compound interest; gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of understated tax under s. 163(2); criminal prosecution under s. 239(1) for tax evasion with fines up to 200% of taxes evaded and imprisonment up to five years for officers/directors who participate.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for unremitted federal withholdings under s. 227.1, including employee source deductions and Part XIII amounts. CRA may use section 160 to recover taxes from shareholders or related transferees. Criminal exposure can trigger travel bans, restraint orders, and restitution orders over personal assets.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the general anti-avoidance rule under s. 245(2) to deny the tax benefit, reallocate income to the economic substance jurisdiction, ignore the Canadian entity as a sham for tax purposes, and assess unremitted Part XIII tax on deemed dividends or branch tax under Part XIV.

Director personal liability: Shareholders who receive value from such an entity may be assessed under s. 160(1) for taxes owed at the time of the transfer. Director liability exists for unpaid corporate tax where the entity was employed as an accommodation party; in cases of fraudulent asset stripping, directors can be personally liable for the outreach period and may face criminal fraud proceedings and travel restrictions.
AML/PCMLTFA Compliance Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties may reach CAD 500,000 for a legal person and CAD 100,000 for an individual per contravention. Criminal offences are punishable by fines and imprisonment up to five years. FINTRAC can also issue compliance orders, suspend registration, and freeze funds.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, permit, or acquiesce in contraventions are personally liable under the PCMLTFA penalty regime. Where money laundering is proven, individuals may be charged criminally, face extradition or travel bans, and be subject to asset forfeiture in addition to personal fines.
Transfer Pricing and T106 Reporting Default

Statutory sanction: CRA may reallocate income at the arm’s-length price and impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty under s. 247(3) on the net adjustment where reasonable efforts and documentation are absent. Failure to file T106 can attract daily penalties up to CAD 2,500, plus additional late-filing penalties. Interest accrues on all principal adjustments.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for unremitted or withheld amounts connected to the mispriced payments, especially where the misuse caused a failure to collect part XIII tax. Personal criminal liability arises under s. 239 if false statements or destruction of transfer pricing records is shown; CRA can assert civil liability against officers who approved the scheme and obtain court orders to compel compliance and payment.
