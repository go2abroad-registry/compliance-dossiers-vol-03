Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — official national tax administration; GST/HST and corporate income tax registration through the CRA Business Registration Online service and My Business Account portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html.

E-government filing portal

A resident or foreign-owned Canadian business corporation must first ensure valid incorporation (federal or provincial), then obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA. Registration is effected online through the CRA Business Registration Service, or by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and GST/HST Account' by mail or fax. The BN is the statutory TIN and supports separate program accounts for GST/HST (RT), corporate income tax (RC), and payroll where applicable. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada, or otherwise making taxable supplies in Canada, must register for GST/HST once the small supplier threshold is exceeded; a non-resident registrant should also appoint a Canadian contact or service address for CRA correspondence and must file an annual T2 corporate income tax return if taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box election; a business corporation is necessarily an entity-level taxpayer. Available elections include: (i) voluntary GST/HST registration by a small supplier (ETA s. 240); (ii) the ETA s. 156 election between closely related corporations to treat intercompany supplies as made for nil consideration; (iii) ITA s. 85 and s. 86 rollover elections for tax-deferred transfers of property; and (iv) allocation elections for the small business deduction among associated corporations under ITA s. 125. The hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2–18.33) are mandatory and contain no elective opt-out, in line with BEPS Action 2.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small supplier threshold, measured on the total value of taxable supplies made by the person in Canada either in a single calendar quarter or over the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters. Once the threshold is exceeded, registration for GST/HST is mandatory, including for non-resident e-commerce suppliers selling outdoor camping equipment into Canada; a person making taxable supplies in Canada who is not a small supplier must register regardless of physical presence.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods exported from Canada are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. To claim the 0% GST/HST rate, the registrant must prove that the goods: (i) are delivered to a common carrier or consignee for shipment outside Canada; (ii) are exported as soon as is reasonably possible after the sale; and (iii) are not resold or used in Canada. Delivery to a non-resident buyer at an address inside Canada is generally taxable at the applicable rate, so physical export evidence is essential. Freight and logistics services from Canada to a foreign destination may also qualify for zero-rating, as may certain services performed on tangible personal property exported for a non-resident. Robust documentary evidence must be retained before the CRA will accept the zero-rated treatment.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) export declaration (electronic export reporting through CARM/CADEX or the paper B13A Export Declaration)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / Cargo Control Document evidencing departure from Canada
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the foreign destination, overseas purchaser, and goods description
  • Proof of payment and FX settlement records (e.g., SWIFT MT103 bank credit, PayPal/Stripe settlement statements, or other verifiable remittance evidence)
  • Carrier transit logs, freight forwarder receipts, and proof of delivery (POD) at the foreign port or foreign warehouse

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls (CTC), or digital stamping regime exists at the federal level in Canada. The CRA does not pre-approve or clear invoices through an API. A registered supplier must issue a GST/HST invoice containing prescribed information: supplier name and Business Number, date of sale, description of goods/services, total consideration, amount of tax charged, and applicable tax rate (including the HST rate where applicable). Electronic invoices and EDI records are accepted if complete and legible. Canada is progressively moving toward a Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework based on Peppol/PINT specifications, but adoption remains voluntary and there is no current API connectivity or stamping obligation.

The CRA may reassess GST/HST within the normal four-year limitation period; the period is unlimited where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud. Export zero-rating claims are a known audit priority: where the exporter cannot substantiate physical export or where export proceeds are not received within a commercially reasonable period, the CRA will reverse the zero-rating, assess GST/HST on the full selling price, disallow related input tax credits, recover any refund previously paid, and charge arrears interest. Penalties include false-statement and gross-negligence penalties under ETA s. 280 and ITA s. 163(2), which may reach up to 50% of the tax avoided in income-tax cases, together with potential criminal prosecution for wilful evasion and promoter penalties for abusive schemes. Although Canadian federal law does not impose a statutory 180-day export proceeds repatriation deadline, the 180-day benchmark from invoice date to confirmed FX settlement is the standard compliance control used to demonstrate a legitimate export sale; prolonged non-receipt of payment can be treated by the CRA as indicia that no true export supply occurred.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has unrestricted convertibility on both current and capital account; the CAD/USD market is deep, free-floating, and free of exchange-control restrictions, surrender requirements, approval quotas, or repatriation delays applicable to dividends, branch profits, royalties, or loan repayments. Profit remittance to non-resident shareholders is subject only to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (25% statutory, reducible to 5-15% under the Canada-US and Canada-UK income tax treaties) and to standard transfer-pricing and thin-capitalization rules; no central bank exchange authorization is required. Practical compliance hurdles are limited to CRA documentation, treaty-relief forms, and bank-level source-of-funds attestations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law imposes no export-proceeds surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters may hold, keep, or invest export receipts in any currency without statutory repatriation deadlines. The only binding obligations are: (i) translation of foreign-currency revenue into Canadian dollars for corporate income tax using the Bank of Canada daily or annual average rate; (ii) FINTRAC reporting of international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, automatically completed by the clearing bank; and (iii) six-year retention of trade invoices and receipts under the Income Tax Act record-keeping requirements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) acts as the monetary authority and lender of last resort; prudential supervision over chartered banks is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange control administration; cross-border trade and capital payments are free from exchange-approval licensing. Mandatory cross-border funds reporting (transactions at or above CAD 10,000) is administered by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and balance-of-payments statistical collection is coordinated by Statistics Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Viability rating: MEDIUM-HIGH as supplemental treasury and settlement rails; LOW as the sole operating bank relationship. Canadian-registered fintech entities such as Wise Canada, Stripe Canada, and PayPal Canada operate as Money Service Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, not as banks under the Bank Act. They can efficiently process merchant card settlements and initiate outbound SWIFT payments, but they cannot provide CDIC-insured deposit custody, commercial letters of credit, licensed escrow facilities, or a full corporate credit relationship. Offshore EMIs that are not registered as Canadian MSBs or as authorized foreign banks under Schedule III of the Bank Act may not lawfully solicit deposit-taking or business-banking services from a Canadian corporation. The intended Stripe and PayPal rails are therefore correctly positioned as merchant-collection settlement layers that sweep daily into a Schedule I operating account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: (i) the federal corporation opens a Schedule I operating account and, where needed, a designated capital escrow sub-account using its CBCA registration certificate and good-standing documentation; (ii) the foreign shareholder initiates a SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfer with a precise purpose text referencing the subscription agreement, such as 'Share Capital Contribution to CA__CA_FED__CA_CORP__ISIC_4791_14_OUTDOOR_CAMPING_EQUIPMENT'; (iii) the beneficiary bank screens the remitter and ordering bank against OFAC, UN, EU, and Canadian sanctions lists and validates name-match with the corporate registry; (iv) the bank applies source-of-funds verification where the transfer is above the risk threshold and documents the purpose and currency; (v) settlement occurs in CAD or an available USD sub-account, with conversion at the bank's commercial spot or the Bank of Canada daily rate if CAD funding is intended; and (vi) the treasury records the contribution as paid-up capital or shareholder loan in the CBCA minute book, issues the corresponding share certificates within the statutory timeframe, and maintains all records for FINTRAC and CRA audit. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate on taxable income, before provincial/territorial allocation; small-business rate of 9% applies to the first C$500,000 of qualifying active business income for a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act for dividends paid to non-residents, subject to applicable tax treaty reduction (treaty rates commonly 5%-15% depending on shareholding ownership and beneficiary status).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Corporations must file within six months of the fiscal year-end; tax balance due is generally due two months after year-end for Canadian-controlled private corporations claiming the small business deduction and three months for other corporations, with instalments due quarterly.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory de minimis threshold exists for contemporaneous documentation; CRA requires transfer-pricing records for all controlled cross-border transactions. A Master File / Local File package is expected where annual aggregate controlled transactions exceed C$1 million; Country-by-Country (CbC) Report is mandatory only for MNE groups having consolidated revenue of C$750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations; CRA uses the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), the economic-substance override in section 247 of the Income Tax Act, and the treaty 'beneficial ownership' doctrine to disregard shell arrangements lacking genuine commercial substance; e-commerce platforms selling through a limited-risk distributor must show real employees, procurement functions, inventory risk, and management decision-making in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion under the Income Tax Act

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under subsection 239(1) results in a fine of 50% to 200% of the amount evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; civil penalties include 25% gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2), prescribed-rate interest, and a Canada-wide tax warrant.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize or acquiesce to evasion are jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 for unremitted employee withholdings and GST/HST, can be personally assessed under subsection 160 for transfers to non-arm's length parties, and may be subject to travel bans, passport restrictions, asset restraint orders, and criminal sentencing for conspiracy to defraud the CRA.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of the arm's-length consideration under section 247(2); transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment amount (section 247(4)) if documentation was not made available; interest on the primary assessment; denial of treaty withholding relief and gross-up of Part XIII tax at 25%; potential GAAR rescission of the entire transaction.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for the corporate liability under section 227.1 where transfer-pricing adjustments leave insufficient corporate assets; participation in a sham transaction may cause a court to pierce the corporate veil and hold directors jointly liable for restitution and punitive damages; travel restrictions are ordinarily incorporated in criminal proceedings if laundering or tax conspiracy is established.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization; CRA can assess Part I tax on receipts as if the foreign intermediary was an agent of the Canadian parent; additional Part XIII gross-up; risk of corporate dissolution for non-compliance; under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), a shell entity benefiting from low-risk designation but engaged in high-risk transactional flows may be assessed monetary penalties up to C$500,000.

Director personal liability: Courts pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a façade or sham; directors are then personally liable for unpaid tax and for fraudulent conveyances; a travel ban and asset freeze can be imposed if CRA obtains a section 231.7 revenue warrant on reasonable grounds that a director is dissipating assets.
Anti-Money Laundering (FINTRAC) Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties up to C$100,000 for an individual and C$500,000 for a corporation per violation; authority for FINTRAC to freeze assets through restraint orders and require mandatory report filings; failing to register as a money services business (if applicable) carries additional penalties and potential criminal liability.

Director personal liability: Directors can be charged for knowingly conducting transactions without AML monitoring, leading to fines, imprisonment for up to five years, and personal forfeiture orders; a restraint order may impose a travel ban restricting the director's ability to leave Canada while the investigation continues.
