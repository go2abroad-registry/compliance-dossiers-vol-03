Limited Partnerships Act of the selected Canadian province (e.g., Ontario R.S.O. 1990, c.L.8, ss.2 and 9; British Columbia PPSA Part 5 or Alberta Partnerships Act, as applicable); extra-provincial registration provisions apply for interprovincial operations; Canada Business Corporations Act, R.S.C. 1985, c.C-44, Part 1 applies where the general partner is a federal corporation. Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), the federal revenue authority for income tax and GST/HST; Business Registration Online (BRO) for BN issuance; My Business Account (Mon dossier d'entreprise) portal for ongoing compliance; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs export control and B13A export declarations.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Confirm Canadian tax nexus by assessing whether the non-resident Limited Partnership carries on business in Canada through manufacturing, inventory warehousing, employees, or a Canadian agent. Step 2: Appoint a Canadian representative or agent for service of process and record the Canadian address for service. Step 3: Apply for a 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account (RT) through the CRA Business Registration Online portal, or by paper forms RC1 (Request for a Business Number) and RC1S (Request for a Business Number and GST/HST Registration for Non-Resident) where the LP has no permanent establishment in Canada. Step 4: Disclose each general and limited partner's full legal name, jurisdiction of residence, and foreign taxpayer identification number to satisfy the T5013 Partnership Information Return requirements. Step 5: Open employer payroll (RP) and corporate income tax (RC) program accounts only where Canadian employees or corporate filing obligations exist; maintain the BN as the single taxpayer identifier for all CRA program accounts. No statutory minimum capital contribution is required, and 100% non-resident ownership is permitted, but the LP must annually report Canadian-source income and distributions attributable to each partner to the CRA.

The LP is fiscally transparent for income tax, but is a distinct person and mandatory registrant for GST/HST purposes under the Excise Tax Act, and acts as the exporter of record for CBSA export declarations. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible - Under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada), the Limited Partnership is not a taxable entity; each partner is taxed on its distributive share of Canadian-source business income, capital gains, and losses as if the amounts were earned directly. The LP is fiscally transparent for income tax, but is a distinct person and mandatory registrant for GST/HST purposes under the Excise Tax Act, and acts as the exporter of record for CBSA export declarations. Territorial scope: non-resident partners are taxed only on the partnership's Canadian-source income, while Canadian-resident partners are taxed on worldwide income through the partnership. The LP does not qualify as a tax-exempt zone entity.

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a domestic check-the-box regime. Available statutory elections for an exporting LP include: (i) subsection 97(2) Income Tax Act election to defer the deemed fair-market-value disposition on property contributed to the partnership; (ii) elections under subsection 102(2) and related rules of section 102 to deem the partnership not to be a Canadian partnership where the members are predominantly non-resident and control and management is exercised outside Canada; (iii) substituted fiscal-period election under subsection 249.1(4) to align the partnership year-end to a foreign parent's fiscal year; (iv) section 211 Excise Tax Act election for a qualifying member to report and remit GST/HST on behalf of the partnership; (v) voluntary registration election for small suppliers to recover input tax credits; and (vi) for US federal tax purposes only, a parallel Form 8832 entity classification election that may re-classify the Canadian LP from default flow-through partnership to an association taxable as a US corporation. Where any such election creates a hybrid mismatch outcome, the LP must document the arrangement under OECD BEPS Action 2 and comply with the Canadian hybrid mismatch rules in sections 18.2 to 18.4, 20.2, and 20.21 of the Income Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable turnover - under section 148 Excise Tax Act, the Limited Partnership is a small supplier exempt from mandatory registration only while its worldwide taxable supplies, including supplies made outside Canada, do not exceed CAD 30,000 in the current calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters. A foreign non-resident LP making taxable supplies in Canada must register once this global threshold is exceeded, even without Canadian residency or a permanent establishment. Zero-rated export sales and foreign domestic sales count within the worldwide taxable-supply base, so an active export manufacturer will almost certainly be required to register once Canadian-bound sales begin.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST-registered LP may apply 0% tax to exports of electric cargo e-bikes and component parts under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. Zero-rating requires that the supplier export the tangible personal property as soon as is reasonably possible after transfer of possession and title to a non-resident buyer, or deliver the goods to a common carrier, customs broker, or freight forwarder for shipment directly outside Canada. Supporting services supplied to non-residents (logistics coordination, design engineering, R&D, and quality testing performed in Canada) are zero-rated where the benefit is enjoyed wholly outside Canada. The LP retains full entitlement to input tax credits on Canadian production costs, including lithium batteries, steel frames, electric motors, tooling, factory electricity, and professional fees, and may recover any GST/HST paid at importation on production inputs through the normal ITC mechanism. Sales made to a Canadian distributor that later resells abroad do not qualify for zero-rating unless possession and title pass directly to the non-resident and export occurs immediately; interprovincial transfers to HST provinces are taxable domestic supplies and cannot be zero-rated as exports.

  • Customs Export Declaration - CBSA Form B13A or electronic ACI eManifest export report, filed by the LP or a licensed customs broker, showing the final foreign destination and export transaction code
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (air) naming the non-resident purchaser and confirming that the cargo e-bikes physically departed Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate - bank SWIFT/Telegraphic Transfer advice or foreign currency conversion receipt proving settlement of the export proceeds by the non-resident buyer
  • Commercial invoice and Canada Customs Invoice containing HS 8711.60 classification, NAICS 336991 reference, total consideration in CAD, and the LP's GST/HST registration number
  • Packing list and carrier manifest (truck or rail) with PARS electronic release or eManifest status evidencing the border crossing date
  • Free-trade certificate (e.g., CUSMA/USMCA Certificate of Origin) where the e-bikes are exported to the United States
  • Internal export-compliance file containing the LP's zero-rating checklist, shipping instructions, cargo insurance, and documents evidencing transfer of risk outside Canada

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control regime exists federally in Canada. GST/HST invoices must display the supplier's BN and RT program account number, the transaction date, a description of the goods (including HS 8711.60 for electric cargo bicycles), the consideration in CAD, and either the tax amount or a clear statement of the applicable GST/HST rate. Invoices may be issued electronically and retained in digital form for at least six years; no pre-approval of invoice layout or API clearance is required. The Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework adopts the voluntary Peppol 5-corner model for B2B exchanges. However, if e-bikes are sold through a Canadian digital marketplace or fulfillment platform, the platform operator may be deemed to make the supply under sections 1.1 to 1.4 of the Excise Tax Act, shifting the GST/HST collection and remittance obligation to the platform rather than the manufacturing LP.

The CRA may deny zero-rating and re-characterize the supply as taxable where the documentary bundle is incomplete or where export does not occur as soon as is reasonably possible, resulting in reassessment within the four-year normal limitation period under subsection 298(1) of the Excise Tax Act. Where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud, the limitation period is unlimited under subsection 298(5), the net tax is fully clawed back with arrears interest under section 280, and a false-statement penalty of 25% of the misstated net tax applies under subsection 285(1). Knowingly obtaining a GST/HST refund or credit to which the LP is not entitled is an offence under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, carrying upon indictment a fine up to CAD 10,000 plus double the understated tax and imprisonment up to five years; the CRA may suspend or offset refunds, issue third-party demands to customers, and publish conviction details under its named-taxpayers program. Although Canadian GST law imposes no express 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, the 180-day benchmark is maintained as a strict treasury control: failure to repatriate export proceeds within the window triggers CBSA transaction-value scrutiny, anti-money-laundering review under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and FINTRAC reporting expectations, transfer-pricing adjustment risk under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, and urgency under the Voluntary Disclosures Program. For related-party component imports feeding the e-bike production line, contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation must be maintained to defend arm's-length margins and to prevent export zero-rating claims from being recharacterized as conduit arrangements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction with full current and capital account convertibility. The Canadian dollar is a freely floating, CLS-settled deliverable currency. There are no exchange controls, no central bank approval for capital inflows or outflows, and no restrictions on profit remittance, dividend payments, or partnership distributions to non-resident partners. The practical hurdles are fiscal and documentary: (i) distributions of Canadian-source business income by a limited partnership to a non-resident partner attract Part XIII withholding tax at the 25% statutory rate, usually treaty-reduced (e.g., under the Canada-US Convention) to 15% or lower; (ii) contributions to partnership capital are generally not taxable and are exempt from withholding provided they are clearly designated as capital; (iii) all international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC under the international electronic funds transfer (IETF) regime; and (iv) the bank will perform sanctions screening against the Canadian Autonomous Sanctions list administered by Global Affairs Canada and the Department of Finance. For the manufacture and export of electric cargo bicycles (ISIC 3091 / NAICS 336991), CAD, USD, and EUR settlement are fully unrestricted, and no currency conversion licensing is required at any stage of the cross-border treasury cycle.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to surrender foreign currency receipts to the central bank or any other authority. Exporters may retain proceeds indefinitely in foreign currency accounts, use them for offshore disbursements, or convert them at commercial rates. Compliance obligations are limited to (i) FINTRAC large-transaction and international EFT reporting where thresholds are met, (ii) Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) export declaration requirements through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for export shipments with a value exceeding CAD 2,000, controlled goods, or goods destined to embargoed parties, and (iii) proper HS classification, typically HS 8711.60 for electric bicycles, with country-of-origin marking and end-use statements. There is no export proceeds surrender or FX conversion certificate requirement in Canada.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC), Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) act as the combined monetary, prudential, and AML/CFT supervisory authorities. Canada maintains no single centralized foreign exchange control administration; foreign exchange matters are handled through the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, the Canadian Payments Act, and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail; Low as a primary treasury vehicle. Canada does not license 'electronic money institutions' (EMIs) as a distinct regulatory category. Foreign digital EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex operate in Canada only as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC. They are not deposit-taking institutions, are not members of Payments Canada, do not participate in the Lynx high-value settlement system, and cannot issue commercial letters of credit under the Bills of Exchange Act. A Canadian limited partnership may lawfully use a FINTRAC-registered MSB for low-value supplier payouts, retailer settlements in the micro-mobility leasing network, or foreign-currency receiving accounts, but the legal constraints include: (i) the MSB cannot hold funds in a manner resembling a demand deposit without triggering the Banking Act; (ii) no CDIC deposit insurance applies to MSB balances; (iii) the resident bank account remains the anchor for payroll, tax remittances to the Canada Revenue Agency, and statutory obligations; and (iv) the bank account at the Schedule I institution must still be opened by the same entity, making the MSB a sub-account corridor rather than a substitute for corporate banking.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The compliant protocol for foreign direct investment and capital contributions into the Canadian limited partnership uses a SWIFT MT103 (direct credit) or MT202 (intermediary credit) wire in CAD or USD to the Schedule I bank operational account, with the beneficiary line stating the exact legal name of the limited partnership, its provincial registration number, and the payment narrative 'capital contribution to limited partnership; non-taxable under Part XIII'. No Bank of Canada exchange control approval is required, but the receiving bank will require: (i) the limited partnership agreement and certificate of registration; (ii) confirmation of the ultimate source of funds with bank statements evidencing the sending account; (iii) FINTRAC IETR reporting automatically triggered at CAD 10,000 or more; (iv) identification of the general partner and each partner above 25% for the UBO record; and (v) written instruction confirming that the payment is capital in nature and not a management fee, guarantee fee, or income distribution, to avoid Part XIII withholding exposure. For USD inbound wires routed through a US correspondent bank, the sender must include the beneficiary bank's US correspondent ABA (e.g., RBC's JPMorgan Chase correspondent), the beneficiary account number, and the 9-digit Canadian transit/routing number; SWIFT beneficiary fields should be completed without abbreviation to prevent exception-based hold. Once the capital is credited, the bank may impose a 1-2 business day availability hold pending final settlement in the Lynx or Fedwire clearing cycle; the escrowed amount is then released to the operational account.For trade settlements related to the manufacturing of electric cargo bicycles, the recommended clearing rails are tiered by value and risk: (i) SWIFT MT103 direct telex transfers for supplier payments below USD 250,000, authenticated through the bank's dual-authorisation electronic banking platform with 2-factor token verification; (ii) irrevocable documentary letters of credit (commercial LCs) issued by the Schedule I bank and advised/confirmed by the supplier's bank under ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600), used for large component purchases such as lithium-ion battery packs and drive systems; (iii) documentary collections (D/P and D/A) under ICC Uniform Rules for Collections (URC 522) for mid-value transactions where open account is not acceptable but a full LC is excessive; and (iv) CAD-denominated Lynx (LVTS) real-time gross settlement wires for domestic Canadian costs such as freight, warehousing, and dealer inventory. USD outbound payments are cleared via the bank's US correspondent through Fedwire or CHIPS; CAD domestic disbursements settle irrevocably and simultaneously on Lynx. For each disbursement, the bank will validate transaction conformity by comparing the beneficiary name, invoice, bill of lading or airway bill, and customs entry; the treasury operation must submit purchase orders and supplier agreements in advance to avoid regulatory holds under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail; Low as a primary treasury vehicle. Canada does not license 'electronic money institutions' (EMIs) as a distinct regulatory category. Foreign digital EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex operate in Canada only as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC. They are not deposit-taking institutions, are not members of Payments Canada, do not participate in the Lynx high-value settlement system, and cannot issue commercial letters of credit under the Bills of Exchange Act. A Canadian limited partnership may lawfully use a FINTRAC-registered MSB for low-value supplier payouts, retailer settlements in the micro-mobility leasing network, or foreign-currency receiving accounts, but the legal constraints include: (i) the MSB cannot hold funds in a manner resembling a demand deposit without triggering the Banking Act; (ii) no CDIC deposit insurance applies to MSB balances; (iii) the resident bank account remains the anchor for payroll, tax remittances to the Canada Revenue Agency, and statutory obligations; and (iv) the bank account at the Schedule I institution must still be opened by the same entity, making the MSB a sub-account corridor rather than a substitute for corporate banking.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate on taxable income for general corporations (after federal abatement), plus provincial/territorial corporate income tax rates ranging from 0% to 12%; combined federal-provincial statutory rate typically 23% to 31% depending on province and business type.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory 25% Canadian withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian transfer pricing documentation requirements apply to transactions with non-arm's length non-residents; mandatory Local File is generally required if the aggregate value of reportable transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000, and Master File is required when total revenue exceeds CAD 100,000,000 (per CRA TP Memorandum TPM-01); Form T106 must be filed for transactions exceeding CAD 1,000,000 aggregate.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) akin to UAE or Bermuda; however, the Canada Revenue Agency applies the 'substance over form' doctrine, statutory anti-avoidance rules (GAAR), and permanent establishment thresholds. For treaty benefits, Canadian domestic law and DTAs require the recipient to be the beneficial owner and to have genuine business substance; shell entities lacking economic activity are denied treaty relief. Part XVI measures and CFC rules (formerly section 17 and 95) require substantial Canadian activity for tax deferral.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act: fine up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years on summary conviction; on indictment, fine up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil penalties under subsection 163(2): 50% of understated tax plus interest. VDP quashing may result in gross negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for corporate tax withholdings (e.g., source deductions, GST/HST); CRA can assess directors personally for unpaid amounts. Criminal liability attaches to directors who authorize, accept, or acquiesce in the evasion; travel bans possible under court orders; civil restitution orders and asset freezing apply.

Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The Canada Revenue Agency may disregard the transaction under GAAR, deny treaty benefits, reassess tax, and impose a 50% gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2). For non-resident owner-managed entities, the CRA may impose Part XIV branch tax and Part XIII withholding on deemed distributions; criminal fines for tax evasion apply if wilfulness is established.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for the corporation's unremitted withholdings; for those involved in promoting shell tax schemes, the promoter penalty under section 162 provides for fine up to CAD 100,000 plus negligence penalties. The court may issue a directing order to restore funds and a travel ban pending criminal investigation.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering Violation

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for corporations; criminal offense penalties include up to 5 years imprisonment. FINTRAC enforcement: penalties range from CAD 250,000 to CAD 2,000,000 for serious non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally fined if they knowingly fail to maintain compliance programs; officers and directors involved in laundering proceeds may face criminal liability with imprisonment up to 10 years under Criminal Code section 462.31; civil asset forfeiture and director travel bans can be ordered by the court.
Failure to File / Failure to Withhold

Statutory sanction: Late filing penalty as described above; failure to remit withholding tax under section 227.1 results in a penalty equal to 10% of the amount not remitted plus daily interest; wilful default may lead to prosecution with fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors are liable under section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST, including interest and penalties; CRA may issue a director's certificate and pursue personal assets. Failure to comply with a court order for restitution or tax payment leads to seizure of assets and potential international travel bans under civil contempt.
