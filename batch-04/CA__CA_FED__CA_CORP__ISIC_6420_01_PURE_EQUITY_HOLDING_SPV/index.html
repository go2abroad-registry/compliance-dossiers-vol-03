Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), Business Number Program and GST/HST Directorate; online portal: Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/register.html

E-government filing portal

For a Canadian-incorporated pure equity holding SPV, the CRA issues the Business Number (BN) automatically upon federal incorporation via Corporations Canada. To open or update tax program accounts, the corporation or its authorized representative files Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts' or uses Business Registration Online (BRO). A GST/HST program account is added with the RT suffix; the corporate income tax account uses the RC suffix. Foreign-incorporated entities that carry on taxable activities in Canada must first obtain a BN by submitting Form RC1, together with constitutive documents, foreign registration proof, and a completed CRA representative authorization (Form AUT-01 or online authorization) where needed. The BN is the statutory TIN for Canada Revenue Agency purposes; no separate federal VAT ID exists outside the GST/HST program account number.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification election. A business corporation is unconditionally treated as a separate taxable entity under the Income Tax Act (Canada). Available elections relevant to an SPV include the ITA s. 85 rollover election for transfers of property on incorporation, s. 88(2) winding-up rollover elections, and s. 93(2) elections respecting dividends from foreign affiliates. Hybrid mismatch rules under ITA ss. 18.4–18.7 and related foreign affiliate provisions apply statutorily and cannot be overridden by an entity classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada over one calendar quarter and the four preceding consecutive quarters, excluding zero-rated exports, financial services, and exempt supplies; passive dividend income and capital gains from share ownership are not commercial activity and generally do not trigger registration for a pure equity holding SPV.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for goods exported from Canada and for certain services supplied to non-residents, provided the supplier retains documentary proof that the goods were exported or the services were performed wholly outside Canada. A pure equity holding SPV deriving only dividends, interest, or capital gains from shares is not making taxable export supplies; such passive income is either exempt as a financial service or outside the scope of GST/HST, and therefore no export zero-rating or related input tax credit entitlement arises.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) filing)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / Cargo Control Document
  • Commercial Invoice and packing list
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire/credit advice evidencing receipt of export sale proceeds in Canadian or foreign currency

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based e-invoice clearance regime for federal GST/HST purposes. GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically through GST/HST NETFILE and issue valid tax invoices containing the supplier's BN, date, GST/HST amount or rate, and prescribed particulars. Pan-Canadian e-invoicing interoperability under the Peppol standard is voluntary; CRA does not require government-side pre-clearance of invoices.

CRA may reassess denied export zero-rating, claw back input tax credits or rebates, and impose penalties under Excise Tax Act s. 280 for false statements or omissions, generally the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the understated tax or overstated rebate/refund. Gross negligence or intentional misrepresentation can extend the four-year reassessment period and attract interest compounded daily under ETA s. 280.1; criminal tax evasion prosecutions under ETA s. 327 may carry fines and imprisonment. CRA scrutinizes export claims for complete documentary evidence of shipment, delivery, payment, and FX repatriation within 180 days.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal general corporate income tax rate. A Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally qualify for a 9% federal small business rate on eligible active business income within the federal business limit, subject to the associated-corporation and investment-income rules. Provincial or territorial corporate income tax applies separately and is not included in the federal rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax generally applies to taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident. Treaty rates commonly reduce the rate to 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership threshold, and to 10% or 15% for other qualifying recipients, depending on the treaty. Under the Canada-China income tax convention, the commonly applicable dividend rates are generally 10% where the beneficial owner is a company controlling at least 10% of the voting power and 15% in other treaty cases, subject to the treaty text and beneficial-ownership requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance of tax is generally due two months after year-end. The balance-of-tax deadline may be extended to three months after year-end for a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation meeting the statutory conditions, including the applicable taxable-income limitation.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. A Canadian taxpayer must make reasonable efforts to determine and use arm's-length prices for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. A formal contemporaneous documentation package is expected by the filing deadline and must be provided to the CRA within three months of a written request. T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate amount of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1 million. Canada generally has no standalone mandatory OECD Master File requirement for every taxpayer; Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to an ultimate parent or surrogate entity of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the regimes adopted by certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the Income Tax Act's general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, sham and beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse rules, foreign-affiliate rules, thin-capitalization rules, and transfer-pricing requirements. A pure holding company should maintain genuine corporate governance, Canadian books and records, documented investment decisions, commercial rationale, bank and accounting controls, directors' resolutions, ownership records, and evidence supporting dividends, financing, guarantees, and service arrangements. A corporation with no personnel or decision-making capacity may still be valid, but the absence of real control and risk assumption can undermine treaty relief, beneficial ownership, transfer-pricing positions, foreign-affiliate treatment, and the application of the general anti-avoidance rule.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: An offence under the Income Tax Act can result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to two years on summary conviction, and potentially imprisonment of up to five years for an indictable offence. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, denial of deductions, and GAAR adjustments may apply independently.

Director personal liability: A director, officer, or other representative who authorized, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct may face prosecution and personal criminal liability. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced, but personal liability can arise under the Income Tax Act, Criminal Code, trust-remittance provisions, and ordinary civil law. Criminal proceedings may produce bail, passport-surrender, or travel restrictions in an individual case; Canada does not impose an automatic tax-evasion travel ban.
Failure to Remit Payroll, Part XIII, or GST/HST Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: The corporation may be assessed for the unremitted amount, interest, late-remittance penalties, and applicable gross-negligence or failure-to-file penalties. For certain trust amounts, statutory penalties can be significant and may apply separately from the underlying tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under Income Tax Act section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions and under Excise Tax Act section 323 for unremitted GST/HST, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. Personal assessments can result in collection against personal assets. Imprisonment is generally linked to separate criminal conduct, not automatic director liability.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under Income Tax Act section 247, adjust income or expenses to an arm's-length amount, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, apply withholding tax, and charge interest. The absence of contemporaneous documentation can eliminate the reasonable-efforts penalty defense.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability where they participated in fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, knowing tax misrepresentation, or trust-remittance failures. Mere approval of a commercially supported transaction does not ordinarily create personal tax liability.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty relief, reassess withholding at 25%, assess the payer for the shortfall, interest, penalties, and information-return failures, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions. Gross-negligence or criminal penalties may apply to deliberate misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly provide false residency or beneficial-ownership information may face civil assessments, penalties, and criminal prosecution. Travel restrictions are case-specific and are not an automatic consequence of a treaty reassessment.
Shell Company, Sham, or Artificial Holding Structure

Statutory sanction: The CRA may disregard sham transactions, deny deductions or treaty benefits, apply GAAR, recharacterize distributions, reassess shareholder benefits, impose withholding tax, and refer deliberate conduct for prosecution. Corporate-law consequences may include invalid transactions, oppression claims, director liability, and dissolution or compliance proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil restitution, breach-of-duty damages, fraudulent-conveyance liability, and personal criminal liability where they knowingly participated in the sham. A corporation's limited liability does not protect intentional fraud or statutory trust liabilities.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Reporting entities regulated by FINTRAC may face administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, reporting restrictions, and publication of violations. Money laundering under the Criminal Code can result in forfeiture, substantial fines, and imprisonment of up to ten years for indictable offences. Proceeds-of-crime confiscation may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners who direct, authorize, or knowingly facilitate the conduct may face prosecution, asset restraint and forfeiture, civil recovery, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions may arise through bail or court orders; there is no automatic director travel ban for an AML compliance breach.
Corporate Record, Ownership, and Filing Fraud

Statutory sanction: Federal and provincial corporate statutes may impose fines, compliance orders, director-officer offences, dissolution or cancellation consequences, and invalidation or challenge of corporate acts. Fraud, forgery, and false-document offences may also apply under the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal fines, civil claims, restitution, disqualification or removal consequences, and imprisonment where the conduct constitutes a criminal offence. Limited liability does not protect intentional falsification or fraudulent concealment.
Unlawful Capital Distribution and Insolvent Dividend

Statutory sanction: The distribution may be voidable or recoverable under the applicable federal or provincial corporate statute and insolvency legislation. Directors may be ordered to restore amounts, and creditor, oppression, fraudulent-conveyance, or preference remedies may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorized an unlawful distribution may be jointly and severally liable to restore the amount, subject to statutory defenses and limitation periods. Additional civil damages, director disqualification, and criminal liability may arise where fraud or intentional creditor prejudice is proven.
Failure to File Foreign Affiliate and Cross-Border Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Information-return penalties commonly accrue at CAD 25 per day, subject to form-specific maximums, with separate penalties for false statements, gross negligence, repeated failure, and failure to provide requested records. Reassessment periods may be extended where required information is missing or incorrect.

Director personal liability: Personal liability normally requires participation in fraud, wilful misconduct, statutory director liability, or another independent cause of action. Directors should retain evidence of review, authorization, and reasonable compliance procedures.
Record Retention and CRA Obstruction

Statutory sanction: The CRA may impose penalties, reassess tax using available information, extend the audit period, obtain compliance orders, garnish accounts, register liens, seize assets, and commence prosecution for obstruction or related offences.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct destruction, obstruction, or fraudulent asset transfers may face personal fines, restitution, civil recovery, contempt or compliance proceedings, and imprisonment for applicable criminal offences. Passport or travel restrictions may arise only through specific court processes.
