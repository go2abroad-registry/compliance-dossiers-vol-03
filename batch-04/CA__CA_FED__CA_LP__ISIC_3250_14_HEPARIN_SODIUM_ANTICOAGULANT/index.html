Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), operating the My Business Account portal (Mon dossier d'entreprise). The single federal intake is Business Registration Online (BRO), which synchronizes with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs import/export (RDI) accounts, with the Department of Finance Canada responsible for statutory policy and the GST/HST legislative framework under the Excise Tax Act.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a Canadian limited partnership (LP) must procure a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account, generally via Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1. Step 1: identify at least one general partner who is resident in Canada or, for a wholly non-resident structure, obtain a BN directly for the LP where the LP has a Canadian permanent establishment carrying on commercial activity; Step 2: submit partnership particulars, including each partner's legal name, country of residence, tax residency status, ownership percentage, and the effective date of the limited partnership agreement; Step 3: register the GST/HST program account when the LP's gross revenue from taxable supplies exceeds CAD 30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters, or when a non-resident partnership makes taxable supplies in Canada through a permanent establishment; Step 4: open an RDI (import/export) account with the CBSA for customs processing of heparin sodium crude and finished anticoagulant shipments; Step 5: submit certified copies of the partnership agreement, evidence of capital contributions, and partner identification records for CRA verification; Step 6: after registration, file an annual partnership information return (T5013) with income allocations computed under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act and issue T5013 slips to each partner, including non-resident partners.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent under Section 96 of the Income Tax Act; the LP itself is not a taxpayer for income tax purposes, and each partner's share of taxable income is computed as if the partnership is a separate person, then allocated to partners. A limited partner's deductible losses are restricted to the partner's at-risk amount under subsection 96(2.1); a non-resident partner carrying on business through the LP is taxable in Canada on the Canadian-source business income attributable to the LP's permanent establishment, and Part XIII withholding tax (generally 25%, reducible by treaty) applies to passive allocations. For GST/HST purposes, the LP is a distinct 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and is the registered vendor that must collect and remit GST/HST on its taxable supplies; the partners are jointly and severally liable for the partnership's net tax to the extent determined under subsection 273(2) of the Excise Tax Act.

Available election forms

(i) Subsection 96(3) Income Tax Act election to treat the LP as a 'Canadian partnership' for deemed disposition and rollover purposes; (ii) Section 216 Income Tax Act election allowing a non-resident partner to file a net-basis Canadian return for rental or timber royalty income instead of gross withholding; (iii) Subsection 34(1) Income Tax Act election to adopt a non-calendar fiscal period for a professional partnership (limited relevance to pharmaceutical extraction); (iv) hybrid mismatch compliance elections under Sections 18.4 to 18.6 of the Income Tax Act and Sections 12 to 14 of the Income Tax Conventions Interpretation Act, which neutralize deductions attributable to mismatches arising from the LP's fiscally transparent treatment versus an entity-level tax classification of the same foreign partner in its residence jurisdiction; (v) voluntary GST/HST registration election under subsection 242(1) of the Excise Tax Act, allowing a small supplier to register before crossing the CAD 30,000 threshold to claim input tax credits on export inputs; (vi) subsection 262(1) Excise Tax Act election for closely related corporations, available at the partner level in a corporate group context; (vii) no 'check-the-box' regime exists at the federal level in Canada, and the tax transparency of the limited partnership is mandatory rather than elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in gross revenue from taxable supplies, measured over any four consecutive calendar quarters; a supplier that exceeds the threshold must register within 29 days after the day that the threshold was exceeded. A non-resident making taxable supplies in Canada must register even below the threshold if the supplies are made through a permanent establishment in Canada or where the CRA requires registration to collect tax. Voluntary registration is permitted for exporters and small suppliers seeking input tax credit recovery.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property, including heparin sodium active pharmaceutical ingredient, are zero-rated under Part V, Schedule VI, Section 1 of the Excise Tax Act when the supplier exports the goods by shipping them out of Canada, or the goods are exported on behalf of the supplier, and the supplier retains documentary evidence of exportation within a two-year period (or such longer period accepted by the Minister). Services supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and that are performed wholly outside Canada are zero-rated under Part V, Sections 5 to 7; a supply of a right, licence, or proprietary manufacturing process relating to heparin sodium extraction supplied to a non-resident is zero-rated under Section 10 of Part V. A person making zero-rated exports may claim full input tax credits on inputs used in the extraction and sale, creating an effective refund of embedded GST/HST; the zero-rated supply is reported on the GST/HST return and the resultant net refund position is payable by the CRA on a prescribed periodic basis.

  • Bill of Lading for ocean shipment or Airway Bill for air freight, evidencing physical departure of the goods from Canada to the non-resident buyer

Canada does not impose mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API-based invoice pre-approval as of the current law. The CRA requires digital recordkeeping and the retention of system-generated invoices containing all prescribed information under subsection 221(2) of the Excise Tax Act, including the supplier's registration number. A voluntary Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework signed to the Peppol BIS 3.0 standard was launched between 2022 and 2025 through the 'Palanca' interoperability pilot, operating on a B2B voluntary basis only. For export transactions, the CBSA Single Window and the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) platform impose electronic filing requirements for commercial export declarations, with exporters transmitting data via EDI API interfaces (CUS/LIN or CEALP) before goods are released for shipment of controlled pharmaceutical active ingredients such as heparin sodium.

When export zero-rating documentation is absent, forged, or inconsistent, the CRA may reassess the supply at 5% GST (plus the applicable HST provincial rate of 13% to 15% in participating provinces) with interest computed from the date the tax would otherwise have remitted. The CRA may also apply: (i) a negligence penalty under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act equal to 20% of the total tax sought, rising to 40% where the conduct is fraudulent or deliberate; (ii) late-filing and late-remittance penalties of up to 6% of the amount plus prescribed interest; (iii) a false statement or misrepresentation penalty under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act equal to 25% of the understated tax with a minimum of CAD 25,000; (iv) criminal prosecution for GST evasion under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, with fines up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment up to five years for officers, partners, and employees involved; (v) retrospective clawback of input tax credits claimed in connection with the failed zero-rating, with a four-year normal reassessment window extended to ten years in cases of misrepresentation or fraud; and (vi) at the income tax level, denial of limited partner loss allocations under the at-risk rules of subsection 96(2.1) and partner-level penalties under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act equal to 50% of the understated tax. The CBSA may further suspend the exporter's CUS program privileges, issue a controlled-goods referral, and place the shipment under full examination at the CARM export window until proof of departure and payment traceability are fully reconciled.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility in compliance with IMF Article VIII. There are no exchange-control approvals for capital injection, dividend or distribution repatriation, loan servicing, trade-related payments, or liquidation of a Canadian limited partnership. A non-resident limited partner's share of Canadian-source income may attract Part XIII withholding or partnership-level withholding obligations, but such obligations are tax commitments, not convertibility or FX approval requirements. All non-resident inward investments should still be screened for control thresholds under the Investment Canada Act and for sanctions/OFAC consistency; passive LP interests generally remain procedural, but legal confirmation is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender, conversion ratio, or statutory timeline for export receipts. A Canadian exporter, including a limited partnership engaged in heparin sodium fractionation, may lawfully retain export proceeds in any convertible currency. Statutory compliance duties include: (i) inclusion of all revenue in the CRA income tax and partnership information returns; (ii) accurate financial statement and transfer-pricing documentation for cost-sharing arrangements; (iii) FINTRAC large-value reporting for cross-border electronic fund transfers and cash transactions above CAD 10,000; and (iv) accurate export documentation with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) and Statistics Canada, using the prescribed Canadian Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A) and Harmonized System tariff classification.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting in coordination with Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Bank of Canada Act, the Currency Act, and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: LOW-TO-MEDIUM as the sole treasury rail; MEDIUM as an auxiliary virtual account or payment facilitator in Canada. Canadian law does not treat online European-style EMI wallet providers as banks: credit-issuing and deposit-taking remain restricted to federally or provincially regulated financial institutions. Marketplace lenders and account-issuing fintechs must be compliant with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), supervised by the Bank of Canada, and must obtain a money-services business or restricted/dealing foreign-exchange registration with FINTRAC where applicable. Online EMI accounts that merely attach virtual CAD/USD accounts do not provide clearing-house access, cheque-clearing capability, or confirmed SWIFT correspondent reliability; they should be used strictly for low-value vendor payments and never for the settlement of letter-of-credit transactions or capital escrow obligations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions from non-resident limited partners must be routed through a tier-1 Canadian bank or a Canadian branch of a foreign bank using SWIFT MT103 or MT202 for bank-to-bank cover. The Canadian beneficiary account title must exactly match the registered name of the limited partnership and be marked with the phrase 'LP capital contribution' in the remittance information field, referencing the limited partnership agreement clause and, if applicable, the contribution schedule. For CAD settlement, the funds are cleared in real time through Payments Canada Lynx (formerly LVTS); for USD settlement, the receiving bank uses SWIFT/CHIPS or a US-dollar clearing account. Beneficiary banks will require a finalized Beneficial Ownership Register, evidence of the partnership formation, and identification of the general partner. Canada does not pre-validate a central-bank purpose code; however, the wire instruction must carry a layered rationale so compliance can reconcile it to the LP's registered undertaking. For a non-resident investor holding a majority or controlling interest, pre-filing and potential review under the Investment Canada Act must be confirmed by counsel before the wire is transmitted.Outbound settlement for raw heparin, chemical reagents, and packaging supplies should use SWIFT MT103 for ordinary wire payments and the ICC UCP 600-compliant commercial letter of credit (MT700) for high-value or documentary trade. Canadian law does not treat online European-style EMI wallet providers as banks: credit-issuing and deposit-taking remain restricted to federally or provincially regulated financial institutions. Marketplace lenders and account-issuing fintechs must be compliant with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), supervised by the Bank of Canada, and must obtain a money-services business or restricted/dealing foreign-exchange registration with FINTRAC where applicable. Online EMI accounts that merely attach virtual CAD/USD accounts do not provide clearing-house access, cheque-clearing capability, or confirmed SWIFT correspondent reliability; they should be used strictly for low-value vendor payments and never for the settlement of letter-of-credit transactions or capital escrow obligations.
