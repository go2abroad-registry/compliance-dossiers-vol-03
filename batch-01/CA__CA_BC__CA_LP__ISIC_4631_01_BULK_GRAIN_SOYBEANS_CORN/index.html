Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada, Business Number and GST/HST Directorate, portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/cra-business); British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Provincial Tax Administration (Consumer Taxation Branch), portal: BC eTaxBC (https://etaxbc.gov.bc.ca)

E-government filing portal

Step 1: A foreign general partner or any non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada through the LP must submit Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) to the CRA, either online via Business Registration Online (BRO) or by mail, to obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) for the partnership. Step 2: The LP must activate the GST/HST account (RT program account) by filing Form RC1A or through BRO, indicating the effective registration date; registration is mandatory if the LP is not a 'small supplier' (worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the last four consecutive calendar quarters) or if it supplies real property in Canada; voluntary registration is available to claim input tax credits. Step 3: For BC PST, the LP must register as a 'vendor' under the Provincial Sales Tax Act via eTaxBC (Form COR), providing the BN (the PST account is linked to the BN as a BC 'Provincial Sales Tax' program account), and is required to collect PST on taxable goods/services supplied in BC; no numerical registration threshold applies for non-resident vendors making taxable sales in BC. Step 4: The LP must file annual T5013 Partnership Information Return with the CRA and issue T5013 slips to partners; a filing exemption may apply only where the partnership has no corporate or partnership members, no non-resident members? (Form T5013 is required where the partnership has a corporation or another partnership as a member, has six or more members, or any member is a non-resident and the partnership is a 'Canadian partnership' carrying on business). Step 5: The LP should also obtain an Import/Export (RM) program account for customs reporting and a customs sub-account for CERS export declarations, and may require a payroll (RP) account if it has employees.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No Canadian equivalent of the US 'check-the-box' election exists; the BC Limited Partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent for income tax (each partner includes its share of income/loss in its own tax return) and is a deemed 'person' for GST/HST purposes in its own right (ETA s. 123(1)(p) and registration rules). Available elections include: (i) ITA s. 34.1/34.2 election to adopt a non-calendar fiscal period for the partnership; (ii) ETA s. 156 election for taxable supplies between the partnership and a 'closely related' corporate partner to be deemed made for nil consideration (requires 90%+ ownership integration); (iii) ITA s. 97(2) election for a partner transferring eligible capital property to the partnership on a tax-deferred rollover basis; (iv) ITA s. 216 election for a non-resident partner to file a return based on the LP's Canadian-source business income? (in practice a non-resident partner carrying on business in Canada through the LP must file a Part I return on a net basis); (v) designation of the LP as a 'registered Canadian partnership' (E File? no - not a statutory election); (vi) hybrid mismatch or 'excessive interest and financing expenses' (EIF) documentation elections? There is no deduction-denial election; instead, the partners must document that no 'deduction/non-inclusion' or 'double deduction' outcome arises under ITA s. 18.4 and s. 94B for hybrid arrangements involving foreign partners, particularly US LLC members whose entity classification differs between Canada and the US; a partner can rely on a 'substance' exception by electing to file the prescribed annual information return in prescribed form (ITA s. 18.4(5) requires prescribed documentation to be completed rather than an election).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or in the four consecutive preceding calendar quarters ('small supplier' threshold under ETA s. 148); BC PST: no numeric turnover threshold—any person making taxable sales, leases or rentals in BC must register as a PST vendor, except a non-resident whose only BC activity is attending a trade show/convention for four or fewer days in a calendar year

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST/HST is available under Part V of Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act for: (i) goods exported by the registrant-supplier—grain shipped by the LP from Canada to a foreign port where the supplier exports the goods through delivery to a carrier or by mail, provided export evidence is retained; and (ii) any supply of a service made to a non-resident person (who is neither registered under Part IX nor resident in Canada) under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7, unless the service is performed in connection with real property situated in Canada, is a personal service performed in Canada, or is performed in connection with goods situated in Canada at the time the service is performed and those goods are not for export. For a grain brokerage commission, the LP must verify with written evidence (ETA s. 178 notification) that the principal/counterparty is a non-resident not registered for GST/HST; if the LP acts as agent/commissionaire under ETA s. 177, the underlying supply of export grain is attributed to the principal and the separate commission charged to that non-resident principal will qualify for zero-rating; a commission charged to a Canadian-resident seller or buyer is taxable at 5% GST and may be claimed as an input tax credit by the resident recipient. Scrutiny requires contemporaneous trade flow documentation proving the goods left Canada within a commercially reasonable period and that the commission is not tied to services performed while the grain is located in Canada before export.

  • Ocean Bill of Lading or other through-bill of lading with foreign discharge port evidencing physical export from Canada
  • Commercial invoice and signed grain sale/purchase contract (including Incoterms such as FOB/CIF, quality certificate, weight certificate and vessel nomination)

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control mandate, digital stamping or government API clearance exists in Canada at federal or BC level; e-invoicing is voluntary, with the Pan-Canadian E-invoicing Framework promoting PEPPOL BIS 3.0 formats; mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns applies to registrants whose annual gross revenue exceeds CAD 1,500,000 in the most recent fiscal year (GST/HST NETFILE or approved EDI); all GST/HST returns and payments must be filed/remitted electronically above that threshold; BC PST returns are filed electronically via eTaxBC (monthly, quarterly or annual frequency based on PST payable volume); CRA maintains an electronic records mandate for all accounting systems, and export declarations must be transmitted electronically through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

Canada imposes no separate statutory export-proceeds repatriation deadline; the CRA's 180-day benchmark is the standard documentary-preservation window during which the exporter must substantiate zero-rating with a complete trace: CERS/B13A declaration, bill of lading, payment proof and non-resident status evidence (ETA s. 169, s. 178 and s. 179). If the CRA audits and finds inadequate export evidence, the zero-rating is reversed and net tax of 5% GST is assessed with arrears interest compounded daily; deliberate omissions or gross negligence in books/records attract penalties under ETA ss. 280 and 285 of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax amount, and misrepresentations in returns can also trigger the general civil penalty under ETA s. 280(1) plus possible criminal prosecution for evasion under ETA s. 326.1 (fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years). In BC, the PST exemption for exported goods is disallowed if the vendor does not retain a copy of the CERS B13A and delivery evidence, leading to a PST assessment at 7% plus interest and a penalty of up to 25% under the Provincial Sales Tax Act, with liability enforceable against the LP and its registered non-resident general partner.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility for both current and capital accounts under the Foreign Exchange Act. As a Canadian resident limited partnership, all current account transactions including profit remittances and trade settlements are freely convertible without prior approval. Capital account transactions are unrestricted. Profit remittance faces no material hurdles beyond standard corporate governance and AML/KYC obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canadian exporters must repatriate foreign exchange proceeds to Canada. No mandatory surrender ratios or conversion requirements apply due to the floating exchange rate. Proceeds should be received promptly; transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 trigger FINTRAC reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Best practice is repatriation within six months for tax compliance.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Canada: 15% federal + 12% British Columbia provincial = 27.00% combined general corporate rate. The Limited Partnership (LP) is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes and pays no entity-level income tax; income flows to partners and is taxed in their hands. Corporate general/limited partners carrying on business in Canada through the LP are taxable at the combined 27.00% general rate on their allocable share, and individual partners are taxable at progressive federal and BC personal rates (top combined marginal rate approximately 53.5%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory rate under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (ITA s.212(2)) on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident payer to a non-resident beneficial owner. In the LP context, guaranteed payments and distributions to non-resident partners are characterized by source: interest, dividends, royalties, or rental amounts sourced in Canada remain subject to Part XIII; business income allocated through a Canadian PE is taxed under Part I on a T2 return instead.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

On or before the last day of the fifth month following the partnership's fiscal period end (e.g., December 31 fiscal year-end is due May 31).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The CRA's contemporaneous documentation expectation applies where the aggregate gross value of non-arm's-length transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year (per Information Circular IC 87-2R and CRA Transfer Pricing Memorandum TPM-01). Mandatory Country-by-Country Reporting and associated Master File/Local File expectations arise for multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of CAD 750,000,000 or more. Penalties under ITA s.247(3) are 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue and adequate contemporaneous documentation is not produced within a reasonable period.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the Cayman/BCI sense. CRA instead applies: (1) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), which recharacterizes artificial transactions and denies tax benefits lacking a genuine economic or business purpose; (2) the common-law sham doctrine and the agency/partnership substance tests; (3) the permanent establishment definitions under ITA s.253 and the Canada-China DTA to ensure the LP's Canadian brokerage income is not artificially routed offshore; and (4) the 'at-risk' limitations on limited-partner loss claims. The LP must maintain real employees, office premises, broker mandates, trade documentation, risk-management functions, and bank accounts in British Columbia to avoid de-recognition of the Canadian business.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: On indictment under ITA s.239(1), a fine of 100% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment for a term of up to 5 years; on summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 200% plus imprisonment up to 2 years. In addition, gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax apply under ITA s.163(2). Criminal Code fraud (s.380) carries imprisonment up to 14 years where the value exceeds CAD 5,000.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and general partners who participated in, assented to, or acquiesced in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for fines and restitution. CRA can issue jeopardy orders freezing personal assets, obtain civil judgments, and impose travel bans by court order preventing departure from Canada. A foreign national director (e.g., Chinese resident) convicted of an offence carrying a maximum prison term of ten years or more may become inadmissible to Canada under IRPA s.36(1)(c) (serious criminality), triggering removal proceedings.
Failure to File / Part XIII Withholding Failures

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties: 5% plus up to 12% additional monthly penalties for T2; CAD 25 per day to CAD 2,500 for T5013; enhanced CAD 100 per day for knowing omissions. Withholding failure under ITA s.227(8): the payer is liable for the full unremitted amount plus prescribed daily compound interest and a penalty of 10% (20% for a second failure within five years). CRA may assess the partnership or the general partner directly and register a lien against the LP's assets.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are personally liable for unremitted withholding tax under ITA s.227.1, with a two-year due-diligence defence available only if they exercised the care, diligence, and skill of a reasonably prudent person. CRA may issue a 'jeopardy' certificate under s.225.2 and seek a court order prohibiting departure from Canada while the debt is outstanding. Willful non-remittance may also be prosecuted criminally with imprisonment.
Shell Company / Artificial Limited Partnership Structure

Statutory sanction: GAAR under ITA s.245 denies the tax benefit and imposes a 25% penalty on the amount of the tax benefit (ITA s.245(4)). CRA may reassess beyond the normal three-year statutory period using the avoidance-transaction exception. The partnership may be disregarded entirely, or the general partner may be deemed to carry on business in Canada, resulting in full Part I tax, interest, and penalties including the 50% gross-negligence penalty.

Director personal liability: Under the BC Partnership Act, a limited partner who takes part in the control of the business loses limited liability and becomes liable for all partnership obligations. CRA will pierce the LP where it is a sham, leaving the general partner and controlling partners personally liable for tax, penalties, and interest. Directors may face civil restitution actions from creditors, receivership orders over personal property, and extradition or admittance refusal where criminal fraud is established.
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act of up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for entities and up to CAD 500,000 per violation for individuals. Criminal prosecution for laundering proceeds of crime under Criminal Code s.462.31 carries imprisonment up to 10 years. FINTRAC can suspend or revoke the LP's registration and refer files to the RCMP.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and the general partner who knowingly fail to implement a FINTRAC compliance program or who direct concealment of beneficial ownership are personally exposed. Travel bans may be imposed pending criminal investigation, and convictions for money laundering or terrorist financing offences render non-resident directors inadmissible to Canada under IRPA s.36(1)(b), with removal proceedings and re-entry bars. Civil liability to unpaid creditors and co-partners also arises under BC partnership and corporate law.
