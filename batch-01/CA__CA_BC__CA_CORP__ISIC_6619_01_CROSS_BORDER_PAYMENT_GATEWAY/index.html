Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - a Canadian business corporation is a separate taxable entity subject to federal and BC corporate income tax; no pass-through status is available for an INC./CORP. under Canadian tax law.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists. Elections include: GST/HST consolidated registration for closely related corporations (Excise Tax Act s.150); Income Tax Act s.85 rollover for transfers to a corporation; Part XIII withholding tax elections; and limited grouping for Canadian-controlled private corporations. Hybrid mismatch rules under ITA s.163.2 and Part VI.1 operate as anti-avoidance, not elective provisions.

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST: CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies over four consecutive calendar quarters (voluntary registration permitted); BC PST: CAD 10,000 or more in taxable sale/lease revenue in a calendar year. Foreign digital payment services supplied to Canadian customers may trigger registration if the threshold is met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for goods exported from Canada (physical export with proof) and for certain services provided to non-residents, including export financial services under ETA Sch. VI that are supplied to non-residents and are primarily for use outside Canada. Payment gateway fees for cross-border merchants can qualify when the recipient is a non-resident and no Canadian input is consumed; supporting commercial evidence is mandatory. BC PST generally does not apply to exported goods/services.

  • B13B Export Declaration filed with Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) or authorized broker
  • Commercial invoice showing exporter, consignee, destination and complete service/goods description
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill confirming physical movement out of Canada
  • Bank transfer records and FC (foreign exchange) settlement reports evidencing cross-border payment
  • Executed service agreement with non-resident merchant and evidence of offshore use (e.g., API logs, gateway transaction metadata)

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping or API e-invoice clearance exists in Canada or British Columbia. Business-to-business e-invoicing is being voluntary adopted under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard (PEPPOL); federal government procurement requires electronic invoicing. PST invoices/records must be retained with prescribed content; returns are filed electronically via eTaxBC.

CRA can reassess within 4 years from the original assessment; the window is unlimited where misrepresentation, fraud or wilful default exists. False zero-rating claims attract a 25% civil penalty on the amount misstated under ETA s.285 and, for deliberate evasion, a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment (ETA s.327). Unsupported export refunds are denied with interest; collection may extend to 10 years. Professional advice and contemporaneous export evidence are essential.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility for both residents and non-residents under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. There are no general exchange-control restrictions; dividends, interest, management fees, royalties, and proceeds of capital dispositions may be repatriated freely in CAD or any convertible foreign currency. For a cross-border payment gateway, the operational constraints are not convertibility but (i) FINTRAC registration as a money services business (MSB) or foreign money services business (FMSB), (ii) anti-money-laundering compliance and transaction reporting, (iii) adherence to Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) guidelines on clearing and settlement risk, and (iv) Canadian Payments Association (Payments Canada) rulebook compliance if the entity obtains access to the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) or the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) through a sponsoring bank. Profit remittance hurdles for a British Columbia corporation are limited to corporate law solvency tests under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia) and any debt covenant restrictions; no exchange-control approval is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement or mandatory conversion ratio in Canada. Exporters and payment service providers may retain foreign currency balances in domestic or offshore accounts. Any CAD conversion is at commercial bank discretion based on market rates, and all cross-border wire transfers are effected through standard SWIFT MT103 or MT202 messages. A PSP acting as an intermediary for merchant export proceeds must nonetheless comply with FINTRAC record-keeping, verification, and reporting obligations for receipts above C$10,000 or for any amount if there are reasonable grounds to suspect a money laundering or terrorist financing offense.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CTF supervision; and the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for federally regulated bank conduct

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) chartered in the United Kingdom or European Economic Area cannot passport into Canada and are not authorized to hold deposits or provide payment clearing accounts as a primary regulated entity in Canada without registering as an MSB/FMSB under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and, if they hold Canadian client funds, being subject to FINTRAC compliance. The operational viability of using an offshore EMI for Canadian treasury is Medium for receiving settlement funds from foreign acquiring banks, but Low for on-behalf-of Canadian merchant settlement, because Canadian funds must flow through a Canadian chartered bank or an MSB with a Canadian clearing sponsor. The recommended structure is a dual-rail: a Canadian Schedule I bank account for CAD funding and merchant settlements, with an offshore EMI used only for collections from Asia-Pacific or EU acquiring platforms where merchant settlement occurs in EUR/GBP. The EMI must not be used for local CAD payout to avoid bank-act and payments-act regulatory infringement.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Initial subscribed capital is applied to a Canadian bank in British Columbia through a standard SWIFT MT103 inbound wire from the shareholder's own bank account. The purpose of payment must clearly designate 'Paid-up capital for British Columbia business corporation – OP/CA-CORP' together with the company's Canadian Business Number (CRA BN) and the corporate GST/HST registry number where applicable. The remitter must provide an attestation that funds are derived from legitimate sources and not from a shell entity lacking economic substance; supporting documents include the resolution of directors authorizing issuance of shares, a share subscription agreement, a certified corporate register showing shareholders, and a receipt from the bank acknowledging funds received. For investments above C$100,000, Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) and FINTRAC may require the bank to submit a Large Cash Transaction Report (if cash is involved) or an Electronic Funds Transfer Report (EFTR) for wire transfers over C$10,000. No prior CRA or Bank of Canada approval is required. Funds must be credited to an operational account and cannot be held in an escrow account without a written escrow agreement. The inbound wire must not be structured as a loan without contemporaneous loan documentation, because a loan with excessive leverage may be recharacterized as part of paid-up capital under the Income Tax Act thin-capitalization rules.Outbound merchant settlements and trade disbursements are executed over the following clearing rails: (1) Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for same-day CAD high-value payments in Canada, cleared in Canadian dollars through Payments Canada; (2) SWIFT MT103 for international correspondent banking wires, used for USD, EUR, GBP, and other foreign-currency settlements outside Canada; (3) SWIFT MT202 for bank-to-bank cover payment ordering among the payment gateway, its sponsor bank, and international correspondent banks; (4) Payments Canada Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) for batch low-value Canadian-dollar payments to merchants and suppliers; (5) commercial letters of credit (documentary credits) opened by the payment gateway's Canadian banker for overseas procurement of technology infrastructure, issued against invoices and transport documents where applicable; and (6) card network settlement via Visa Net and Mastercard Global Clearing Management System, if the gateway is a principal member or third-party processor with an acquiring sponsor. For each outbound settlement, the SWIFT MT103 must state a valid purpose code (for example, type 601A, 601B, or 602A according to the counterparty's jurisdiction; for Canadian outbound flows the CRA and Statistics Canada require the payment purpose to be classified in accordance with the Canadian Balance of Payments classification, such as 'Other business services' or 'Financial services'). The beneficiary bank account must be pre-verified through an account-name-match service, and each outbound wire must be screened against the Consolidated Canadian Autonomous Sanctions List, the United Nations sanctions list, and OFAC SDN List, even if the transaction is not a US-person transaction, to protect correspondent banking access.Spot FX contracts with a Canadian Schedule I bank or authorized foreign exchange dealer for CAD/USD and CAD/EUR conversion, not subject to exchange-control approval because Canada has free capital flows.Outright forward foreign exchange contracts (vanilla deliverable forwards) for up to 12 months, entered into under an ISDA Master Agreement or a bank FX line, with credit support annex if required; E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) chartered in the United Kingdom or European Economic Area cannot passport into Canada and are not authorized to hold deposits or provide payment clearing accounts as a primary regulated entity in Canada without registering as an MSB/FMSB under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and, if they hold Canadian client funds, being subject to FINTRAC compliance. The operational viability of using an offshore EMI for Canadian treasury is Medium for receiving settlement funds from foreign acquiring banks, but Low for on-behalf-of Canadian merchant settlement, because Canadian funds must flow through a Canadian chartered bank or an MSB with a Canadian clearing sponsor. The recommended structure is a dual-rail: a Canadian Schedule I bank account for CAD funding and merchant settlements, with an offshore EMI used only for collections from Asia-Pacific or EU acquiring platforms where merchant settlement occurs in EUR/GBP. The EMI must not be used for local CAD payout to avoid bank-act and payments-act regulatory infringement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a fixed CA$ threshold above which transfer pricing documentation is mandatory. However, penalties under s. 247(3) apply when the net transfer pricing adjustment exceeds the lesser of CA$5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue for the year. The taxpayer must prepare and retain contemporaneous documentation (including industry analysis, comparability, and method selection) before the T2 filing deadline to obtain penalty relief. For controlled transactions involving a PE in Canada, the same documentation standards apply to all cross-border amounts.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) regime comparable to Caribbean/offshore centres. Instead, the Income Tax Act contains the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) (s. 245) which denies a tax benefit arising from an avoidance transaction lacking bona fide business purpose or economic substance. A BC payment-services corporation must demonstrate real local substance: actual employees, premises, treasury functions, and operational decision-making in British Columbia. If the Canadian entity is a mere shell funneling fees through a tax-haven parent, the CRA will re-characterize the arrangement, deem the parent to have a Canadian PE, and assess full Canadian tax on net income plus penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under section 239 of the Income Tax Act: fine up to 200% of the income tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, or both. Civil reassessment with gross negligence penalty of 50% on the understated tax (s. 163(2)). CRA may also assess arrears interest compounded daily.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and accountants who knowingly participate in tax fraud may face personal monetary penalties, restitution orders, travel bans, and imprisonment. CRA can also use civil remedy under section 160 to recover assets transferred to shareholders or related persons.
Shell Company / Anti-Surplus Stripping

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess on behalf of the non-resident parent under section 253 (deemed PE), impose full Canadian tax on the attributable income, deny deductions for the shell's intercompany expenses, and apply GAAR penalties (s. 245). Surplus stripping rules under s. 212.1 can convert tax-free dividends into taxable deemed dividends and apply Part XIII withholding at 25%.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the corporate tax debt arising from a sham re-assessment under s. 227.1 if they authorized the artificial structure. A travel ban may be issued under the Revenue Collection Act to prevent leaving Canada while tax liabilities remain outstanding.
AML / MSB Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMP) from FINTRAC up to CA$1,000,000 per violation for a body corporate. Failure to report suspicious transactions or large cash transaction reports under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can also trigger criminal liability under the Criminal Code for laundering proceeds of crime (imprisonment up to 10 years).

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly fail to ensure AML compliance may be prosecuted for criminal negligence and money-laundering; they face personal fines, imprisonment, and prohibition from acting as directors. FINTRAC may also publicly name the corporation, causing irreparable reputational damage.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: A 10% transfer pricing penalty is imposed on the net adjustment when the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CA$5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue. The full adjustment is added to income, plus back taxes and interest. If the taxpayer files false statements, the 50% gross negligence penalty may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve intercompany prices without personal knowledge of their arm's length nature can be held liable for the corporate penalty if they were knowingly directing the transaction (director's liability under the Income Tax Act). In egregious cases, the CRA may seek a court order to recover amounts from directors personally under s. 160.
Director's Personal Liability for Unremitted Source Deductions and GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Under Excise Tax Act s. 323 and Income Tax Act s. 227.1, the corporation's directors are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted amounts, including interest and penalties. CRA can pursue civil collection against directors' personal assets, impose garnishments, and freeze bank accounts without a court order.

Director personal liability: Directors' liability is automatic; diligence defences are limited. Directors may be subject to personal bankruptcy proceedings, civil restitution orders, and prohibition from serving as directors of any Canadian corporation. In cases involving criminal unpaid taxes, directors can face imprisonment under the Income Tax Act.
