Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Competent federal tax authority: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC). Provincial sales tax administration: British Columbia Ministry of Finance, BC Taxation Branch, via BC eTaxBC portal. Federal online portal: CRA My Business Account / Represent a Client.

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident limited partnership carrying on business in Canada must determine whether it is required to register for the CRA Business Number (BN). If the partnership makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not a small supplier, it must apply for a BN and a GST/HST program account before the first taxable supply. Application is made using CRA Form RC1, delivered by paper, by mail, or online through an authorized representative via Represent a Client / My Business Account. The foreign LP must supply its partnership agreement, proof of legal existence from its home jurisdiction, and confirm the names, addresses, and residency of the general partner and in-Canada authorized representative. CRA issues a 9-digit BN with a 2-letter program identifier (e.g., RT0001) for GST/HST. Since the partnership is fiscally transparent for income tax, the BN is also used for T5013 partnership information return filing and any payroll program accounts (RP) if the LP has Canadian employees. For British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax, the LP must register as a non-resident seller through the BC eTaxBC portal by submitting the PST-A application, before making taxable retail sales into BC. The seller may be required to appoint an authorized agent in BC for service and record-keeping purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not operate an IRS-style 'check-the-box' entity election. A British Columbia limited partnership is fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act; no federal election can change this classification. Relevant elections include: (i) partnership fiscal period election under subsection 249.1(4) of the Income Tax Act; (ii) section 97(2) rollover election for property transferred to the partnership; (iii) section 98(2)/98(3) elections on partnership termination; (iv) section 216 or 216.1 elections for non-residents earning Canadian rental or timber income; and (v) hybrid mismatch elections/de minimis provisions under sections 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act that may apply to disregarded payments from foreign branch or hybrid entities. For GST/HST, the 'special quick method' for registrants may be elected in certain sectors, and a non-resident registrant may elect to use a simplified reporting procedure under section 243 of the Excise Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Federal GST/HST small supplier threshold: CAD 30,000 in worldwide annual gross taxable supplies, measured over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. BC PST: no general monetary threshold; a non-resident or remote seller must register if it makes taxable sales into BC through a regular course of trade, including catalogue or internet solicitation, unless the transaction qualifies as a casual one-off sale.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For GST/HST, a supply of tangible personal property is zero-rated where the supplier exports the goods, where the goods are delivered by the supplier to a common carrier for delivery outside Canada, or where the supply qualifies under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act (e.g., goods sold to a non-resident for use outside Canada). Services are generally zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident and not consumed in Canada, or when performed wholly outside Canada, subject to exceptions for services in respect of real property located in Canada or tangible personal property ordinarily used in Canada. For BC PST, goods sold for delivery outside British Columbia by the vendor or a common carrier are exempt from PST if the property leaves BC and the vendor retains documentary proof of export and delivery outside BC.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A or automated Canadian Export Reporting System output for controlled or statistical goods, where required)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / Cargo Manifest
  • Inward Remittance Certificate
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List stating the export destination and applicable Incoterms
  • Certified proof of export (carrier certification, port authority receipt, foreign customs entry evidence, or equivalent)

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API-inspected e-invoicing, or digital stamping regime is currently in force in Canada or British Columbia. A GST/HST registrant must issue invoices containing prescribed particulars (supplier name, BN, invoice date, description, tax rates, and tax amounts) and retain all accounting records, including electronic records, for six years. The CRA is participating in voluntary pan-Canadian e-invoice interoperability initiatives and may accept electronic invoices, but there is no pre-clearance obligation. BC PST requires ordinary sales invoices or receipts containing enough particulars to verify the tax collected; no electronic fiscal device is mandated.

CRA scrutinizes export zero-rating claims by verifying the exporter's documentary evidence. If the evidence is insufficient, the zero-rating may be reversed and the registrant may be assessed GST/HST on the full supply, with arrears interest and penalties. Willful false statements or omissions attract penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act (5% of the tax sought to be evaded plus 1% per month up to 12 months), gross negligence penalties up to 50% of the understated tax, and potential criminal prosecution. Under BC PST, a vendor who fails to collect or remit PST may be assessed for uncollected tax, interest, and penalties under the Provincial Sales Tax Act, and the exemption for export sales is denied unless the vendor proves the goods left British Columbia. Required records, including customs and shipping documents, must be retained for six years from the end of the taxation period to which they relate.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a free-floating, fully convertible currency regime. There are no exchange controls on current-account payments (imports of industrial MRO goods, supplier invoices, freight, customs duties) or on capital-account movements (limited-partner capital contributions, partner loan repayments, profit distributions, and repatriation of after-tax operating earnings). No prior approval, FX licence, 'approved remittance' application, or balance-of-payments rationing is required from the Bank of Canada, the Department of Finance, or any provincial authority. The practical friction points are tax compliance and documentary evidence rather than convertibility: (i) business income allocated to a non-resident limited partner constitutes taxable income earned in Canada under section 115 of the Income Tax Act and must be reported on a Canadian non-resident income tax return; (ii) interest, royalties, management fees, and certain deemed distributions paid to non-resident partners are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at 25% (treaty-reduced, e.g., 10-15% under the Canada-US treaty); (iii) thin-capitalization and transfer-pricing rules under sections 18(4), 247, and 212 of the Income Tax Act apply to non-resident partner debt and cross-border service payments; and (iv) the receiving bank will require source-of-funds documentation for CRS/FATCA classification of each partner. Foreign exchange for profit remittance is available at any Canadian bank at the market rate, and there are no blocked-funds or repatriation-timing restrictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada does not impose any statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation timeline. The BC LP selling industrial MRO supplies through electronic catalog retail may retain export sales proceeds in foreign currency indefinitely or convert them to CAD at its bank's market rate. FINTRAC imposes only informational reporting: every incoming or outgoing electronic funds transfer (SWIFT or ACH) of C$10,000 or more must be reported by the Canadian financial entity within 15 business days; physical currency or monetary instruments of C$10,000 or more crossing the border must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency. These obligations are surveillance measures, not surrender or conversion rules, and do not restrict the LP's treasury autonomy.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority and currency issuer; prudential regulation of federally chartered banks is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA) regulates provincially incorporated credit unions and trust companies; FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) administers the federal AML/ATF regime and cross-border electronic funds transfer reporting; the Minister of Finance and the Canada Border Services Agency support sanctions and cross-border currency declaration enforcement.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium for ancillary treasury use; Low for capital escrow and principal government/tax payments. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, Nium, dLocal) are not Canadian chartered banks and do not hold deposits covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). They typically operate as money-services businesses (MSBs) maintaining pooled program accounts at sponsor banks, which introduces independent insolvency and legal-subordination risk for the LP. Legal viability in British Columbia requires that (i) the EMI itself be registered with FINTRAC as an MSB if it provides remittance or virtual-currency services to Canadian-resident clients; (ii) the EMI comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, including a Canadian AML program, if it has a Canadian nexus; and (iii) any services physically or systemically marketed within BC respect BC's provincial money-services regulatory framework where applicable. The LP should reserve the EMI channel for fragmented operational supplier disbursements under C$25,000 per transaction, benchmark pricing against the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate, and never route limited-partner capital injections, escrow releases, or CRA tax remittances through the EMI. The primary legal clearing spine must remain a CDIC-insured Canadian bank account opened in the name of the limited partnership through its general partner.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound protocol for a BC limited partnership: (1) the originating limited partner instructs its bank to execute a SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 if through a correspondent intermediary) denominated in CAD, USD, or EUR, with the 'For Further Credit To' field naming the LP, the receiving Canadian bank, and the segregated Capital Escrow Account number; (2) the remittance should carry an ISO 20022 purpose code appropriate to a capital advance, primarily 'CAPT' (Capital Payment) with the free-text 'Limited partner capital contribution under LP agreement dated [date]'; for a partner loan, use 'INTC' (intra-company payment) and attach a term loan agreement; (3) USD inbound transfers clear through the Canadian bank's US correspondent or its US banking subsidiary (e.g., RBC Bank (Georgia) or TD Bank, N.A.) into the Canadian USD account; CAD legs settle in real time through Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS); (4) the receiving bank screens the transfer against OFAC, UN, and Canadian sanctions lists under the Special Economic Measures Act, classifies the sender for FINTRAC purposes, and files a Large Electronic Funds Transfer Report to FINTRAC within 15 business days for any EFT of C$10,000 or more; (5) the LP must contemporaneously produce a capital-contribution ledger and supporting partnership-source documents; no Bank of Canada approval, FX allocation, or surrender obligation attaches to the injection; and (6) if the contribution is later converted into CAD, the LP should execute the conversion as a spot trade with its bank's treasury desk and retain the FX confirmation for audit and tax basis purposes.Outbound treasury rail for trade settlements: (a) for supplier invoices below the bank's high-value threshold and for routine operating payments, use SWIFT MT103 corporate wires with purpose code 'TRAD' (Trade Payment) or 'INTC' for intra-group settlements; CAD domestic vendor payments settle via Payments Canada LVTS for wire-value payments or via the Retail Batch System (the ACSS successor) for batch ACH payments; (b) for large purchase orders or when an overseas MRO supplier demands payment security, open a documentary import Letter of Credit through the Canadian bank's trade-services desk under UCP 600 and, where required, obtain confirmation from a correspondent bank in the supplier's jurisdiction; (c) USD disbursements should be drawn from the LP's USD clearing account and settled through the Canadian bank's US correspondent (Fedwire or CHIPS); EUR and other currency legs are converted at the bank's treasury desk under the LP's FX master agreement; E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Medium for ancillary treasury use; Low for capital escrow and principal government/tax payments. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, Nium, dLocal) are not Canadian chartered banks and do not hold deposits covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC). They typically operate as money-services businesses (MSBs) maintaining pooled program accounts at sponsor banks, which introduces independent insolvency and legal-subordination risk for the LP. Legal viability in British Columbia requires that (i) the EMI itself be registered with FINTRAC as an MSB if it provides remittance or virtual-currency services to Canadian-resident clients; (ii) the EMI comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, including a Canadian AML program, if it has a Canadian nexus; and (iii) any services physically or systemically marketed within BC respect BC's provincial money-services regulatory framework where applicable. The LP should reserve the EMI channel for fragmented operational supplier disbursements under C$25,000 per transaction, benchmark pricing against the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate, and never route limited-partner capital injections, escrow releases, or CRA tax remittances through the EMI. The primary legal clearing spine must remain a CDIC-insured Canadian bank account opened in the name of the limited partnership through its general partner.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal and British Columbia general corporate rate (federal 15% + BC provincial 12%); 11% combined rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income under the Small Business Deduction (federal 9% + BC 2%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident company to a non-resident beneficial owner; the rate applies to the taxable portion of dividends distributed by a Canadian corporate partner, not to an LP allocation per se.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 10,000,000 gross revenue is CRA's administrative threshold below which the 'reasonable efforts' defense applies; above CAD 10,000,000, contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation must be prepared, and a demand from CRA requires production within 3 months. Canada does not maintain a separate Master File/Local File threshold; CRA applies OECD-aligned documentation standards.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulation regime. Anti-avoidance substance doctrines include: (i) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245), which permits CRA to deny tax benefits from transactions lacking genuine economic substance or business purpose; (ii) 'sham' doctrine at common law; (iii) the deemed permanent establishment rules for non-resident partners of Canadian partnerships; and (iv) the BC Partnership Act, which requires that a limited partner not participate in control of the LP — substantive commercial operations conducted from the LP's BC premises and genuine trading with third-party MRO vendors are necessary to preserve both limited liability and tax allocation credibility.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under s. 163(2): 50% of the understated tax. Criminal conviction under s. 238(1): fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment (or up to 2 years less a day on summary conviction).

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, or limited partners who directed, authorized, assented to, or acquiesced in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for the penalty and can be personally charged criminally. CRA may issue a certificate of non-compliance leading to a travel ban through the Federal Court route; asset seizure and civil restitution orders are available under s. 462.34 Proceeds of Crime provisions.
Late Filing / Failure to File Returns

Statutory sanction: T5013: CAD 25/day up to CAD 2,500 (or CAD 100/day up to CAD 10,000 for 51+ partners). T2: 5% penalty plus 1% per month up to 17%; further, failure to comply with a demand-to-file is punishable by fine between CAD 1,000 and CAD 25,000 plus imprisonment for up to 12 months (summary conviction).

Director personal liability: Directors' liability under s. 227.1 attaches to unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and other specified amounts, not ordinary corporate income tax; however, where the LP is used to channel funds abroad while obligations are unpaid, the directors are jointly liable and a CRA registration of the debt may suspend trade and trigger asset freezes. For an LP, a limited partner who participates in day-to-day control loses limited liability and faces joint liability for all partnership debts.
Transfer Pricing / Cross-Border Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment under s. 247(2) with upward income adjustment; 10% transfer-pricing penalty under s. 247(3) where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue and documentation is not made available; failure to provide demanded records attracts a penalty of CAD 1,000 per month (minimum CAD 5,000, maximum CAD 25,000).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign non-compliant pricing arrangements can be assessed personally for gross-negligence penalties (s. 163.1); officers can be prosecuted under s. 242 where they participate in tax evasion; travel restrictions and exit-control orders may be applied where criminal referral occurs for egregious profit-stripping.
GAAR / Artificial Loss & Shell-Like Structure

Statutory sanction: GAAR denial of the tax benefit under s. 245(2), imposition of a 25% penalty of the denied benefit where the transaction is misrepresentation-based (s. 245(4)), and interest charges compounded on the reassessment.

Director personal liability: CRA may pierce the LP structure under the 'sham' doctrine, leaving partners jointly and severally liable for the partnership's obligations; directors of a corporate general partner may incur personal liability for dividends or distributions paid out of misstated reserves, and a court order may impose civil restitution and asset freezing; willful participation may lead to criminal tax-evasion charges and a ban on cross-border travel.
Anti-Money Laundering (PCMLTFA) Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: up to CAD 1,000,000 for a corporation per violation for non-compliance with record-keeping, reporting, and foreign-asset verification requirements; criminal prosecution for laundering proceeds under the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors who knowingly fail to report a suspicious transaction face personal fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; CRA and FINTRAC coordination may trigger a tax-evasion referral, and directors of the corporate general partner can be held civilly liable for breach of fiduciary duty, with restraining orders impeding foreign travel.
