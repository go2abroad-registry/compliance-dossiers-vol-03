Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); federal registration portals: Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (MyBA); BC provincial nexus: BC Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC portal) for Provincial Sales Tax (PST); corporate registry: BC Corporate Online operated by BC Registry for the Certificate of Limited Partnership.

E-government filing portal

A British Columbia limited partnership is not a separate taxpayer for income tax but is allocated a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with dedicated CRA program accounts, principally the RT (GST/HST) account and RP (payroll) account where applicable. Step 1: file Form LP1 under the BC Partnership Act to create the limited partnership and obtain a BC Certificate of Limited Partnership, maintaining a registered office and records office in BC. Step 2: apply for the BN through Business Registration Online, by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts', or by written request to the CRA Business Window; non-resident partners may appoint an authorized Canadian representative using Form RC59. Step 3: supply a notarized partnership agreement, BC Certificate of Limited Partnership, complete partner register (name, jurisdiction of residence, and CRA account numbers of corporate partners), registered office address, and a description of business activity mapped to NAICS 425120. Step 4: register the GST/HST RT account, which is mandatory once taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in the preceding four calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter, and register with eTaxBC for PST if the partnership sells tangible personal property in BC. Step 5: file Form T5013 Partnership Information Return annually, allocating income and losses to partners under section 96 of the Income Tax Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible: the LP is fiscally transparent under Income Tax Act subsection 96(1), pays no entity-level income tax, and each partner reports distributive net income or loss. For GST/HST purposes, the partnership is a separate 'person' under the Excise Tax Act and is the registrant of record for the RT account, subject to a designated-partner election. Territorial scope: resident partners are taxed on their worldwide allocable shares; non-resident partners are taxed only on Canadian-source allocable business income, with potential Part XIV branch tax or treaty relief; the LP's offshore re-invoicing margin is generally taxable in Canada where central management and control and the substance of the trading operations are exercised in British Columbia.

Available election forms

(i) Form RC459 election designating one partner as the single GST/HST registrant-administrator for the partnership under ETA section 273; (ii) partnership-agreement allocations of income and loss under ITA section 96(1), binding on the CRA if determined at arm's length on a reasonable basis; (iii) Canada has no US-style check-the-box regime, but US partners may file IRS Form 8832 to classify the Canadian LP for US federal tax purposes; (iv) Canada's hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA sections 18.2, 18.3 and associated deeming provisions) may deny deductions or re-characterize payments to the extent the arrangement produces double non-taxation through the LP's fiscal transparency; (v) election under ETA subsection 143(2) may be filed by a non-resident supplier to collect GST/HST on otherwise imported taxable supplies; (vi) an optional ITA section 216 election may be used by non-resident partners deriving Canadian property income, though it does not apply to active trading income of a re-invoicing hub.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: a person ceases to be a 'small supplier' and must register for GST when taxable supplies made in Canada total more than CAD 30,000 in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter; a non-resident person carrying on business in Canada or soliciting orders in Canada is denied small-supplier status for certain supplies, and registration is mandatory without regard to the CAD 30,000 threshold where the non-resident has a permanent establishment in Canada. For BC PST, there is no minimum vendor registration threshold; registration via eTaxBC is required before selling taxable tangible personal property in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated supplies are taxable supplies at 0% GST and therefore generate full input tax credit recovery. Eligibility criteria: (A) tangible personal property exported from Canada by the supplier within 60 days after the consideration becomes due, or exported by or on behalf of the recipient, or delivered outside Canada pursuant to the supply (ETA Schedule VI, Part V, section 6); (B) services supplied to a non-resident person who is not registered for GST/HST (ETA Schedule VI, Part V, section 7), provided the service does not relate to real estate situated in Canada, is not performed directly on tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance, and is not rendered to an individual present in Canada; (C) a pure offshore re-invoicing model where goods never physically enter Canada falls outside the scope of Canadian GST/HST entirely, while the BC LP's commission, margin, or agency fee on those flow-through trades qualifies for zero-rating as an offshore service to the non-resident principal; (D) transportation and freight-forwarding services related to exports are separately zero-rated under sections 8 and 10 of Schedule VI. Trade-finance services that accompany re-invoicing (letters of credit, guarantees, foreign-exchange forward settlements) are exempt supplies rather than zero-rated supplies, which permanently restricts input tax credit recovery on related costs and requires a fair and reasonable allocation methodology under ETA section 141.01.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) export declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for physical exports with a value above CAD 2,000, lodged within 15 days after export, or a Form B13A mail/courier declaration where CERS is unavailable; the CERS export confirmation number must be retained.
  • Original Bill of Lading (ocean, air waybill, or highway manifest) naming the BC LP as shipper/consignor and the offshore buyer as consignee, evidencing continuous movement of the goods to an export destination.
  • Commercial invoice and purchase-order trail showing the Canadian exporter, non-resident buyer, Incoterms, foreign-currency consideration, and a goods description cross-referenced to the CERS export declaration.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank settlement evidence: Canada has no statutory inward remittance certificate; the CRA accepts SWIFT MT103 credit advices, foreign-exchange conversion slips, bank statements, and aged accounts-receivable schedules evidencing offshore remittances within the 180-day commercial benchmark.
  • Packing lists, weight certificates, freight manifests, and CBSA e-manifest data corroborating the cross-border movement of goods, together with any certificates of origin or destination control statements.
  • For zero-rated offshore services: executed service or agency contract, performance evidence generated outside Canada, confirmation that the foreign principal is not a GST/HST registrant, and documentation demonstrating that no part of the service relates to Canadian realty or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance.

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing regime exists in Canada as of 2025. The CRA promotes voluntary adoption of Peppol BIS 3.0 / PINT e-invoicing standards for business-to-business transactions. GST/HST returns (Form GST34) must be filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or an approved E-file service provider, with net tax remitted to the CRA; BC PST returns are filed through eTaxBC. Books, records, and export substantiation documents must be retained for six years (ETA subsection 286(1), ITA subsection 230(1)) and, if kept outside Canada, must be produced electronically on CRA demand.

The CRA may reassess within the normal four-year reassessment window (unlimited where misrepresentation, fraud, or non-arm's-length transactions exist) to deny zero-rating, convert the supply to taxable status at 5% GST plus 7% BC PST where applicable, and claw back previously refunded input tax credits with statutory interest. Civil penalties include 25% of the net tax understated for false statements (ETA section 285(1)), a CAD 10,000 penalty for false valuations (ETA section 285.1), late-filing and failure-to-remit penalties (ETA section 280), and a 50% gross-negligence penalty on understated income tax (ITA section 163(2)). Criminal exposure arises under ETA sections 327-328 and ITA section 239, with fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment. Canada does not impose a statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, but unrepatriated export receivables are subject to ITA section 12(4) income-inclusion rules, and the 180-day benchmark is applied by the CRA and trading partners as an audit indicator of genuine offshore turnover. The CRA's Export Verification Project and the integrated CBSA-CRA export controls cross-match CERS declarations against GST/HST returns; fabricated export documentation triggers clawback of the entire input tax credit amount, denial of future zero-rating, and joint and several liability of general partners and participating limited partners under ETA subsection 272.1(1) and BC partnership law.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible Canadian dollar under a liberalized foreign exchange regime. Current account transactions, including trade settlements and profit remittances, face no restrictions. Capital account transactions, such as equity investments or loans exceeding thresholds, require prior Bank of Canada approval. Profit remittances by Canadian entities to non-resident limited partners are permitted without restriction, subject to Income Tax Act reporting and AML compliance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender of export proceeds to the Bank of Canada. Exporters may retain foreign currency offshore provided repatriation occurs within a reasonable timeframe for operational and tax purposes. All export sales must be recorded under the Income Tax Act; non-repatriation may trigger adverse tax treatment or regulatory review under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Fintech electronic money institutions face stringent regulatory barriers for cross-border corporate services in Canada. Licensed banks remain the only viable channel for full corporate banking, trade finance, and FX services. Offshore digital EMIs are generally prohibited without specific exemptions and require enhanced AML/KYC oversight.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a comprehensive Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to EU; however, General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), transfer pricing rules, and beneficial ownership requirements apply to prevent artificial arrangements

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under Income Tax Act, fines up to $25,000 per count, imprisonment up to 2 years, plus restitution of evaded tax

Director personal liability: Directors and partners can face personal liability for unpaid taxes, civil penalties, and in cases of gross negligence, criminal charges
Failure to File or Late Filing

Statutory sanction: Penalty of $50 per month of non-filing up to $1,000; $1,000 per day for failure to provide information; 50% gross negligence penalty

Director personal liability: Personal liability for penalties, potential director disqualification in severe cases
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of income, penalties up to 10% of transaction value, interest charges

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if willful
AML and Beneficial Ownership Violations

Statutory sanction: Fines under Proceeds of Crime Act or Income Tax Act, up to $100,000+; potential criminal charges

Director personal liability: Imprisonment for willful violations, travel bans in some cases, civil restitution
