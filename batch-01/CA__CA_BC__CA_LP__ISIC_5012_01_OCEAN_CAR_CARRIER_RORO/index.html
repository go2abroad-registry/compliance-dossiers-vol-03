Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes. Subject to the specific facts, partnership income and losses are computed at the partnership level and allocated to the partners under the partnership agreement and applicable Canadian tax rules. The partnership itself generally does not pay ordinary Canadian income tax as a separate taxable corporation, but it may have filing, information-reporting, withholding, GST/HST, customs, payroll, and provincial obligations. A non-resident partner may be taxable in Canada on its allocated share of Canadian-source business income and may be subject to withholding under applicable rules. A limited partnership can also be treated differently for foreign tax, treaty, anti-hybrid, or entity-classification purposes.

Available election forms

Canada has no general U.S.-style check-the-box election for classifying a Canadian limited partnership as a corporation or disregarded entity. Classification follows Canadian legal form and Canadian tax principles, subject to applicable anti-hybrid rules. Relevant mechanisms may include a section 97(2) tax-deferred rollover for qualifying property contributed to a partnership, section 85 elections for qualifying transfers to a taxable Canadian corporation rather than to a partnership, elections concerning partnership fiscal periods and specified partnership reporting matters, and treaty-based or statutory elections where available. A partnership agreement may allocate income, losses, and distributions, but allocations must comply with Canadian partnership and tax law and cannot independently convert the partnership into a corporate taxpayer. Foreign partners must separately assess foreign entity-classification elections and hybrid-mismatch consequences in their residence jurisdictions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, generally measured over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters, is the principal small-supplier threshold for most commercial persons, including partnerships. A partnership may register voluntarily before exceeding the threshold. Registration is generally required from the effective date prescribed by the Excise Tax Act once the threshold is exceeded. Special rules can apply to non-residents, charities, public institutions, associated persons, and persons making supplies through specified digital-economy regimes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For GST/HST purposes, a supply of goods exported from Canada may be zero-rated where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or shipment outside Canada and documentary evidence establishing export. International freight transportation services can be zero-rated where they meet the Excise Tax Act requirements, including qualifying transportation of goods from a place in Canada to a place outside Canada, or other prescribed international freight circumstances. Services supplied in respect of qualifying commercial vessels, cargo, or international transportation may qualify under specific Schedule VI provisions; the precise treatment depends on the contractual service, customer, route, place of supply, and whether the service relates to qualifying exported goods or a qualifying vessel. Offshore services are not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer is non-resident or payment is received from abroad. The supplier must test the applicable zero-rating provision and retain objective evidence. Input tax credits or GST/HST refunds require valid tax documentation, commercial records, proof that inputs were acquired for commercial activities, and compliance with filing and documentary requirements. BC PST treatment is separate and may provide exemptions for qualifying exports or international-use property, but no general GST/HST conclusion automatically applies to PST.

Canada does not generally impose a nationwide real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance, electronic tax-stamping, or mandatory B2B e-invoicing regime for ordinary commercial invoices. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices or other prescribed documentation containing sufficient information to support the tax charged and input tax credit claims, retain books and records, and comply with CRA electronic-record standards. Electronic invoices and records are generally acceptable if they remain accessible, reliable, readable, and capable of audit. GST/HST returns may be filed electronically through CRA systems where required or elected. Customs declarations are filed electronically through CBSA systems, including the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) and the CARM Client Portal for applicable import processes. There is no general requirement for CRA pre-clearance or digital GST/HST stamping of each invoice. Sector-specific procurement or contractual e-invoicing requirements may nevertheless apply.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days, and no universal Canadian inward-remittance certificate requirement for GST/HST zero-rating. CRA may audit the transaction, deny zero-rating, assess GST/HST that should have been charged, deny related input tax credits or refunds, and assess interest from the relevant due date. A supplier that incorrectly treats a taxable supply as zero-rated may become liable for the uncollected tax, subject to statutory relief and contractual recovery limitations. Gross-negligence penalties can apply where an amount is misstated or a claim is made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; false statements, evasion, obstruction, or destruction of records may trigger additional civil or criminal consequences under the Excise Tax Act and Income Tax Act. Customs non-compliance may result in monetary penalties under the CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System, seizure or forfeiture, reassessment of duties and taxes, suspension of customs privileges, and prosecution in serious cases. Records generally must be retained for at least six years after the end of the relevant year, subject to extended periods for objections, appeals, reassessments, or specific statutory requirements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for legitimate commercial and investment transactions. Canada does not generally impose exchange controls, capital controls, mandatory repatriation rules, or restrictions on the conversion of foreign currency into Canadian dollars. Capital contributions, shareholder or partner loans, intercompany funding, dividends, partnership distributions, debt service, freight receipts, vessel-related payments, and other ordinary commercial remittances may generally be made through Canadian financial institutions, subject to contractual bank controls, sanctions screening, anti-money-laundering requirements, tax withholding, transfer-pricing rules, partnership allocation rules, and proof of the underlying transaction. A Canadian limited partnership should document its partnership agreement, general-partner authority, ultimate beneficial ownership, source of funds, tax classification, related-party funding terms, and commercial purpose before receiving or remitting material amounts. Payments to non-residents may trigger Canadian withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, including potential withholding on interest, dividends, royalties, management fees, rents, and certain other amounts; treaty relief generally requires applicable residence and beneficial-ownership documentation. Payments connected with vessels, cargo, ports, customs, sanctions-sensitive jurisdictions, restricted parties, dual-use goods, or high-risk counterparties may receive enhanced review.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory requirement for exporters to surrender export proceeds to a bank, convert a prescribed percentage of export receipts into Canadian dollars, or repatriate export proceeds within a prescribed period. Ocean-carrier receipts may therefore be retained in Canadian or foreign-currency accounts, subject to contractual treasury policy, accounting controls, tax reporting, sanctions compliance, and bank transaction monitoring. Canadian financial institutions must comply with applicable FINTRAC obligations, including electronic funds transfer reporting and suspicious transaction reporting requirements. A business should retain bills of lading, charter-party or carriage contracts, invoices, customs records, freight statements, proof of delivery, settlement statements, and foreign-exchange confirmations. Canadian tax, customs, securities, maritime, and sanctions laws may impose separate reporting or withholding obligations even though no general export-proceeds surrender rule applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, with relevant federal oversight and enforcement interfaces involving the Department of Finance Canada, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Canada Border Services Agency, the Canada Revenue Agency, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, and Global Affairs Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for ancillary treasury functions and low to medium for the principal operating account. A foreign electronic-money institution or payment institution may support collection, foreign-exchange conversion, virtual accounts, and international transfers only where its licensing perimeter, Canadian access model, correspondent-bank arrangements, sanctions controls, and client-money safeguarding structure permit those services. An offshore EMI does not automatically provide a Canadian deposit account, Canadian payment-system access, Canadian deposit insurance, or authority to conduct regulated deposit-taking or payment activities in Canada. The limited partnership should retain a Canadian chartered bank as the primary account provider for capital receipts, payroll, taxes, vessel expenses, customer collections, and regulated trade settlements. Fintech use should be restricted by documented limits, dual approval, beneficiary controls, reconciliation requirements, and a contingency banking arrangement.

Route capital contributions and partner or shareholder loans from an account held in the documented name of the investor, partner, lender, or group treasury entity through a regulated bank using SWIFT or an equivalent bank wire. Use a consistent payment narrative such as "capital contribution under limited partnership agreement" or "intercompany loan under agreement dated [date]" and include the Canadian legal name, account number, general partner reference, and internal transaction identifier. Canada does not use a single universal statutory purpose-code regime for ordinary commercial inbound wires; the sending bank, correspondent bank, and receiving bank may nevertheless require a payment-purpose code or narrative. Maintain the executed partnership agreement, contribution or loan instrument, source-of-funds evidence, ownership records, foreign-exchange confirmation, and accounting entry. Reconcile the received amount to the capital or loan ledger, verify sender-name consistency, screen all parties and banks against applicable sanctions lists, and avoid third-party funding unless pre-cleared. The receiving bank may request evidence of beneficial ownership, commercial purpose, expected use of funds, tax treatment, and the relationship between the remitter and the limited partnership.Use the Canadian operating account for freight, bunker, port, terminal, agency, ship-management, insurance, customs, payroll, tax, and supplier payments. Standard bank wires may be processed using SWIFT customer-credit-transfer messaging, including MT103 or the applicable successor message under the bank's ISO 20022 migration framework. For high-value or higher-risk trade settlements, use a documentary commercial letter of credit, standby letter of credit, documentary collection, or bank-guaranteed payment where commercially appropriate. Match each payment to an approved purchase order, charter-party, service agreement, invoice, bill of lading, port statement, customs record, or insurance document. Require dual approval, sanctions screening, beneficiary-account verification, segregation of duties, and payment-release evidence. Canadian banks may delay or reject payments involving sanctioned persons, opaque intermediaries, restricted cargo, unusual routing, or insufficient supporting documentation. The entity should maintain a payment calendar for vessel calls, port charges, crew and payroll obligations, taxes, debt service, and customer refund exposure, together with minimum operating liquidity thresholds.Deliverable foreign-exchange forwards with a Canadian bank or appropriately regulated derivatives dealer, used to hedge forecast or contracted foreign-currency receipts and payments and supported by documented hedging limits, tenor controls, counterparty approvals, and cash-flow forecasts.Non-deliverable forwards where the relevant currency, jurisdiction, counterparty, and transaction are legally eligible and the dealer is authorized to provide the product. NDF settlement, documentation, collateral, and reporting must comply with applicable Canadian derivatives rules and the dealer's onboarding requirements.Foreign-exchange swaps for short-term liquidity management or currency conversion, subject to counterparty credit limits, documented commercial purpose, collateral terms, and applicable derivatives reporting and clearing requirements.Cross-currency swaps for longer-term debt or committed funding exposures, subject to formal risk approval, valuation controls, collateral and close-out provisions, accounting treatment, and applicable provincial derivatives regulation.Currency options, collars, and other structured hedges may be used only after confirming suitability, fair-value, disclosure, accounting, and derivatives-compliance requirements. The partnership should prohibit speculative trading and require every hedge to be linked to an identified exposure.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for ancillary treasury functions and low to medium for the principal operating account. A foreign electronic-money institution or payment institution may support collection, foreign-exchange conversion, virtual accounts, and international transfers only where its licensing perimeter, Canadian access model, correspondent-bank arrangements, sanctions controls, and client-money safeguarding structure permit those services. An offshore EMI does not automatically provide a Canadian deposit account, Canadian payment-system access, Canadian deposit insurance, or authority to conduct regulated deposit-taking or payment activities in Canada. The limited partnership should retain a Canadian chartered bank as the primary account provider for capital receipts, payroll, taxes, vessel expenses, customer collections, and regulated trade settlements. Fintech use should be restricted by documented limits, dual approval, beneficiary controls, reconciliation requirements, and a contingency banking arrangement.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (Federal 15% general corporate rate plus British Columbia 12% general corporate rate) for corporate partners; the Limited Partnership itself is fiscally transparent and not a taxpayer

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, applicable to dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to non-residents; however, distributions from a British Columbia limited partnership to its partners are not statutory dividends and are not subject to Part XIII WHT (except recharacterized payments).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general statutory dollar threshold for contemporaneous documentation; however, the CRA applies an administrative relief if aggregate cross-border related-party transactions are at or under CAD $1,000,000. Local File and Master File documentation must be available on request for any transaction with a non-arm's length non-resident. Country-by-Country reporting is mandatory for Canadian ultimate parent groups with consolidated group revenue of CAD $750,000,000 or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not codify Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as seen in offshore common-law jurisdictions. Instead, economic substance is enforced through (i) the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245 ITA), which disregards transactions lacking bona fide commercial purpose, and (ii) s.247 transfer pricing recharacterization. The MLI's principal-purpose test and the Canada-US treaty limitation-on-benefits clause require real activity and business substance for treaty access. A BC limited partnership with no substantive shipping operations in BC may lose treaty or provincial income tax benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under s.239(1) ITA, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; s.163(2) imposes a civil gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the under-reported tax; the CRA may also assess a 10% repeated-filing penalty.

Director personal liability: The directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unremitted amounts under s.227.1(1) ITA; a criminal conviction may lead to disgorgement of profits, travel banishment or denial of bail, seizure of the vessel under the Shipping Act, and extradition pursuant to the Canada-US Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty.
Shell Company and GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR applies to deny the benefit of the treaty or provincial rate, with an interest-charged re-assessment and a 25% penalty under s.245(3) ITA; the BC Information Act provides for administrative de-registration of the LP; treaty relief may be withdrawn under the MLI principal-purpose test.

Director personal liability: The CRA may pierce the corporate veil where the limited partnerships are a sham; directors and general corporate partners face civil restitution for unpaid taxes, anti-avoidance penalties, injunctive liquidation and bans on serving as a director under the Canada Business Corporations Act.
Money Laundering and AML Deficiencies

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties up to CAD $1,000,000 for entities and CAD $500,000 for individuals; Criminal Code s.462.31 imposes imprisonment up to 10 years for laundering proceeds; vessels and cargo may be forfeited as offence-related property.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partner officers may be subject to fines and imprisonment; asset seizures and travel restrictions are frequently imposed; directors can be convicted for causing the corporation to commit AML offences.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalization Misstatements

Statutory sanction: A s.247(4) penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment applies; interest under Part I accrues; thin-capitalization rules under s.18(4) deny excessive interest deductions with a 25% rate of tax on the disallowed amount for non-residents.

Director personal liability: Directors who participated in the decision to price intercompany services artificially may be held personally liable for the corporate penalty under s.227.1 ITA when the corporation has not remitted amounts withheld; CRA may seize the GP's assets and obtain a court order preventing the GP from leaving Canada.
