Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — Business Number (BN) and GST/HST program account (RT) via My Business Account portal (canada.ca/my-cra-business-account); customs administration by Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal and eManifest/ACI systems; British Columbia Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC portal) for provincial sales tax (PST) registration and BC employer health tax (EHT); BC Registries and Online Services for the limited partnership statutory registration.

E-government filing portal

(1) Register the BC limited partnership under the Partnership Act (BC) via BC Registries/OneStop, obtaining the BC registration number and registered office in British Columbia. (2) Obtain the partnership's federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 or using CRA Online Registration; the LP is the legal registrant for GST/HST as a separate person for excise tax purposes, distinct from its partners. (3) Open the GST/HST RT program account; include the partnership's declared activity code aligned to NAICS 424420 / ISIC 4632. (4) Register with CRA/CBSA for the Import/Export (RM) program account and enroll in CARM to report customs transactions and duties/GST on import. (5) Register with the BC Ministry of Finance for PST (eTaxBC) if selling taxable goods or services within BC (food wholesaling generally PST-exempt; PST registration may nevertheless be required for non-exempt ancillary products and equipment). (6) Register for BC EHT if total BC remuneration exceeds CAD 500,000 annually (rate 2.925% above threshold). (7) Obtain the Safe Food for Canadians Act licence from the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) for the import of frozen meat and fish — a statutory licence prerequisite to lawful commercial importation. (8) File T5013 partnership information returns via the general partner where the partnership has a corporate partner or more than five partners; no partnership-level income tax account is required because the LP is flow-through for income tax.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; the BC limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes under Income Tax Act (ITA) s.96; profit and loss are computed at partnership level and allocated to partners in accordance with the partnership agreement; the partnership itself is not subject to Part I tax. Territorial scope: the LP is a Canadian partnership where its central management and business operations are conducted in Canada; non-resident partners are deemed to carry on business in Canada through the LP and are taxable under Part I on their share of Canadian-source income (subject to any applicable tax treaty). GST/HST treatment is entity-level: the partnership is a separate person for excise tax purposes and must account for GST/HST independently. Tax-exempt zones: no meaningful income tax exemption zone applies in BC; customs duty deferral programs (Duty Deferral, Export Distribution Centre) are available but do not confer income tax exemption. Tax classification default: wholly taxable Canadian commercial entity; no statutory minimum capital requirement for the LP.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a U.S.-style 'check-the-box' regime. For a BC limited partnership, pass-through classification is statutory and not elective; the LP cannot elect to be taxed as a corporation. CRA administrative policy permits certain fiscally transparent foreign entities (e.g., a U.S. LLC) to elect deemed corporate status for Canadian tax purposes where the entity is a 'foreign entity' under CRA interpretation bulletins and the Canada-US Treaty; such an election affects the LLC, not the LP. Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss.18.4-18.6, in force for payments on/near 2021-2023 implementation) may disallow deductions where payments involve a hybrid instrument or hybrid payor arising from the LP's tax-transparent status or a partner's foreign jurisdiction classification, and a documented election or designation may be required for certain financial arrangements. GST/HST elections available to the LP include the election under the Excise Tax Act permitting a general partner to bind the partnership for GST/HST registration and filings; no election permits the LP to be disregarded as a GST/HST registrant.

Standard VAT / GST rate

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A registrant may zero-rate tangible personal property supplied by way of sale for export under Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where (i) the supply is made by a GST/HST registrant, (ii) the goods are sold for export and are exported by the registrant — or, for non-resident buyers, by the purchaser — as part of the supply within the time and manner contemplated by the commercial agreement, (iii) possession and risk of loss pass to the foreign buyer in Canada or abroad as contractually agreed, and (iv) the registrant holds sufficient evidence of export in accordance with CRA Guide RC4022. For the LP's frozen beef and seafood import/re-export model, basic groceries exported in their original condition also qualify for zero-rating; the zero-rated status of an export does not depend on the exporter holding a specific licence, but the CFIA export certification must accompany regulated meat or fish shipments. Export zero-rating permits the registrant to claim input tax credits (ITCs) for GST paid on overheads and any non-zero-rated inputs, generating a GST refund where ITCs exceed tax collected on exempt or taxable domestic supplies.

  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List (full description of goods, HS classification, quantities, weight, terms of sale per Incoterms)
  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or equivalent ACI eManifest export report) authenticated by the CBSA export report number and date of export
  • Bill of Lading / Sea Waybill or Airway Bill evidencing the carrier's receipt of goods, export destination port, and date of shipment
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or bank statement evidencing receipt of foreign currency payment from the overseas buyer, matching the exported shipment value

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance mandate, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping for domestic B2B invoicing. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and has been facilitated through the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard (Peppol network) adopted by federal and provincial procurement programs. Mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns applies to registrants with annual gross taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1,000,000; all GST/HST registrants must retain invoices and books for six years in readable electronic or paper format, with each invoice showing the supplier's BN, date, vendor/buyer identity, description, and tax charged. For customs, CBSA mandates electronic transmission of cargo reports, import documentary declarations, and export documentation through eManifest and CARM, including real-time or near-real-time transmission of customs accounting declarations; AMPS penalties apply for paper-based or non-compliant filings.

Canada does not prescribe a statutory 180-day repatriation deadline for export proceeds; in practice the 180-day window is adopted as the prudent compliance benchmark for CRA audits of intercompany trade balances, transfer pricing under ITA s.247, and banking AML reviews. Under-transfer or delayed repatriation of export proceeds beyond 180 days may be recharacterized as a shareholder benefit or an interest-free loan and trigger income inclusion or withholding assessments. CRA audit reassessment window for GST/HST is generally four years from the filing date (eta s.298), extended indefinitely where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established. Where an exporter zero-rates a supply without documented export proof, CRA may reassess the supply at 5% GST, disallow ITCs, add arrears interest, and impose a gross negligence penalty of 25% of the tax that would have been evaded under ETA s.285(1) (minimum CAD 100). Fraudulent export zero-rating is criminal tax evasion under ETA s.327(1), punishable on indictment by a fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax, imprisonment up to five years, or both; summary conviction penalties of up to CAD 100,000 or two years may apply. CBSA administrative monetary penalty system (AMPS) separately penalizes false or omitted export reporting (up to CAD 25,000 per violation) and may seize goods for customs misrepresentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a fully convertible floating currency under IMF Article VIII. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are free from exchange-control restrictions; there is no requirement for Bank of Canada authorization for inbound or outbound wire transfers, portfolio investment, or direct investment. Profit remittance by a non-resident partner of a British Columbia limited partnership is not subject to exchange-control hurdles, but it is subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada): the statutory rate is 25%, generally reduced to 15% under the Canada-US tax treaty or another applicable DTA, and the levy attaches to amounts paid or credited to the non-resident partner as partnership income, not to gross revenue. A T106 partnership information return, NR6 waiver election, or NR4 remittance filing must be completed before any distribution is wired abroad.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender obligation, no mandatory repatriation timeline, and no forced conversion ratio for export receipts or any foreign-currency proceeds. Exporters and importers may hold USD, EUR, or other foreign currency in domiciliary accounts indefinitely. The only compliance duties are: (i) FINTRAC reporting for electronic fund transfers of CAD 10,000 or more; (ii) Canadian income inclusion of foreign-exchange gains and business income; and (iii) standard commercial invoicing and CFIA documentation for regulated frozen beef and seafood goods. For this import-oriented BC LP, no export surrender obligations arise.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulation is administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering supervision is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada abolished its Foreign Exchange Control Regulations in 1951, and no central-bank approval is required for any cross-border capital movement.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM with binding legal constraints. Canada has no UK/EU-style Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence regime; therefore a foreign EMI cannot take deposits, issue Canadian clearing numbers, or operate as a Bank Act Schedule I bank. Digital cross-border payment providers (e.g., Wise Platform, Payoneer, Ebury, Corpay) may operate only as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses (MSBs) and may execute FX and cross-border payments, but they cannot serve as the primary operating or settlement bank. Such platforms are viable strictly as secondary disbursement or vendor-payment rails, and only after the core treasury account at a Tier 1 Schedule I bank is fully activated and the MSB's FINTRAC registration is verified.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound partner contributions must arrive via SWIFT MT103 from the contributing partner's own same-name account at a qualifying financial institution, with beneficiary being the BC LP's operational account at a Tier 1 Schedule I Canadian bank. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A British Columbia limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to Canadian corporate income tax. Taxable income is allocated to the partners. If a taxable Canadian corporation carries on the business instead, the combined federal and British Columbia general corporate rate is generally 27%: 15% federal plus 12% British Columbia. The combined small-business rate is generally 11% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations on eligible active business income within the applicable limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic withholding tax rate on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident payer to a non-resident is generally 25%, subject to reduction under an applicable income tax treaty. A Canadian limited partnership generally does not pay dividends; distributions are analyzed as partnership allocations, return of capital, interest, royalties, service payments or other amounts. A corporation distributing after-tax profits may create dividend withholding obligations.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 partnership information return is generally due within five months after the partnership fiscal period-end. Information slips are generally due by the last day of the third month after the partnership year-end. GST/HST returns are due according to the assigned reporting period; the balance is generally due on the filing deadline. A partnership fiscal period generally cannot exceed 12 months and is subject to Canadian tax rules.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is generally due six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end, or three months for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting statutory conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian transfer-pricing documentation is governed principally by Income Tax Act section 247 and is not subject to one universal transaction-value threshold for maintaining contemporaneous documentation. A T106 filing generally applies where the relevant non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million in aggregate, subject to statutory rules and exclusions. The transfer-pricing penalty exception commonly uses a CAD 5 million threshold for the total value of non-arm's-length transactions in the year, but this does not eliminate the arm's-length requirement or the need for defensible records. Canada generally requires a Local File-style contemporaneous analysis but does not impose a general domestic Master File filing requirement; Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to multinational enterprise groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime titled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore regimes. Canadian tax outcomes are tested through the arm's-length principle, beneficial-ownership rules, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, sham-transaction principles, corporate-residence rules, and factual commercial substance. A Canadian LP should maintain real decision-making, books and records, commercial contracts, inventory and logistics evidence, appropriate personnel or outsourced operational capability, banking controls and risk allocation consistent with the business. Circular cash flows, accommodation parties, nominee arrangements and profit allocations unsupported by functions, assets and risks are high-risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment, tax, arrears interest and gross-negligence penalties may apply. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act; GST/HST offences may carry separate fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners and persons directing the conduct may face prosecution, fines and imprisonment. Directors may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where statutory due-diligence defences fail. Corporate separateness is not a defence to personal criminal conduct; Canadian law does not impose an automatic general travel ban, but court orders, bail conditions, arrest processes and immigration consequences may restrict travel.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, apply withholding tax and charge interest. Repeated or deliberate misrepresentation may create gross-negligence or criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face civil claims, derivative claims, director-liability assessments for statutory remittances and criminal liability where they knowingly participate in false records or evasion. Personal liability is not created solely by an adverse transfer-pricing adjustment.
Customs, Import Valuation and Food-Safety Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include reassessed customs duties and GST, interest, administrative monetary penalties, seizure, detention, refusal of release, product recall, licence suspension or cancellation, prosecution and statutory fines or imprisonment under customs and food-safety legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers and responsible individuals may be prosecuted where legislation creates personal offences or where they authorized, acquiesced in or participated in the conduct. Civil restitution, recall costs and customer claims may attach to the entity and culpable individuals; travel restrictions are not automatic.
AML, Sanctions and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, reporting restrictions, seizure and forfeiture, and prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, Criminal Code and sanctions legislation may apply. Criminal proceeds-of-crime or sanctions offences can result in substantial fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly participate, direct or facilitate conduct may be prosecuted, fined, imprisoned and subject to forfeiture. Directors can face regulatory and civil consequences for inadequate controls, but personal liability depends on the applicable statute and evidence of authorization, participation or negligence.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, recharacterize income, deny deductions, impose withholding tax, assess penalties and reassess prior years. Corporate records, beneficial-ownership reporting and anti-money-laundering failures may create separate offences.

Director personal liability: General partners, directors of corporate partners and controlling persons may face civil claims, statutory remittance liability, fines and imprisonment for fraud, obstruction or knowingly false filings. Piercing the corporate veil is fact-specific and is not automatic merely because an entity has limited substance.
Unremitted Payroll, Withholding and GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Assessments, trust penalties, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, collection action and prosecution may apply. Directors may be personally assessed for certain unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under Canadian statutory director-liability provisions.

Director personal liability: Personal assessments may include the unremitted amount, penalties and interest, subject to statutory limitation periods and due-diligence defences. Criminally culpable officers or general partners may face fines and imprisonment. Civil restitution may be ordered where proceeds or trust funds were misappropriated.
Obstruction and Destruction of Records

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-keep-records penalties, compliance orders, adverse reassessment assumptions, contempt or obstruction charges, fines and imprisonment may apply depending on the authority and statute involved.

Director personal liability: Individuals who destroy records or obstruct officials may be prosecuted personally and may face fines or imprisonment. The entity may also lose evidentiary defences and suffer reassessment, seizure, licence and collection consequences.
