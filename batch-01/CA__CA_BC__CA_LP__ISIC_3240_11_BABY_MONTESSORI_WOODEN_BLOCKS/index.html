Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – federal tax administration for income tax, GST/HST, and business number registration. Portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses.html); British Columbia provincial indirect tax compliance (e.g., PST) is administered by the BC Ministry of Finance through eTaxBC (https://etax.gov.bc.ca).

E-government filing portal

For a British Columbia limited partnership with foreign owners, file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) with the CRA by mail or register online through the CRA Business Registration Online service. Provide the legal name and BC registry number of the partnership, the registered office and principal establishment addresses, the name/address of every general and limited partner (including non-resident partners), the primary business activity (NAICS 339930 / ISIC 3240 - manufacture of wooden toy blocks), the start date of commercial activity, the estimated annual worldwide taxable supply revenue, and confirmation that exports of tangible personal property will be zero-rated. Upon approval, the CRA issues a nine-digit Business Number; the GST/HST account is identified by the Business Number with the program account suffix 'RT0001' for the partnership. A non-resident limited partnership that does not carry on business in Canada is not generally required to register for GST/HST solely because it exports goods, but registration is mandatory when the taxable supply threshold is met or when a permanent establishment/canvassing activity exists in Canada; voluntary registration may still be elected to claim input tax credits on Canadian expenses.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No U.S.-style federal check-the-box election exists under Canadian law; a British Columbia limited partnership is fiscally transparent by default under the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, a partnership may elect to designate a participating partner as the representative partner responsible for collecting and remitting tax, and may file an election under section 273 of the Excise Tax Act to streamline partnership acquisitions and recoveries. A non-resident person may also elect under section 240(4) of the Excise Tax Act to voluntarily register for GST/HST. For hybrid mismatch exposure, Canadian hybrid rules (Income Tax Act sections 18.2 and 18.3) require the LP to maintain contemporaneous documentation and, where a mismatch arises, to file the prescribed form/return to align the deduction treatment between CRA and the foreign partner's home jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 - a person ceases to be a small supplier and must register for GST/HST once total annual worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated) from the preceding four calendar quarters exceed C$30,000, or once taxable supplies made in the current quarter exceed C$30,000. A non-resident that solicits orders in Canada or operates a permanent establishment in Canada must register for GST/HST regardless of the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of the baby Montessori wooden blocks qualifies for zero-rated GST/HST treatment under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the goods are sold to a non-resident purchaser and the vendor either exports the goods directly outside Canada as part of the supply or delivers them to a carrier for direct export. The vendor must retain contemporaneous documentary evidence that the goods were exported, including carrier waybills, customs export declarations, and the purchaser's foreign destination evidence. If the goods are subsequently diverted for domestic Canadian use or consumption, zero-rating is inapplicable and GST/HST plus, where relevant, BC PST must be self-assessed. Services incidental to the export (e.g., freight arrangements) are separately reviewed under the place-of-supply rules.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form A45/A48 or CAED export declaration where required; for US-bound shipments, the carrier may rely on AES/ACI e-manifest transmission)
  • Bill of Lading (carrier-issued proof of outbound shipment showing Canadian origin and foreign destination)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank or financial institution statement evidencing receipt of export proceeds in the partnership's account, or equivalent payment confirmation)

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, pre-clearance API stamping, or mandatory digital stamping for GST/HST invoices. Taxpayers may issue electronic invoices (PDF, EDI, XML, or secure portal) provided the invoice contains the prescribed information under section 284.01 of the Excise Tax Act. CRA's Business Gateway API supports filing and remittance transmissions, but it does not pre-clear invoices. In British Columbia, eTaxBC supports PST account management and filing; neither the CRA nor BC requires digital fiscal stamping for export invoices. Mandate: retain all source invoices, customs documents, carrier records, and payment receipts in readable electronic format and produce them to CRA or BC Ministry of Finance upon audit demand.

CRA may deny the zero-rating and assess unrecovered GST/HST at 5% plus statutory interest if export documentation is incomplete, contradictory, or fabricated. For false statements or fraudulent claims, CRA may impose penalties under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act (including 50% gross-negligence penalties in aggravated cases) and may reassess beyond the normal four-year limitation period in cases of misrepresentation or fraud. Penalties may also apply under sections 280-283 of the Excise Tax Act for failure to register, file, or remit, and under Income Tax Act section 163.2 for false statements supplied to a tax preparer. Provincial reassessment exposure for BC PST arises only if goods are withdrawn from export status or deemed sold, rented, or consumed in British Columbia; in such cases BC may assess PST at 7%, plus penalty and interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating CAD with fully liberalized capital and current accounts under IMF Article VIII. No exchange control legislation exists; the repatriation of profits, payment of partnership distributions to non-resident limited partners, and remittance of royalties are unrestricted. Withholding tax (Part XIII) applies to certain outbound payments but does not restrict convertibility. Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) requires reporting of foreign-source funds and Form T1134/T1135 only for specified thresholds, not as a convertibility control.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement and no mandated conversion ratio for export proceeds. Exporters may hold receivables in foreign currency accounts at any Schedule I bank or authorized foreign currency bank account in Canada, and may settle trade invoices in CAD or foreign currency at commercial discretion. Foreign exchange settlement is executed at the prevailing market rate through banks or registered MSBs. Exporters must only comply with general CRA tax reporting and FINTRAC large cash transaction reporting (over CAD 10,000).

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) with OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) as prudential banking regulator; FINTRAC administers the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium as a complementary settlement rail; not viable as a primary banking substitute. Global digital EMIs (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury) are permitted in Canada only as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses (MSBs) under PCMLTFA, not as banks. They do not offer CDIC deposit insurance and cannot hold escrowed partnership capital or provide Canadian-dollar clearing through Lynx/Real-Time Rail unless backed by a Schedule I partner bank. Legal constraints include mandatory MSB registration, Canadian-entity presence, and prohibition on accepting deposits in the course of business without a bank license. EMIs are acceptable for disbursing seller payouts from Amazon Pay/Stripe but must be downstream of a Schedule I primary account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (combined federal 15% plus British Columbia provincial 12% for general corporate income; a Canadian-controlled private corporation claiming the small business deduction pays a reduced rate of 11% on active business income up to the federal/BC business limit)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

For a limited partnership, profit distributions to partners take the character of partnership allocations and are not subject to Canadian dividend withholding tax at the partnership level. When the corporate partner later distributes a dividend from its after-tax retained earnings, the statutory Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid to a non-resident is 25% under Income Tax Act section 212, subject to treaty reduction (typically 5% for substantial shareholdings and 15% for portfolio dividends under the Canada-US treaty, and 10% for portfolio dividends and 5% for significant shareholdings under many other Canadian tax treaties including Canada-China).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian transfer-pricing rules under section 247 of the Income Tax Act and CRA Information Circular 87-2R apply to transactions between a Canadian taxpayer (including a limited partnership and its partners) and a non-resident related person. A taxpayer must prepare and retain contemporaneous documentation (functionally a Local File and Master File) where the aggregate value of transfers of tangible goods, intangible property, services, or interest with non-resident related parties exceeds C$1,000,000 in the taxation year, or where the aggregate loans outstanding exceed C$1,000,000 at any time during the year. For smaller taxpayers, the threshold is C$1,000,000 for all covered transactions combined; the CRA may request the documentation under section 247(4) regardless of magnitude where a transfer-pricing risk is identified.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the offshore Economic Substance Regulations applicable to the UAE or similar regimes, but the CRA may apply the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act to disregard transactions lacking a bona fide business purpose. For a BC limited partnership manufacturing Montessori wooden toys for export, the partnership must demonstrate real economic substance through a BC permanent establishment, active business operations, payroll, premises, and risk-bearing functions. A corporate partner using a treaty jurisdiction or a low-tax jurisdiction without substantial commercial activity may be denied treaty benefits under treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions or be treated as having a permanent establishment in Canada. For purposes of the Canada-US treaty, a US limited partner who provides only capital is generally not deemed to have a permanent establishment, but if the BC general partner or a related agent habitually concludes contracts in Canada, the US partner may be exposed to Canadian taxation on the partnership profits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act sections 238 and 239, false statements or evasion are punishable on indictment by imprisonment for up to two years (or five years for fraud-related evasion) and fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded, plus civil reassessment penalties under section 163(2) of up to 50% of the understated tax and gross-negligence penalties of 50%; a conviction may result in a prohibition from serving as a director under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the BC Business Corporations Act.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner or corporate limited partner can be held personally liable for unpaid corporate tax, GST/HST, source deductions, and other amounts under the Income Tax Act section 227.1, the Excise Tax Act section 323, and the BC Income Tax Act; civil restitution and administrative monetary penalties apply; the court may order a director to account for the partnership's withheld amounts, prohibit cross-border travel where criminal prosecutions are pending, and impose imprisonment for deliberate evasion. Directors cannot rely on the limited liability shield of the LP when they have signed or participated in a false return or directed the unlawful activity.
Shell Company and Sham Partnership

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under GAAR (section 245) to disregard the partnership's allocation, deny treaty benefits, impose transfer-pricing adjustments, and apply penalties under section 162(1) for failure to file information returns and under section 247(3) for failure to provide documentation; BC may revoke the limited partnership registration and impose penalties of up to C$5,000 for carrying on business without registration; where a company is used as a sham to misrepresent owners, the BC courts may pierce the corporate veil under common law and the Business Corporations Act (S.B.C. 2002, c.57) s.144, leaving the owners liable for all business debts and taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors and controlling partners who participate in the creation or use of a shell partnership may be subject to statutory liability for the tax amounts of the partnership under joint and several liability rules; civil penalties for negligent misrepresentation extend to personal assets; criminal prosecution for fraud (Criminal Code section 380) carries imprisonment up to 14 years and mandatory restitution orders; a director may face travel bans under court order and enforced collection against personal bank accounts and property.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and FINTRAC Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c.17), a non-compliance conviction carries fines up to C$500,000 for a first offence and C$1,000,000 for subsequent offences, and imprisonment up to six months for individuals; administrative monetary penalties can be imposed by FINTRAC up to C$1,000,000 for significant non-compliance; the courts may issue restraint and forfeiture orders over the partnership assets under the Criminal Code and the Civil Forfeiture Act (British Columbia) if the funds are alleged to be proceeds of unlawful activity.

Director personal liability: Officers, directors, and designated compliance officers are personally liable for penalties if they knowingly cause, authorize, permit, or acquiesce in the corporation's AML violation; the legislation allows FINTRAC to pursue personal findings, and the BC Civil Forfeiture Office may bring civil actions for restitution; individual officers face travel restrictions and imprisonment where money laundering charges are prosecuted, and professional or business-license revocation prevents them from operating in the toy export industry.
International Tax Non-Disclosure and Reportable Transactions

Statutory sanction: Late-filing T1135 penalties are C$25 per day up to C$2,500, with a strict failure case penalty of C$10,000 plus C$250 per month for continued non-compliance up to 24 months; T1134 penalties are C$100 per day up to C$2,500 plus C$500 per month up to C$10,000; failure to disclose a reportable transaction carries a penalty of C$100,000 for corporations and C$50,000 for individuals under section 237.3, and the general anti-avoidance rule may be applied; Canadian taxpayers who fail to report a transaction are precluded from claiming the associated deductions and credits.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly fail to file or who consent to the false filing of foreign-asset or reportable-transaction information returns may be prosecuted under the Income Tax Act as employees or agents, and the persons who directed the omission can be assessed personally; the court can order repayment of avoided tax, and a pattern of intentional non-disclosure may support charges of tax evasion under section 239(1) with imprisonment up to five years and mandatory restitution orders; the CRA may freeze bank accounts and obtain registered liens against the personal property of the directors.
Transfer-Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3), a documentary penalty of C$5,000 (or C$500 per month up to C$20,000) is imposed for failure to provide section 247(4) documentation within 90 days of a written demand; transfer-pricing reassessments apply the arm's-length price, and consequential adjustments are denied where documentation is not complete; interest and penalties apply on the tax deficiency and, under subsection 163(2), gross-negligence penalties of 50% apply if the failure was due to wilful blindness or misconduct.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approved inadequate documentation that enabled understated income can be assessed as persons who signed or participated in the false return; they face personal civil liability under the common-law duty of care in the BC Supreme Court, restitution of taxes, disgorgement of profits, and, in egregious schemes, penal prosecution under section 239 with imprisonment up to five years; CRA collection can include seizure of the director's personal assets and garnishment of personal bank accounts.
