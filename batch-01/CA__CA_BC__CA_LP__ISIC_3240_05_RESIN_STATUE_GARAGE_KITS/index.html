Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST, Corporate Income Tax, Payroll and Excise Tax administration; business registration via CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account (MyBA). Provincial sales tax (PST) is separately administered by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance's Consumer Taxation Branch (CTB).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity not resident in Canada must register for a CRA Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account before commencing taxable commercial activity in Canada. For a BC limited partnership, the partnership itself is a separate taxpayer for GST/HST and must register through CRA Business Registration Online or by completing Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts.' The partnership must provide its legal name, principal place of business in Canada, a Canadian contact address, and ownership details. A non-resident general partner may need to designate a representative under CRA's electronic services terms. After the BN is assigned, the partnership receives separate program accounts: GST/HST (RT), corporate income tax (RC), payroll (RP), and import/export (RM). Registration is mandatory once taxable worldwide revenues exceed the $30,000 small-supplier threshold or if the partnership imports goods, makes taxable supplies in Canada, or enters into elective registration. The partnership should also register with the BC Consumer Taxation Branch for PST collection, generally as a vendor if selling taxable goods through Canadian sales channels.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no full 'check-the-box' entity classification election. A limited partnership is statutorily treated as a flow-through for Canadian income tax purposes: income is computed at partnership level and allocated to partners under the partnership agreement, with tax payable by each partner. For GST/HST purposes, the partnership is a separate 'person' and must account for GST/HST on its own. A non-resident partner may make an optional filing election under subsection 216(1) of the Income Tax Act to file a Part I tax return on Canadian-source business income instead of being subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on distributions, provided the partnership income is Canadian-source business income. A partnership may also elect to be a 'qualifying subsidiary' for GST/HST group reporting purposes in limited conditions, and joint GST/HST election rules under the Excise Tax Act allow related partnerships to use simplified reporting. No hybrid mismatch election is available under domestic law; hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act and BEIS Action 2 are mandatory and disallow specific mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies over the trailing four calendar quarters; mandatory registration if exceeded unless the supplier is a small supplier. BC PST registration is required for vendors selling taxable goods or services in BC, with a similar CAD 30,000 registration threshold for out-of-province or foreign sellers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods delivered by the supplier to a purchaser who is a non-resident and exported directly by the supplier are zero-rated under section 21 of the Excise Tax Act, provided the supplier maintains documentary proof of export in accordance with CRA guides (GST/HST Memorandum 14-1 and current 'Proof of Export' requirements). Services that are performed wholly outside Canada or supplied to non-residents in respect of real property outside Canada may also be zero-rated under sections 23 and 24. For resin statue garage kits sold to US or foreign buyers, the supplier must show that legal ownership and physical possession of the goods transferred to a non-resident purchaser and that the goods were exported as part of the sale. The BC PST does not apply to exported goods, and the supplier should maintain evidence that delivery occurred outside British Columbia. Supply made by a non-resident supplier to a Canadian recipient is generally subject to GST/HST unless a specific zero-rating provision applies.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or Canadian export reporting via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) when export value exceeds CAD 2,000 or as otherwise required)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill showing consignee outside Canada
  • Commercial invoice with the purchaser's non-resident address and description of the resin statue kits
  • Packing list and, where applicable, carrier or freight-forwarder certificates of export
  • Evidence of payment and settlement (Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign bank remittance / credit card settlement record for prepayment deposits via Stripe or PayPal)
  • Any customs brokerage confirmation and proof of international shipment
  • GST/HST zero-rated export documentation retained per CRA six-year record retention rules

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing API transmission, or digital stamping for GST/HST. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA's GST/HST NETFILE, My Business Account, or approved EDI software. Tax invoices must meet Excise Tax Act section 169 and section 3 of the Electronic Filing and Reporting Requirements Regulations; electronic records and invoices must be retained in a readable format and made available to the CRA upon audit. BC PST uses a separate return-based filing system through the BC eTaxBC portal; no real-time digital clearance obligation exists. A foreign seller using payment rails such as Stripe and PayPal should verify that its processing documentation satisfies the evidentiary requirements for zero-rated export supplies.

The CRA may reassess GST/HST returns within the normal four-year limitation period, or longer in cases of misrepresentation or fraud. If export evidence is insufficient, the CRA will recharacterize sales as taxable domestic supplies and assess GST/HST at 5%, plus interest and penalties. The Excise Tax Act imposes penalties under section 280 (generally CAD 250 or 1% of unreported amounts, subject to a maximum) and penalties of up to 25% of the unreported amount for knowingly making false statements; gross negligence penalties under subsection 285(1) can be up to 50% of the tax understatement, and criminal prosecution may apply under subsections 285(2) and 286 for willful evasion. The BC PST has separate clawback provisions with penalty interest, and failure to remit collected PST may expose the partnership to director liability. Substantial non-compliance with export documentation requirements can result in denial of zero-rating, retroactive assessment of GST/HST and PST, and audit scrutiny of future refund claims.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is a fully free-floating and freely convertible currency. Canada maintains no exchange control gates, no prior approval for foreign direct investment (Investment Canada Act review is limited to national-security-sensitive sectors, not resin statue fabrication), and no restriction on profit remittance. Both current account and capital account transactions are freely permitted. The decisive frictions are tax-driven and AML-driven: non-resident limited partners of a Canadian-resident LP are taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act (ITA) on Canadian-source business income allocable through a permanent establishment, while certain distributions characterized as dividends, interest, rents, or royalties attract Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory 25% rate, generally treaty-reduced (e.g., 15% or 5% on dividends under the Canada–US and Canada–China treaties; 0% or 10% on interest/royalties depending on the treaty). Return-of-capital distributions are not taxable; the partnership's banking records must segregate capital contributions, accumulated taxed income, and withholding-tax components.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, mandatory conversion, or repatriation timeline on export proceeds; a resident limited partnership may retain 100% of foreign currency receivables in original currency. However, CRA requires books to be maintained in CAD under ITA s. 261 using Bank of Canada published exchange rates. FINTRAC requires financial institutions and MSBs to report every international electronic funds transfer (IEFT) of CAD 10,000 or more executed within a 24-hour window, and physical cross-border movement of currency or bearer negotiable instruments exceeding CAD 10,000 must be declared on Form E311.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) as monetary authority and sole issuer of Canadian dollar (CAD) banknotes; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as federal prudential regulator for Schedule I banks; Payments Canada as operator of Lynx, ACSS, and the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) real-time rail; FINTRAC as AML/ATF supervisor under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). There is no dedicated Canadian foreign exchange control bureau; FX transactions are governed by the Currency Act and generally applicable bank supervisory rules.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for collections/disbursements and Low as primary treasury carrier for a BC-resident LP. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., UK FCA-regulated EMI licenses) cannot passport into Canada; carrying on deposit business in Canada requires an order under s. 524/534 of the Bank Act and a federally authorized foreign bank branch or recognized MSB/PSP structure. Domestic and cross-border fintech providers (Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury) operate in Canada as FINTRAC-registered MSBs and, from the RPAA rollout window (2024-2026), must be registered with the Bank of Canada as payment service providers. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All partner capital must be remitted by SWIFT MT103 (CHIPS/Fedwire for USD from US partners) to the LP's operational account at the designated Schedule I bank, with Field 70 containing 'Capital Contribution - CA_LP - [partner name]' and no merchant invoice reference. Canada does not maintain a statutory wire purpose-code list; banks register a balance-of-payments narrative and the ISO 20022 code 'EQUI' (equity capital) or 'TRADE' is recommended. For USD wires from the US, the sender must include the recipient bank's ABA routing number and the LP account number; for wires from mainland China, routing through a Hong Kong or New York settlement point is standard. Canadian banks must file a FINTRAC IEFT report for every international transfer of CAD 10,000 or more in a 24-hour period; capital injections must never be split into tranches below CAD 10,000 to avoid structuring offences. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A British Columbia limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not subject to Canadian corporate income tax at the partnership level. Taxable income is allocated to partners. If income is earned through a Canadian-resident corporation, the general combined federal and British Columbia corporate rate is generally 27% (15% federal and 12% British Columbia). The combined small-business rate may be 11% (9% federal and 2% British Columbia) for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit; a limited partnership is not itself a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident is 25%. Under the Canada-U.S. treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying company holding at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other treaty cases. Other treaties commonly reduce the rate to 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and recipient status. A limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend and must be analyzed by underlying character, partner status, and applicable treaty provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Generally due five months after the end of the partnership's fiscal period. Each partner's income tax return follows the partner's own statutory deadline. A partnership with a fiscal period ending on December 31 will generally have a T5013 deadline of May 31 of the following year.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Due six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any corporate balance is generally payable two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting statutory conditions.

Deadline: · Frequency:

NR4 information returns are generally due by the last day of March following the calendar year. Part XIII withholding is generally remitted by the 15th day of the month following the month in which the amount was paid or credited.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no standalone statutory Local File or Master File threshold in the OECD terminology. Contemporaneous documentation is required for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. Form T106 reporting is generally triggered where the applicable aggregate non-arm's-length non-resident transaction threshold is met, commonly CAD 1,000,000 for the reporting period, subject to the current CRA form instructions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million. The CAD 1,000,000 threshold should not be treated as a safe harbour from the general arm's-length and documentation requirements.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a general standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the Canadian general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose or anti-abuse provisions, sham and simulation principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, transfer-pricing rules, and permanent-establishment analysis. The partnership should maintain real Canadian commercial activity, production capability, inventory controls, personnel or appropriately supervised contractors, decision-making records, premises or operational infrastructure, intellectual-property ownership evidence, customer contracts, and bank and accounting records. A Canadian entity that merely invoices, receives payments, or holds intellectual property without genuine functions, assets, and risks faces heightened reassessment and treaty-benefit risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false reporting

Statutory sanction: Canadian tax evasion under the Income Tax Act may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to five years, reassessment of tax, gross-negligence penalties, interest, and civil recovery proceedings. GST/HST fraud may generate separate penalties and criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: A director, general partner, officer, or controlling individual who authorized, assented to, acquiesced in, or participated in the conduct may face prosecution, fines, restitution, and imprisonment. Directors may also be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability rules. Travel restrictions may follow criminal process, bail conditions, enforcement warrants, or outstanding tax judgments; there is no automatic tax-evasion travel ban in every case.
Partnership misclassification and limited-liability failure

Statutory sanction: The general partner may have unlimited liability for partnership debts, taxes, employee claims, product claims, and regulatory liabilities. A purported limited partner may face loss of limited-liability protection under applicable British Columbia partnership law, depending on conduct and statutory facts.

Director personal liability: Limited partnerships do not have directors unless a corporate general partner is used. Individuals behind the general partner may be personally liable through direct participation, guarantees, fraudulent conduct, statutory assessments, or veil-piercing principles. A corporate general partner does not eliminate exposure for director source-deduction and GST/HST liabilities.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unremitted tax, daily-compounded interest, a 10% failure-to-withhold penalty, or a 20% penalty for knowing or grossly negligent failure. False treaty certifications may create additional penalties and reassessments.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for corporate source deductions and certain statutory remittances if the corporation failed to remit and the director did not exercise due diligence. Individuals who knowingly directed the failure may also face criminal prosecution.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny deductions, recharacterize transactions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, and assess interest. Corresponding-adjustment relief is not automatic.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable for a corporate transfer-pricing assessment solely because of office. Personal exposure can arise from knowing participation in fraud, false statements, obstruction, statutory remittance liability, or separate civil claims.
Shell company, treaty shopping, and beneficial-ownership abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the 25% domestic withholding rate, invoke the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions, reassess tax and interest, impose penalties, and refer deliberate conduct for prosecution.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly create false residence, beneficial-ownership, or limitation-on-benefits evidence may face tax prosecution, fines, imprisonment, civil restitution, asset preservation orders, and potential restrictions on international travel arising from criminal or enforcement proceedings.
GST/HST and marketplace non-compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess unremitted GST/HST, deny input tax credits, impose late-filing, gross-negligence, false-statement, and failure-to-remit penalties, and charge interest. Fraudulent conduct may be prosecuted.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted GST/HST in accordance with statutory director-liability rules. A general partner may be directly liable for partnership GST/HST obligations. Criminal participation can result in fines, imprisonment, restitution, and enforcement-related travel restrictions.
AML, sanctions, and suspicious payment activity

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC, sanctions, customs, and criminal-law consequences may include administrative monetary penalties, seizure or freezing of funds, transaction restrictions, prosecution, substantial fines, and imprisonment. The applicable result depends on the activity, amount, knowledge, and statute.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and responsible managers may face personal regulatory or criminal exposure where they directed, authorized, acquiesced in, or knowingly ignored the conduct. Courts may impose asset restraint, bail, reporting, or travel conditions.
Payroll, contractor, and worker misclassification

Statutory sanction: Reassessments may include income-tax source deductions, CPP and EI, interest, penalties, workers' compensation premiums, employment standards amounts, and possible criminal charges for intentional evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted payroll deductions under the Income Tax Act and for certain employment-related amounts under applicable legislation. The general partner may also be personally liable for partnership employment obligations.
Product, customs, and intellectual-property misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include customs reassessment, duties, penalties, seizure, import restrictions, civil damages, injunctions, statutory damages, and criminal prosecution for deliberate counterfeiting or customs fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face personal exposure for knowing infringement, fraudulent import declarations, or direct participation. Civil restitution and injunctive orders may restrict assets and business operations; travel restrictions arise only where connected to criminal process or specific court orders.
