Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - Business Number and GST/HST programs administered centrally via My Business Account portal and Business Registration Online (BRO). Provincial Sales Tax (PST) in British Columbia is administered by the BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch, via the eTaxBC portal. CRA portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html; BC PST portal: https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes/sales-taxes/pst.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Extra-provincially register the foreign Limited Partnership in British Columbia under the BC Partnership Act (Extraprovincial Limited Partnership Registration), obtaining a BC registration number and a Canadian registered office/service address. Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) and program accounts using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts), filed by mail, fax, or via Business Registration Online; the foreign LP must select program account suffix RT (GST/HST), RM (import/export for customs clearance), and RP (payroll, if employees). Step 3: The LP itself, as a separate statutory 'person' under the Excise Tax Act, registers for GST/HST under ETA s. 240(1), notwithstanding its income-tax transparency; voluntary registration under s. 240(2) is also available to claim input tax credits. Step 4: Register as a BC PST vendor with the BC Ministry of Finance through the BC Corporate Registry / OneStop BC or eTaxBC once taxable sales are made or intended in BC. Step 5: Activate the RM import/export account and, where applicable, post security under ETA s. 299. Non-resident applicants must provide evidence of foreign incorporation/registration, the partnership agreement, and the name of a Canadian agent for service; no minimum capital is required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; Territorial_Scope; Tax_Exempt_Zone: None (no Canadian federal or BC tax-free zone applies to this activity). The BC Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96: income, gains, losses, and credits are computed at the partnership level and allocated to partners under the partnership agreement. Non-resident partners are deemed by ITA s. 253 to carry on business in Canada through the LP if the LP carries on business in BC, making them liable for Part I tax on their Canadian-source business income, subject to applicable tax treaty relief. For GST/HST and BC PST, the LP is the registrant/vendor and the person liable for collection and remittance; the partners are not jointly liable for the LP's GST/HST obligations except as provided by ETA partnership rules.

Available election forms

Available elections: (1) Partnership fiscal-period election under ITA s. 249.1(4), allowing the LP to adopt and maintain a non-calendar year-end for income tax; (2) annual GST/HST filing election under ETA s. 251.1 for qualifying non-resident registrants, and the same-day filing and remittance election for registrants with multiple program accounts; (3) voluntary GST/HST registration election under ETA s. 240(2) to claim input tax credits on imports and pre-registration acquisitions; (4) partnership allocation elections under ITA s. 96(1), respecting agreed allocations that are fixed before year-end and have economic substance; (5) hybrid-mismatch ordering elections under ITA ss. 18.4(3) and 18.4(4) that may defer the deductibility of a hybrid payment until actual payment, preventing a deduction-without-inclusion outcome for cross-border payments to partners; (6) no Canadian 'check-the-box' election exists - entity classification follows legal form, so a BC limited partnership is per se a flow-through partnership for Canadian income tax.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - the 'small supplier' threshold under ETA s. 148(1). A non-resident LP carrying on commercial activity in Canada must register for GST/HST once its total taxable supplies from Canada-linked commercial activity, including those of associated persons, exceeds CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (computed on worldwide relevant revenue where the non-resident sells into Canada). Non-residents under the threshold may voluntarily register. For BC PST there is no turnover threshold: a vendor must register with the BC Ministry of Finance before making taxable sales or leases of goods or taxable services in BC, or before bringing taxable goods into BC for sale.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating is available under the Excise Tax Act, Schedule VI, Part V: (a) section 1 zero-rates tangible personal property (TPP) exported by the supplier in the course of a commercial activity, where the goods are delivered to a common carrier or consignee in Canada for export or exported directly by the supplier as soon as is reasonable and proof of export is retained; (b) section 5 zero-rates TPP delivered in Canada to a non-resident person who is not a GST/HST registrant, where the non-resident does not acquire the goods for consumption, use, or supply in Canada and exports them within 60 days of delivery; (c) sections 6 and 7 may zero-rate services supplied in respect of exported goods or to non-residents where the service is for consumption outside Canada. For wholesale SSD and server-processor trading, the LP must substantiate physical export of the goods from Canada to a foreign customer; where goods are sold and delivered entirely offshore without entering Canada, the supply is generally outside the scope of GST and no zero-rating claim is required. The BC PST export exemption mirrors the requirement of documented export directly or through a licensed carrier to a destination outside BC.

  • Customs Export Declaration: B13A 'Canada Customs Export Declaration' or a CERS (Canadian Export Reporting System) validation number evidencing the export transaction
  • Bill of Lading: original negotiable ocean bill of lading, air waybill, rail waybill, or truck manifest showing the LP as shipper, the foreign consignee, the Canadian port of exit, and an accurate description of the SSD/CPU goods
  • Commercial Invoice: fully itemized commercial/customs invoice with HS classification (e.g., 8542 for processors/controllers, 8473 for parts/accessories), declared export value, and currency of settlement
  • Inward Remittance Certificate: bank SWIFT MT103, foreign-exchange conversion record, or bank statement crediting the LP's Canadian account in the CAD equivalent of the export sale proceeds
  • Cargo Control Document (CCD) or customs manifest confirming actual departure of the goods from Canada
  • Supporting trade documentation retained contemporaneously: signed purchase order, contract, packing list, and insurance certificate

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, government invoice clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada for B2B invoices. The CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns (GST/HST NETFILE or TELEXML web services) and electronic remittance for payments exceeding CAD 10,000; registrants must retain invoices that meet ETA s. 223 disclosure requirements. The CRA offers API-based and XML web-service transmission for payroll, customs (CERS), and GST/HST programs, but does not pre-authorize invoices. BC PST returns and remittances are filed through the eTaxBC portal with no invoice clearance requirement. There is no e-invoicing validation mandate comparable to Latin American or EU continuous transaction control regimes.

CRA treats export zero-rating as a high-risk audit area. Where the documentary proof bundle is deficient, goods are diverted into the Canadian market, or export timelines are not met, the supply is recharacterized as taxable at 5% GST plus 7% BC PST, and the LP is assessed for uncollected tax, interest, and penalties. Consequences include: (1) reassessment within the normal 4-year statutory period under ETA s. 298, extended indefinitely for misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default; (2) failure-to-file penalty under ETA s. 280 of 1% of the net tax plus 0.25% per complete month of arrears (maximum 12 months); (3) gross-negligence penalty under ETA s. 285 equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the understated net tax; (4) daily compound interest at the CRA prescribed rate (published quarterly); (5) income-tax gross-negligence penalty under ITA s. 163(2) of 50% of the understated Part I tax if the false zero-rating claim reduced taxable income; (6) BC PST penalties for false or missing export evidence, including 10% of the tax or CAD 50 (whichever is greater), interest, and a 100% penalty for intentional evasion under the Provincial Sales Tax Act; and (7) security requirements under ETA s. 299 and, in criminal cases, prosecution under ETA s. 327 for knowingly making false statements. While no federal statute fixes a hard 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, failure to repatriate export proceeds within approximately 180 days may trigger transfer-pricing adjustments under ITA s. 247, foreign-accrual-property-income recharacterization, and increased audit scrutiny of the zero-rating claim.

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility under IMF Article VIII. Current account and capital account operations of a British Columbia limited partnership (CA_BC LP) are unrestricted: foreign capital contributions, offshore borrowing, trade payments, and profit remittances require no central bank approval, purpose code, or conversion mandate. Receivables and disbursements may be held indefinitely in USD, CAD, CNH, or other trade currencies. The only material cross-border frictions are tax compliance obligations, not exchange controls: non-resident partners are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (generally 25%, treaty-reducible) on Canadian-source income allocations and distributions via the T5013/NR4 reporting framework, while capital contributions are exempt from withholding.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Nil. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for export proceeds. SSD/CPU wholesale export receipts may be retained in foreign-denominated bank accounts indefinitely. The only applicable obligations are general FINTRAC reporting for electronic funds transfers equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 and CBSA cross-border currency reporting for monetary instruments equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulation of deposit-taking institutions by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF and cross-border electronic funds transfer surveillance by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada repealed the Exchange Control Act in 1951; no dedicated foreign-exchange control agency, registration, or licensing regime exists for current or capital account transactions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium. FINTRAC-registered money services businesses and electronic money institutions (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer) may legally open remote multi-currency corporate accounts for collections, supplier payouts, and FX-lite settlement; however, they lack direct access to the Lynx RTGS wholesale CAD clearing core, do not carry CDIC deposit insurance, and are treated by Schedule I banks as enhanced-risk counterparties for high-value semiconductor spot trades. Canadian EMI/MSB accounts are viable as top-up and payout corridors but cannot serve as the primary treasury anchor; any EFT at or above CAD 10,000 through an EMI triggers FINTRAC reporting and the same underlying source-of-funds evidentiary burden.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Foreign partner capital contributions are routed via SWIFT MT103 from the partners home bank to the LPs British Columbia operating account. Remittance field 70 must identify the LP name and state Capital Contribution [Partner Name] [Date]. Canada applies no mandatory import purpose code, but the banks KYC classification will record the flow as contribution to partnership capital. FINTRAC EFT reporting attaches at CAD 10,000 or above; CBSA cross-border currency declaration applies at CAD 10,000 or above for bearer negotiable instruments. Capital contributions are excluded from Part XIII withholding and no NR4 is required; the partnership capital account entry and an internal tax memorandum corroborate non-income treatment. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium. FINTRAC-registered money services businesses and electronic money institutions (e.g., Wise Business, Payoneer) may legally open remote multi-currency corporate accounts for collections, supplier payouts, and FX-lite settlement; however, they lack direct access to the Lynx RTGS wholesale CAD clearing core, do not carry CDIC deposit insurance, and are treated by Schedule I banks as enhanced-risk counterparties for high-value semiconductor spot trades. Canadian EMI/MSB accounts are viable as top-up and payout corridors but cannot serve as the primary treasury anchor; any EFT at or above CAD 10,000 through an EMI triggers FINTRAC reporting and the same underlying source-of-funds evidentiary burden.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined federal and BC general corporate rate (15% federal + 12% BC); the limited partnership itself is fiscally transparent and is not subject to entity-level corporate income tax.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII for dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. A BC limited partnership itself does not pay dividends; distributions are partnership allocations. Non-resident limited partners may nonetheless be subject to 25% Part XIII withholding on allocated Canadian-source income, subject to relief under an applicable tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$5 million in aggregate non-arm's-length cross-border transactions in the taxation year is the threshold at which CRA contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is expected; Country-by-Country reporting applies where the consolidated group revenue is at least C$750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime in the manner of Gulf Cooperation Council jurisdictions. However, the CRA may disregard artificial arrangements under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and may challenge a limited partnership lacking commercial substance. The LP must maintain real business premises, decision-making, employees, and documentary evidence of economic activity in BC to sustain its flow-through tax classification and avoid GAAR, foreign accrual property income, or deemed-dividend recharacterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of the transfer-pricing adjustment under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act, plus interest on reassessed tax. Gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of understated tax may apply under subsection 163(2). In egregious cases, criminal prosecution for tax evasion may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a corporate general partner who direct, authorize, or acquiesce in the mispricing may be assessed for gross negligence penalties. Directors may be personally liable for unremitted corporate amounts under subsection 227.1 and may face travel bans, asset seizure, and imprisonment for up to five years if the conduct rises to wilful evasion.
Wilful Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act carries a fine of at least 50% and up to 200% of the tax evaded, plus imprisonment of up to five years. The civil reassessment includes unreported income, interest, and gross-negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: The directing mind of the LP or the corporate general partner is exposed to criminal conviction. Civil restitution may include seizure of personal assets, and travel bans are commonly imposed as bail conditions or ancillary orders in tax evasion prosecutions.
Failure to Withhold Part XIII Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent is jointly and severally liable for the unwithheld Part XIII amount, with interest computed from the date the payment was made. Repeated failures trigger further administrative penalties and may lead to prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner can be assessed personally for unremitted Part XIII tax under subsection 227.1. The general partner itself bears direct civil liability to the CRA, and imprisonment or travel restrictions may result from criminal proceedings for failure to deduct or remit.
Artificial / Shell Arrangement and GAAR

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule permits the CRA to disregard the tax benefit and reassess the participants. Interest, gross-negligence penalties up to 50%, and third-party civil penalties up to C$100,000 may be imposed. The CRA may recharacterize the partnership as a foreign corporation or apply deemed dividend rules.

Director personal liability: Directors, promoters, and partners who knowingly implement a sham arrangement may be jointly and severally liable for resulting tax and penalties. Courts may freeze assets, issue travel bans, and impose imprisonment when the arrangement is found to be a fraudulent conduit.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties can reach seven-figure amounts per violation. Conducting transactions with proceeds of crime triggers Criminal Code money-laundering offences carrying imprisonment of up to 10 years, fines, and forfeiture of assets.

Director personal liability: Officers, directors, and compliance officers who knowingly facilitate money laundering are personally liable to prosecution. They face imprisonment, significant personal fines, asset restraint, travel bans, and effective debarment from regulated wholesale financial activity.
