Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST and federal corporate income tax; BC Ministry of Finance — British Columbia Provincial Sales Tax (PST). Portals: CRA My Business Account, CRA Business Registration Online, and BC eTaxBC.

E-government filing portal

A British Columbia business corporation must first obtain a federal/provincial Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online or during incorporation. For GST/HST, register the BN as a GST/HST account with CRA, either mandatorily when the entity ceases to be a small supplier or voluntarily if a small supplier. For BC PST, register separately with the BC Ministry of Finance through eTaxBC and obtain a BC PST account number. Both accounts must be active before taxable sales commence; CRA issues the BN confirmation notice, and eTaxBC issues the PST registration certificate.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None analogous to U.S. check-the-box. A Canadian business corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act and cannot elect pass-through treatment. GST/HST-specific elections include voluntary small-supplier registration and fiscal-year filing elections; BC PST has no entity classification election. No hybrid mismatch check-the-box election exists under Canadian domestic law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 gross worldwide taxable supply revenue over any preceding four consecutive calendar quarters for the GST/HST small-supplier threshold; a corporation carrying on business in Canada must register once the threshold is exceeded. BC PST has no fixed revenue exemption threshold and requires registration before making taxable sales or leasing taxable goods in British Columbia.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of enterprise B2B SaaS/software services to a non-resident customer is generally zero-rated for GST/HST where the supply is made to a person who is not resident in Canada, the supply qualifies under section 23 and Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act, and the supply is not in respect of Canadian real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. The supplier may claim input tax credits (refunds) for GST/HST incurred on Canadian business inputs attributable to zero-rated exports. Documentary evidence must demonstrate the customer's non-resident status and that delivery and use of the SaaS occurred outside Canada.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A or CERS report) where SaaS is delivered on physical media; otherwise a digital supply/access log with customer jurisdiction and server delivery evidence.
  • Commercial invoice quoting 0% GST/HST and referencing the applicable Schedule VI zero-rating provision.
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill where physical goods are shipped; for SaaS, an execution/access report or CDN delivery logs.
  • Executed SaaS/enterprise license agreement evidencing non-resident counterparty, payment currency, and territorial delivery outside Canada.
  • Inward remittance evidence (SWIFT MT103, bank credit advice, Wise/FX settlement statement, or payment gateway settlement report) proving funds received from abroad.

Canada does not operate a national real-time electronic invoicing clearance or digital stamping regime. GST/HST registrants whose annual gross revenue exceeds C$1 million must file GST/HST returns electronically using CRA-compliant software or GST/HST NETFILE; invoices must include the supplier's BN, date, description, and GST/HST amount per the Excise Tax Act. BC PST returns are filed through eTaxBC, with no API-based invoice pre-clearance or fiscal stamping requirement.

CRA may reassess a claimed zero-rated supply as taxable at 5% GST plus interest if compliance documentation fails to prove non-residency or Canadian use. False or grossly negligent zero-rating claims trigger penalties under the Excise Tax Act, including interest and penalty charges up to 25% of the tax sought to be evaded, minimum C$100 penalties for misstatements, and possible criminal tax evasion prosecution in serious cases. Registrants must retain all export substantiation records for at least six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully liberalized foreign exchange regime. The Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and no current federal statute imposes exchange-control licensing, currency surrender mandates, or repatriation quotas. Both current-account flows (B2B SaaS subscription exports) and capital-account flows (equity injections from a non-resident US or international parent, intercompany shareholder loans, dividend repatriations) are freely convertible without Bank of Canada pre-approval. The Income Tax Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)) is the only substantive constraint on profit remittance: dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder attract Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory 25% rate, reduced to 5% under Article X(2) of the Canada-US Tax Treaty for a US-resident corporate parent beneficially owning at least 10% of the voting stock, or 15% for portfolio dividend recipients; treaty-rate relief requires the non-resident parent to file form NR-301 with the Canada Revenue Agency. No provincial-level foreign-exchange controls or capital-export taxes apply in British Columbia. Operationally, FINTRAC reporting obligations apply: every Canadian financial institution, including Schedule I banks, Wise Payments Canada Corp. (MSB), and Stripe Payments Canada Ltd. (PSP), must report to FINTRAC any electronic funds transfer (EFT) of CAD 10,000 or more entering or leaving Canada within 24 hours under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17). Large capital injections are not approval-gated but must be supported by clean source-of-funds documentation

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender, no mandatory repatriation timeline, and no minimum conversion ratio. A Canadian B2B SaaS exporter may retain USD, EUR, GBP, and other foreign-currency subscription receipts indefinitely in foreign-currency-denominated operational accounts or treasury wallet structures at a Canadian Schedule I bank (multi-currency accounts at RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank, or CIBC), or offshore. The sole statutory obligations are: (i) FINTRAC EFT reporting for any cross-border electronic transfer of CAD 10,000 or more; (ii) corporate income tax recognition of all export revenue under Part I of the Income Tax Act (with the optional functional-currency election under subsection 261(3) permitting a USD tax-reporting currency where audited USD books are maintained); and (iii) GST/HST zero-rating compliance under section 21 of the Excise Tax Act for subscriptions supplied to non-resident persons not registered under the ETA, supported by retained proof-of-foreign-supply documentation (customer contracts, IP addresses, billing addresses, and business registration evidence of the non-resident customer)

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada, BoC) as the national monetary authority; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the central financial intelligence unit and cross-border transaction reporting supervisor; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as the federal prudential regulator governing Schedule I banks; Payments Canada as the national payment clearing and settlement operator (owner-operator of Lynx, ACSS, and the Automated Funds Transfer system)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: High as a complementary disbursement and collection rail, but legally constrained as a primary treasury repository. Wise (operating in Canada through Wise Payments Canada Corp., a FINTRAC-registered money services business under the PCMLTFA and a licensed money-services business in British Columbia under the provincial Money Services Act) is a viable rail for B2B SaaS foreign-currency contractor payouts, vendor settlement, and multi-currency wallet operations, providing routing through local clearing systems in CAD, USD, EUR, and GBP. Stripe Billing via Stripe Payments Canada Ltd. is the optimum inbound subscription merchant-acquiring rail, offering global card processing and bank-debit collection in over 135 currencies. CRITICAL LEGAL CONSTRAINT: a foreign-domiciled EMI (e.g., a UK FCA-authorised EMI or an EU-authorised payment institution) cannot directly access Canadian clearing rails (Payments Canada ACSS and Lynx systems) and cannot provide native Canadian CAD account infrastructure (Canadian 3-digit institution number, 5-digit transit number, and account number); such offshore EMIs will settle through correspondent banks, incurring SWIFT-level costs, and their holding of corporate funds is neither CDIC-insured nor OSFI-supervised. Therefore, the treasury architecture must be a dual-rail design: Schedule I bank operating accounts for payroll, HST remittance, statutory filings, and liquidity reserves, complemented by Wise and Stripe as operational collection/disbursement engines. A foreign offshore EMI must never hold the company's primary liquidity, corporate treasury reserve, or capital-injection proceeds

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity capital injection is executed as a SWIFT MT103 wire from the non-resident parent's account to the Canadian operating company's USD-denominated account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. The MT103 must carry an unambiguous payment narrative referencing the share subscription agreement and the purpose code 'Direct Investment in Canada'; the receiving account identifier comprises the Canadian 3-digit bank institution number, the 5-digit transit number (BC branch), and the company's account number, together with the receiving bank's SWIFT BIC. Since Canada imposes no exchange-control filing or pre-approval, the injection is immediately available; the only mandatory reporting obligation is the FINTRAC cross-border EFT report (CAD 10,000+ threshold) filed by the receiving bank within 24 hours. Board minutes, the share subscription agreement, priced share certificates, and an updated capitalization table must accompany the onboarding documentation; for injections exceeding CAD 100,000, the bank's source-of-funds questionnaire must be completed with the parent's financial statements and source-of-wealth evidence. The equity contribution is treated as tax-free contributed surplus/capital under Part I of the Income Tax Act (no Canadian withholding or capital tax), and the BC corporate register (or federal CBCA register) must be updated within the applicable statutory filing window. Tier 1 - Domestic payroll and Canada Revenue Agency remittances: settled through Payments Canada ACSS using batch EFT files originated from the Schedule I operating account, including source deductions (T4), CPP, EI, and GST/HST filings. Tier 2 - International vendor and contractor wires: executed as SWIFT MT103 across the SWIFT gpi network from the Schedule I operational account; international wire fees range from CAD 15 to 85 per wire at premium treasury tiers; each wire exceeding CAD 10,000 triggers the mandatory 24-hour FINTRAC EFT report. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: High as a complementary disbursement and collection rail, but legally constrained as a primary treasury repository. Wise (operating in Canada through Wise Payments Canada Corp., a FINTRAC-registered money services business under the PCMLTFA and a licensed money-services business in British Columbia under the provincial Money Services Act) is a viable rail for B2B SaaS foreign-currency contractor payouts, vendor settlement, and multi-currency wallet operations, providing routing through local clearing systems in CAD, USD, EUR, and GBP. Stripe Billing via Stripe Payments Canada Ltd. is the optimum inbound subscription merchant-acquiring rail, offering global card processing and bank-debit collection in over 135 currencies. CRITICAL LEGAL CONSTRAINT: a foreign-domiciled EMI (e.g., a UK FCA-authorised EMI or an EU-authorised payment institution) cannot directly access Canadian clearing rails (Payments Canada ACSS and Lynx systems) and cannot provide native Canadian CAD account infrastructure (Canadian 3-digit institution number, 5-digit transit number, and account number); such offshore EMIs will settle through correspondent banks, incurring SWIFT-level costs, and their holding of corporate funds is neither CDIC-insured nor OSFI-supervised. Therefore, the treasury architecture must be a dual-rail design: Schedule I bank operating accounts for payroll, HST remittance, statutory filings, and liquidity reserves, complemented by Wise and Stripe as operational collection/disbursement engines. A foreign offshore EMI must never hold the company's primary liquidity, corporate treasury reserve, or capital-injection proceeds
