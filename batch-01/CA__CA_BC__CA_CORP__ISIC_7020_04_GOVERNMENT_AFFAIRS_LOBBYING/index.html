Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — Canadian-resident corporation taxed under Income Tax Act Part I on worldwide net income (federal base rate 38% subject to general 28.0%/13.0% abatement/reduction, effective federal 15.0%; BC general corporate rate 12.0%; combined ≈ 27% for general income); no territorial remittance basis; foreign tax credits available under s. 113/126; non-resident shareholders subject to Part XIII withholding on dividends unless treaty-reduced (Canada–US generally 5% if ≥10% voting ownership, else 15%); BC PST/GST collected at point of supply; not a Tax_Exempt_Zone; no Pass_Through_Eligible status for an incorporated entity

Available election forms

No US-style 'check-the-box' election exists in Canada; corporate status is fixed. Available elections: (a) ITA s. 216(1) — non-resident may elect to file Part I return on Canadian rental/property income in lieu of Part XIII; (b) ITA s. 39(4) — deemed disposition/capital-gain elections on emigration; (c) ITA s. 256(9)/CCPC — associated-corporation allocation of small business deduction; (d) GST/HST elections: ETA s. 156 (closely related corporations net tax), ETA s. 167 (joint election on sale of a business), ETA s. 211 (financial institutions); (e) Form RC4060 (GST/HST status) and Form RC4167 (small supplier election to register voluntarily); (f) hybrid mismatch: Canada applies OECD BEPS Action 2 rules in domestic statute (s. 18.2, 18.4, 18.6 ITA) — no elective opt-out; hybrid entities are classified under a purpose-based paradigm consistent with the OECD model

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Services (including government affairs advisory, lobbying, trade policy research, and stakeholder engagement) supplied to a non-resident client are zero-rated under ETA s. 25 if: (i) the recipient is not a Canadian resident at the time of supply; (ii) the supply is not 'specified' — i.e., not in respect of real property located in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property delivered in Canada, not in respect of a service performed wholly in Canada on tangible personal property, and not a 'advisory/consulting' service substantially performed in Canada unless supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and is not a prescribed 'listed financial institution'; practical test: performance from BC for an overseas government or foreign trade association qualifies as zero-rated if the recipient is outside Canada and the benefit/carriage completed abroad; BC PST: services are not generally subject to PST unless specifically enumerated — lobbying/advisory services to non-residents are PST-exempt as 'services performed solely outside BC' or 'for use exclusively outside BC' (BC PST Regulation 4.01); goods exported under ETA s. 23 and BC PST s. 178 (export exemption) are zero-rated/exempt if exported within 60 days and documentary proof retained

  • Commercial Invoice with HS classification (if goods) or detailed service invoice (ISIC 7020 / NAICS 541820) referencing zero-rating sections (ETA s. 25; s. 23)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / Cargo Importer Export Report (for physical exports) or proof of export of tangible deliverables (e.g., USB/printed materials) if any
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or bank statement evidencing receipt of payment in foreign currency from the non-resident client, including FX conversion record (CRA CRA-ARC audit standard)
  • Signed engagement contract/soa identifying the non-resident recipient, country of benefit, absence of Canadian in-Canada consumption, and scope of lobbying activities with the Canadian federal/BC government body being advised on behalf of the foreign principal
  • If lobbying: written confirmation of OCL/BC lobbyists registration disclosing 'foreign principal' status — supporting the nature of cross-border consultation services rather than domestic advocacy

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both capital and current accounts, with no residual exchange controls since the repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951. A British Columbia business corporation may lawfully receive foreign equity injections, hold foreign-currency bank accounts, and remit dividends or repatriate capital without central bank approval. Profit remittance is unrestricted subject only to tax compliance: Part I federal corporate tax on taxable Canadian income, Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on dividends and specified payments (statutory 25%, reduced to treaty rates generally 0% to 15% for shareholders resident in comprehensive income tax treaty jurisdictions), and, for a BC-resident corporation, no provincial remittance control or capital repatriation quota.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed repatriation deadline for export receipts. A government-affairs advisory corporation may retain foreign-currency export proceeds in a Canadian bank account indefinitely. Compliance obligations are confined to: (i) goods/services export data reporting to Statistics Canada where applicable (Canadian Export Declaration or International Trade in Services surveys); (ii) FINTRAC large cash transaction reports and cross-border electronic funds transfer reports for transactions at or above C$10,000; and (iii) standard corporate record-keeping under the Income Tax Act and the BC Business Corporations Act for a retention period of six years.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada does not maintain a separate Foreign Exchange Administration; exchange rate policy is determined by the Department of Finance Canada and the Bank of Canada under a free-floating currency regime, with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervising financial institutions and FINTRAC administering AML/CFT reporting for cross-border electronic funds transfers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Low as a primary banking rail; Moderate as an ancillary collection channel. Canada has no distinct Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence class, and foreign EMI authorizations (e.g., UK FCA EMI, EU EMI Directive) do not passport into Canada. Digital payment providers must register as Payment Service Providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act, administered by the Bank of Canada, and money transmitters must register as Money Services Businesses with FINTRAC. A BC corporation may use an offshore digital EMI for foreign-currency receipt aggregation, but it cannot serve as the main operating account because: (i) EMI deposits are not CDIC-insured; (ii) EMI rails do not natively support C$ compliance obligations such as GST/HST remittance and payroll; (iii) routing lobbyist retainers through an unlicensed overseas EMI elevates the bank's AML risk rating and may trigger enhanced due diligence; and (iv) Canadian privacy law and RCMP/CFSEU guidance disfavor opaque fintech layering for politically exposed client portfolios.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder equity injections are routed by SWIFT MT103 to the corporate operational account at a CDIC-member Schedule I bank; the Beneficiary field must exactly match the registered corporate name; the Remittance Information field should reference the share issuance resolution or subscription number; no Bank of Canada approval is required, but FINTRAC EFT reporting applies at or above C$10,000, and the bank may require the corporate resolution and subscription agreement on file. For investments by non-Canadian stakeholders, verify whether Investment Canada Act notification or national-security review is triggered; for routine treasury purposes the routing protocol is: (i) confirm the remitter is the named shareholder; (ii) annotate purpose code as direct investment/equity; (iii) utilize the bank's inward remittance service to obtain CAD settlement at a transparent corporate FX rate; and (iv) retain the SWIFT reference, FX ticket, and board resolution for a minimum of six years.Cross-border service disbursements are cleared primarily via SWIFT MT103 for international retainer payments and subscription fees, executed through the bank's correspondent network in CAD, USD, or EUR. Domestic clearing within Canada uses Payments Canada's LYNX real-time settlement system for high-value payments above C$730,000, and the older automated clearing settlement system for routine EFT; Interac e-Transfer is used for small-value emergency disbursements. United States-dollar payables may be settled through Fedwire or cross-border ACH via a US correspondent account. Commercial letters of credit are not ordinarily required for advisory services but remain permissible under UCP 600 for transactions with counterparties in high-risk jurisdictions. Every outbound transaction requires an accompanying purpose narrative, supporting invoice or contract, and screening against sanctions lists; the corporation must preserve the SWIFT reference, FX confirmation, and underlying contract for audit and income tax purposes.Outright deliverable foreign exchange forwards (CAD/USD and CAD/EUR) executed with Schedule I dealer banks under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement, used to fix future retainer revenues and fee disbursements.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) restricted to illiquid or restricted-currency pairs where physical delivery is unavailable; CAD pairs are deliverable, so NDF usage is limited to exotic emerging-market currency exposures arising from trade-policy advisory engagements.Cross-currency interest rate swaps (CAD/USD) and currency basis swaps for long-dated balance-sheet hedging of intercompany loans or foreign shareholder capital exposure.OTC vanilla foreign exchange options (purchased call and put options only) permitted for treasury hedging without speculative intent, subject to the bank's derivatives suitability review and the corporate hedging policy.Spot foreign exchange contracts traded on the bank's electronic trading platform in accordance with the FX Global Code, with settlement through continuous linked settlement (CLS) where available to eliminate Herstatt risk.

Canada has no distinct Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence class, and foreign EMI authorizations (e.g., UK FCA EMI, EU EMI Directive) do not passport into Canada. Digital payment providers must register as Payment Service Providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act, administered by the Bank of Canada, and money transmitters must register as Money Services Businesses with FINTRAC. A BC corporation may use an offshore digital EMI for foreign-currency receipt aggregation, but it cannot serve as the main operating account because: (i) EMI deposits are not CDIC-insured; (ii) EMI rails do not natively support C$ compliance obligations such as GST/HST remittance and payroll; (iii) routing lobbyist retainers through an unlicensed overseas EMI elevates the bank's AML risk rating and may trigger enhanced due diligence; and (iv) Canadian privacy law and RCMP/CFSEU guidance disfavor opaque fintech layering for politically exposed client portfolios.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate: 27% (federal Part I effective 15% after general rate reduction + BC general rate 12%). Eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) pay 11% combined on active business income within the C$500,000 small business deduction limit (federal 9% + BC 2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory Canadian dividend withholding tax under Part XIII is 25% of gross dividends paid by a resident Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder; treaty relief can reduce the rate to 5% where the beneficial owner holds a substantial interest (generally 10% or more of voting shares) and otherwise to 15% under Canada-U.S. and many other DTA frameworks.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate fair-market value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents triggers mandatory Form T106 filing. Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation, aligned with CRA Information Circular 87-2R and OECD TP Guidelines, should be maintained separately for each material intercompany transaction; Country-by-Country Report obligations apply when multinational group consolidated revenue is above the C$750 million threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain Cayman-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the Income Tax Act s.245 General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), sham doctrine, substance-over-form, and treaty anti-abuse rules create a functional economic substance test. A BC government-affairs corporation must demonstrate real decision-making, personnel, office, operational expenditure, and risk exposure in Canada; a passive shell fronting for non-resident owners is exposed to recharacterization of income, denial of treaty benefits, and full reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239: on summary conviction, fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, fine up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years. Gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) is 50% of the understated tax, plus compounding interest and reinstated taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate or acquiesce in evasion are exposed to criminal liability and can be assessed for unpaid source deductions under s.227.1. The corporate veil is pierced where the company is a facade; directors face civil restitution, personal fines, asset seizure, court-ordered travel bans, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust all cross-border prices to an arm's-length result under ITA s.247(2). Where documentation is inadequate and an adjustment arises, s.247(3) imposes a penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment, in addition to tax, interest, and potential gross negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false T106 schedules or misleading TP studies can be prosecuted under tax-evasion and false-statement provisions. While TP penalties are corporate, personal liability arises for fraudulent documentation; directors may be ordered to make restitution and may face travel restrictions during criminal proceedings.
Shell Company Abuse and General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR)

Statutory sanction: GAAR under s.245 ITA allows CRA to deny the entire tax benefit, recharacterize the transaction, and assess on the true commercial substance. Sham transactions may be void; false-statement penalties under s.163.2 apply up to 50% of misreported tax. Non-resident treaty-shopping arrangements are separately challenged under treaty limitation-on-benefits provisions.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced where the shell is used to commit fraud or evade obligations; directors may be held personally liable for corporate tax and restitution. Departure bans, passport seizures, and imprisonment follow criminal abuse where foreign-controlled shell profits are fraudulently repatriated.
Unregistered Government Lobbying and Influence-Trade Offences

Statutory sanction: Federal Lobbying Act s.68(1): fine up to C$250,000, imprisonment up to 2 years, or both; BC statute penalties include monetary fines, disgorgement, and compliance orders. Illegal lobbying expenses are not deductible under ITA s.18(1)(a), and a conviction may debar the corporation from federal contracts and compliance audits.

Director personal liability: Officers who direct or fail to prevent unregistered lobbying are parties to the offence and can receive personal criminal sentences, civil orders for disgorgement of fees, travel bans on judicial order, and restrictions on holding directorships in regulated sectors.
Anti-Money Laundering and FINTRAC Compliance Failures

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act empowers FINTRAC to impose administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 for corporate violations; criminal money-laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31 is punishable by imprisonment up to 10 years. Forfeiture orders may capture all laundered proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct or benefit from laundering schemes are individually criminally liable; civil proceedings can strip personal assets and impose restitution. Bail terms in serious AML cases routinely include travel bans, surrender of passports, and court-supervised reporting.
