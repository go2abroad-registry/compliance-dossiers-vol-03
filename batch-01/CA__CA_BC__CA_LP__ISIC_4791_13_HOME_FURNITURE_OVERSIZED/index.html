Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital for a BC limited partnership. The initial limited partners' capital contributions are set out in the Limited Partnership Agreement and in the Declaration; the Partnership Act imposes no escrow or trust-account requirement. Contributions are payable to the partnership's bank account in accordance with the Limited Partnership Agreement and recorded in the register of partners.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification issued by the local fire department under the BC Fire Code; structural occupancy/load clearance from the municipal building department; electrical safety permit from Technical Safety BC; and WorkSafeBC occupational health and safety registration and clearance before workplace operation.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – federal GST/HST and income tax administration; British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch – provincial sales tax (PST) administration. Primary portal: CRA My Business Account via canada.ca; BC eTaxBC via gov.bc.ca.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-formed limited partnership that is not resident in Canada and does not carry on business in Canada may nevertheless obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1, Request for a Business Number and Related Accounts, or by using CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO). The Canadian resident general partner or a designated representative must supply the partnership agreement, certificates of existence of the foreign partners, and the allocation percentages of each partner. The CRA issues a 15-digit BN comprising a 9-digit base number, a 2-letter program identifier (e.g., RT for GST/HST), and a 4-digit account reference. For BC PST, the foreign LP must register through eTaxBC and obtain a separate provincial vendor account; the application requires the federal BN, likely place of business, and description of taxable sales in BC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no 'check-the-box' election; status as a partnership is determined by domestic law. A limited partnership is not a separate taxpayer for federal income tax purposes and its income or loss is allocated to partners under the Income Tax Act (Canada). For GST/HST, the partnership may elect to be a 'selected listed financial institution' under section 225.2 of the Excise Tax Act if applicable. The partners may also make a section 249.1 election to establish a common fiscal period for the partnership, and hybrid mismatch rules under section 18.4 of the Income Tax Act require informed filing elections only where a non-Canadian hybrid element is present; otherwise no elective classification beyond statutory partnership transparency is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD worldwide taxable supplies for GST/HST registration; BC PST has no general turnover threshold for foreign sellers, but registration is mandatory if the seller carries on business in BC, has physical presence in BC, or makes marketplace sales through a platform that is deemed to be a PST-collecting marketplace facilitator. For non-resident remote sellers with no Canadian presence, PST collection may still be required for goods, software, and certain services purchased in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of tangible personal property is zero-rated if the property is exported by or on behalf of the supplier in accordance with section 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 or 25 of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. An online retailer of oversized furniture must show that the furniture was sold to a customer outside Canada, that the goods were delivered outside Canada or placed in a customs bonded warehouse before delivery, and that the consideration is paid to the seller. For services incidentally supplied with exported goods, zero-rating applies where the supply is made to a non-resident person and the service is not performed in Canada, subject to CRA technical guidelines. The supplier must keep contemporaneous documentary evidence that the goods and services were supplied for export and were actually exported.

  • B13A Export Declaration or authorized electronic export reporting manifest (CBSA/Statistics Canada)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, truck, or rail) showing a foreign consignee and foreign final destination
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order identifying the goods, price, and non-resident buyer
  • Cargo Control Document evidencing physical exit from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank confirmation of foreign currency payment from the non-resident customer
  • Packing list for oversized goods
  • Limited partnership registration extract and beneficial ownership schedule

Canada does not impose a national real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime. GST/HST invoices must contain prescribed particulars under the Excise Tax Act, including the supplier's GST/HST registration number, date, tax amount, and vendor name. Electronic invoices are accepted and may be transmitted via PEPPOL, EDI, or other machine-readable formats on a voluntary basis. There is no API-based clearance mandate, no continuous transaction controls, and no pre-issuance approval of e-invoices by CRA. BC PST returns and vendor filings are submitted electronically through eTaxBC, but no real-time tax cockpit or digital fiscal stamping applies.

CRA may reassess within four years for ordinary assessments, and without a fixed limitation period where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default exists. If zero-rating is denied, the taxpayer is liable for uncollected GST/HST, daily compounding interest, and penalties of 6% of the net tax remittance deficiency plus 2% per month of arrears. For gross negligence or wilful evasion, the federal penalty may be up to 25% of the amount sought to be evaded, and criminal prosecution may lead to additional fines and imprisonment. BC PST audits apply similar clawback and penalty provisions for PST improperly remitted or for invoices in which a PST exemption was claimed without export evidence.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully free-floating regime without exchange-control restrictions under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act. Both current-account (goods/services) and capital-account (equity, loans, dividends, profit repatriation) transactions are freely convertible in CAD. A BC limited partnership may remit allocable profits to non-resident partners without Bank of Canada approval; the only friction is Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, potentially reduced by an applicable tax treaty (e.g., 15% for US partners), and FINTRAC reporting for EFTs exceeding CAD 10,000. No profit-remittance licensing, no queue, and no prior approval is imposed.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender obligation to the central bank. Export revenues in USD/EUR may be retained abroad, held in foreign-currency accounts at Canadian Schedule I banks, or converted on the open market. The sole regulatory duty is the FINTRAC cross-border electronic funds transfer report (EFTR) for any wire transfer of CAD 10,000 or more entering or leaving Canada.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank prudential regulation and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF supervision

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility with material legal constraints. Foreign digital EMI platforms (Wise, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury, Payoneer) may serve as payment facilitation channels only if they are registered as money service businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC; they cannot hold deposits or operate a full current-account relationship because deposit-taking is reserved for entities licensed under the Bank Act (Canada). A BC LP must maintain its primary operating account with a Schedule I bank; EMIs are viable for collections from marketplace rails (Stripe, Affirm BNPL settlement) and for vendor disbursements below CAD 10,000, but all cross-border flows above that threshold must be visible to FINTRAC via the underlying bank. Enhanced due diligence applies where the EMI is domiciled outside Canada and funds are pooled with unrelated merchants.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident partner capital contributions must be remitted by SWIFT MT103 from the partner's foreign bank to the LP's CAD or foreign-currency account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. Field 57A (Account With Institution) must carry the Canadian bank BIC; field 59 (Beneficiary) must show the exact legal name and account number of the BC limited partnership; field 70 (Remittance Information) must state 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION — LIMITED PARTNERSHIP AGREEMENT DATED [DATE]'. The ordering bank must populate the originator and beneficiary identifiers to satisfy FINTRAC EFT reporting. The LP must maintain a capital account ledger, an executed subscription/resolution package, and proof of the partner's source of funds. Any transfer of CAD 10,000 or greater is automatically reported to FINTRAC by the bank; no prior regulatory approval is required.Trade settlements for oversized furniture imports settle through (i) SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers for open-account vendor payments; (ii) the domestic Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) or the new Real-Time Rail (RTR) for same-day CAD-denominated payments to Canadian carriers, customs brokers, and 3PL warehouses; and (iii) Commercial Documentary Letters of Credit issued under UCP600 where the supplier demands bank recourse.
