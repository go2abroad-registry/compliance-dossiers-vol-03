Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account (GST/HST). For British Columbia: BC Ministry of Finance – PST Registration via BC eTax or provincial tax portal

E-government filing portal

Foreign limited partnerships must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account by completing Form RC1 with Schedule RC1A (partnership information) or filing online through BRO after receiving partnership agreement and Canadian contact/agent details. The CRA issues a 15-digit program account identifier with RM or RT prefix. For BC PST, the partnership must register with the BC Ministry of Finance using Form FIN 447 or the BC Online Portal, providing name, ownership information, and evidence of commercial presence/activity in British Columbia.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election for limited partnerships; the BC LP is fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act, with partners taxable on allocated income. Partnerships may elect a non-calendar fiscal period under ITA subsection 96(4), utilize capital cost allowance elections under ITA subsection 96(8), and apply GST/HST elections under Excise Tax Act subsection 273 (partner as agent). Hybrid mismatch and excessive eligibility rules are mandatory, not elective, under ITA sections 18.2/18.3 and 18.4/18.5.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 in total worldwide taxable supplies in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters (small supplier threshold); once the threshold is exceeded, registration is generally mandatory by the end of the following month. Export zero-rated supplies count toward the threshold. BC PST registration has no minimum threshold for a vendor making taxable sales in BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Part I of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act applies to goods supplied by way of sale for direct consideration and delivered by the vendor or the customer to a destination outside Canada. The seller must maintain documentary proof that the goods were exported before payment and within a reasonable time after supply. For offshore services provided to non-resident clients and wholly performed outside Canada, zero-rating may also be available. The goods must not be further altered or used in Canada before export, and export must occur under a valid CBSA export declaration.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A) filed through CBSA electronic export reporting
  • Commercial invoice or customs drop-shipment invoice issued by the seller
  • Outbound Bill of Lading / Airway Bill / Cargo Control Document naming the Canadian exporter and foreign consignee
  • Bank inward remittance certificate, SWIFT confirmation, or documentary letter of credit showing FX repatriation to Canada

No federal or provincial real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing mandate, or API data stamping is currently in force. CRA accepts electronic invoices if they are readable and auditable. The Canadian Invoicing Standard (CIUS) is optional. GST/HST returns may be filed electronically via CRA My Business Account; BC PST returns are filed through the provincial tax portal. No continuous transaction controls are implemented.

CRA may reassess GST/HST where zero-rating is claimed without proof of actual export, requiring recovery of tax not paid, statutory interest, and penalties. False statements or omissions exposing a person to a qualified insolvency or misrepresentation may attract a penalty under Excise Tax Act section 285 of C$5,000 plus 50% of the tax in question, or larger civil penalties. Criminal prosecution for fraudulent export claims can lead to fines and imprisonment. BC PST is typically not chargeable on exported goods, but mischaracterizing a domestic BC sale as an export triggers PST exposure and section 62 penalties under BC tax statutes.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under both current and capital account items. There are no exchange control laws, no quantitative restrictions, and no prior approval requirements for capital inflows or outflows under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act. Profit and capital remittances by a non-resident limited partner are freely permitted; however, income tax treaty considerations (Canada–foreign tax treaties) and potential Part XIII withholding taxes may apply to certain payments, mitigated by treaty rates. For a BC limited partnership treated as fiscally transparent, the non-resident partner is subject to Canadian tax on Canadian-source income through the partnership, with no separate remittance approval hurdle.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement exists in Canada. Exporters are not obliged to repatriate or convert export proceeds into CAD within any timeline. However, all cross-border transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 (or foreign currency equivalent) must be reported to FINTRAC by the financial institution; the exporting entity must retain underlying commercial documents (invoices, bills of lading, customs declarations) for a minimum of five years for audit readiness.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange administrative oversight exercised jointly with the Department of Finance Canada and FINTRAC for reporting obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low to Medium feasibility. Canada has no EU-style EMI charter; non-bank payment service providers must register under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada, and must not hold customer funds unless partnered with a regulated bank. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., UK-licensed, EU-licensed) generally cannot open domestic Canadian settlement accounts and are not acceptable for receiving urea/potash trade proceeds through BC commercial banking rails. A regulated Schedule I bank account remains the only fully compliant treasury backbone for this entity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: Non-resident limited partners remit funds via SWIFT MT103 to the bank-designated receiving account in Vancouver, BC. Wire details must include: (a) beneficiary legal name of the BC limited partnership exactly as registered; (b) beneficiary account number and bank SWIFT/BIC of the Canadian institution; (c) purpose of payment stated as 'Capital Contribution to Limited Partnership' (field 70) referencing the partnership agreement article; (d) remitting bank of the partner's own institution outside Canada; (e) for USD remittances settle via the bank's US clearing correspondent (Fedwire or CHIPS); for EUR remittances via SWIFT with the Canadian bank's Euro correspondent. For amounts exceeding CAD 10,000, the financial institution will submit a FINTRAC report; no pre-approval regulatory filing is required under the Investment Canada Act for a new limited partnership where aggregate asset value of the Canadian business is below the WTO threshold (which currently applies to acquisitions — a capital contribution to a wholly new LP does not trigger review). Confirm the non-resident partner does not acquire control of an existing Canadian enterprise.Primary rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 for direct payment against pro-forma invoice (prepayment or on open account terms) — recommended for trusted, recurring suppliers of potash and urea within the OECD; (2) Commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) issued via the Canadian bank's trade finance desk, settled through SWIFT MT700/MT798 — mandatory for first-time counterparties or trades involving non-OECD supply sources; L/C should be irrevocable, at-sight, conforming to UCP 600, with a description drawn from the purchase contract and a required presentation set including commercial invoice, full set of clean-on-board bill of lading, insurance certificate, and inspection certificate. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for goods transactions, CAD 500,000 for services, and CAD 250,000 for other transactions with non-residents (per CRA guidance). Master File required if gross revenue exceeds CAD 50 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) like UAE, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles apply. The CRA may challenge transactions lacking economic substance. For LPs, the partnership must have real business activity and substance to avoid recharacterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and BC provincial statutes; fines up to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners may be personally liable for unpaid taxes if they participated in or acquiesced to the evasion; civil penalties and potential travel bans.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR to deny tax benefits, reassess income, and impose penalties of 25% of the tax benefit; interest on arrears.

Director personal liability: Partners may be jointly and severally liable for taxes and penalties if they participated in the scheme; potential criminal charges for fraud.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), fines up to CAD 2 million for individuals and CAD 10 million for entities; criminal prosecution possible.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners can be personally fined and imprisoned up to 5 years for knowingly failing to comply.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA can adjust prices, impose penalties of 10% of the net adjustment (if gross negligence), and charge interest on reassessed tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they directed or participated in the non-compliance; civil liability for restitution.
