Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

Foreign-owner LP: (1) apply to CRA for a 15-digit Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or Form RC1; (2) activate the GST/HST program account; (3) add an Import/Export account for customs; (4) register with BC through eTaxBC for a Provincial Sales Tax (PST) number; (5) provide foreign incorporation documents, non-resident ownership declarations, and a Canadian contact address. No minimum capital is prescribed for BC limited partnership formation under the Partnership Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a US-style check-the-box election. Under the Excise Tax Act, the LP is treated as a separate 'person' for GST/HST registration purposes, while partners are taxed on allocated income. Available elections include a partnership fiscal-period election under Income Tax Act s. 249.1(7), a GST/HST net-tax election for affiliated or closely related corporations under ETA s. 156, and PST election/endor registration options for BC distributors.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST: CAD 30,000 annual worldwide taxable supplies (small-supplier threshold); PST: no small-supplier exemption for ordinary wholesalers making taxable commercial sales in BC, so registration is required from the first taxable sale.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of craft beer to non-resident buyers are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 20 of the Excise Tax Act (ETA), provided the goods are exported directly by the LP or delivered to a common carrier for export. The LP must obtain documentary proof of export within two years from the supply date and retain it for audit. PST does not apply to goods delivered outside British Columbia. Canada imposes no statutory FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day standard is adopted as an internal compliance convention.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A / CAED)
  • Commercial invoice describing the goods as zero-rated export
  • Bill of lading (ocean/air/truck) showing the exporter of record
  • Foreign customs import documentation where available
  • Evidence of payment or FX receipt such as bank wire confirmation; an inward remittance certificate is not a CRA requirement

No real-time fiscal clearance, API clearance, or digital stamping mandate exists in Canada or British Columbia. CRA accepts structured electronic invoices if all prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act is legible and retained; GST/HST returns must be filed electronically where annual input taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,000,000 or where the supplier elects to file electronically.

CRA actively audits export substantiation. Failure to produce sufficient export evidence triggers reassessment at applicable GST/PST, denial of input tax credits, retroactive clawback of zero-rating, interest from the original remittance date, and statutory penalties for false statements or omissions, including gross-negligence penalties and possible criminal prosecution for knowingly making false or deceptive declarations.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction; the CAD is fully convertible and floating with no exchange controls on current or capital account transactions. Current-account payments (beer inventory imports, kegs, freight, logistics, licensing) are unrestricted. Capital-account flows: inbound capital contributions to a BC limited partnership require no central bank approval and no exchange licence; outbound profit remittances are governed by the Income Tax Act — Part XIII withholding (25% statutory, treaty-reducible) on specified Canadian-source payments to non-resident partners (interest, royalties, management/admin fees); non-resident partners' shares of Canadian active business income are taxed under Part I via non-resident T1/T2 returns; non-resident corporate partners may incur Part XIV branch tax (25% statutory; typically reduced to 5% for US corporate partners under the Canada-US treaty) on Canadian earnings not re-invested. Procedural barriers: FINTRAC Large Transaction Reports for cash ≥ CAD 10,000; FINTRAC Cross-Border Currency Reports for physical movements ≥ CAD 10,000; T1135 foreign-property reporting for Canadian-resident partners with foreign assets > CAD 100,000; Investment Canada Act notification (Form EN 8-09) if a non-Canadian establishes the Canadian business. Overall remittance-hurdle rating: low, assuming clean source-of-funds documentation and tax filings substantiate the distribution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio exists in Canada. Export receipts may be retained in CAD or USD in any Schedule I bank account or FINTRAC-registered MSB account. Mandatory compliance: (1) export sales of goods must be zero-rated for GST/HST (Excise Tax Act, Sch. VI, Part V) with supporting export documentation; (2) CBSA B13A export declarations for commercial keg shipments; (3) FINTRAC Large Transaction Report for cash deposits ≥ CAD 10,000; (4) CRA six-year books-and-records retention; (5) T1135 reporting by resident partners if applicable. FX conversion timing and ratio are unrestricted and market-driven.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulator of Schedule I banks and authorized foreign banks: Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT supervisor and cross-border currency reporting administrator: Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); exchange fund and FX reserve management: Department of Finance Canada / Bank of Canada. No separate exchange-control administration exists in Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium. Foreign and domestic electronic-money institutions (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, Corpay) operate lawfully in Canada as FINTRAC-registered money-services businesses (MSBs) under the PCMLTFA; a BC LP may use them as execution rails for low-value CAD/USD payables and receivables, often at sub-bank FX cost. Legal constraints: EMIs are not banks under the Bank Act; funds are not CDIC-insured; no lending, LC, or overdraft capacity; BC's Money Services Business Act (S.B.C. 2019, c.17), when brought into force, will add provincial registration; account freezes at KYC refresh are common where the LP has non-resident UBOs. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital must be wired by SWIFT MT103 from the non-resident partner's bank to the LP's CAD/USD account at a Schedule I bank, with the beneficiary name identical to the BC-registered LP, and reference field citing 'Capital Contribution — [LP legal name] — Partnership Agreement Section [x]'. Settlement: overseas USD wires clear via the bank's US/UK correspondents and enter the Canadian LVTS (Large Value Transfer System) real-time in CAD; USD-denominated injections may remain in a USD clearing sub-account. Document pack per injection: (1) executed capital-commitment schedule or amended schedule; (2) corporate-GP resolution; (3) contributor's source-of-funds evidence (bank statements, sale/loan agreements); (4) BC Limited Partnership registration certificate; (5) CRA Business Number confirmation; (6) Investment Canada Act notification receipt (if non-Canadian establishment); (7) FINTRAC Large Transaction Report generated by the receiving bank. No exchange-control approval, purpose-code classification, or conversion licence is required; the narrative purpose field must honestly describe the commercial purpose ('inbound capital for BC craft-beer wholesale operations').Recommended clearing hierarchy: (1) SWIFT MT103 international wires for foreign brewery suppliers (US/UK/EU) — include supplier BIC/IBAN, commercial invoice number, and CBSA/customs reference; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit (UCP 600, confirmed at beneficiary's bank) for kegs, canning lines, or equipment ≥ USD 250,000; (3) domestic CAD disbursements via LVTS for same-day payments to BC/Canadian brewers, or the Real-Time Rail (RTR)/E-transfer for sub-CAD 10,000 invoices; (4) US-dollar clearing services (ACH/Fedwire) for recurring USD supplier payments; (5) FX conversion at a same-day spot contract when CAD/USD mismatch requires it. Every disbursement must be documented with the commercial invoice, packing list, proof of delivery, excise-release or import documentation, and copy of the licence, to satisfy bank sanctions screening and CRA record-retention rules. Bank-imposed controls typically include daily aggregate limits, allow-listing of counterparty IBANs, and two-factor authorisation by the GP officers.Deliverable FX spot and outright forwards (CAD/USD, EUR/CAD) with Schedule I banks or authorized foreign banks under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement; tenors 1–12 months; must evidence underlying trade exposure (import payables/export receivables) to pass bank hedge-appropriateness review.Cross-currency swaps (XCCY) and long-dated forward structures for USD capital expenditures; subject to OSFI Guideline B-7 uncleared margin rules and, above thresholds, mandatory clearing at the Canadian Derivatives Clearing Corporation (CDCC).Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) — legally permissible and enforceable as eligible financial contracts under Canadian insolvency law (BIA s.161.1; WURA s.22); market practice: atypical for freely floating CAD; primarily available through offshore counterparties.Vanilla FX options, zero-cost collars/tunnels, and participating forwards for asymmetric protection on forecast USD cash flows; require GP resolution and premium funding.Exchange-traded CAD/USD futures on the Montréal Exchange (TMX) as a margin-efficient hedge for USD receivables/payables mismatches.Natural-hedge netting: aligning USD invoicing with major US brewery suppliers and periodic offset under a documented master netting agreement, reserved for proven trade volumes.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium. Foreign and domestic electronic-money institutions (Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex, Corpay) operate lawfully in Canada as FINTRAC-registered money-services businesses (MSBs) under the PCMLTFA; a BC LP may use them as execution rails for low-value CAD/USD payables and receivables, often at sub-bank FX cost. Legal constraints: EMIs are not banks under the Bank Act; funds are not CDIC-insured; no lending, LC, or overdraft capacity; BC's Money Services Business Act (S.B.C. 2019, c.17), when brought into force, will add provincial registration; account freezes at KYC refresh are common where the LP has non-resident UBOs. Strategic conclusion: use a Schedule I bank as the primary treasury spine and a registered EMI only as a secondary, ring-fenced clearing rail.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (combined federal 15% + British Columbia provincial 12% general corporate rate, applicable to corporate partners on their allocated LP income)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax applies to dividends paid by a Canadian corporation. As an LP, dividends are not paid; repatriation of profits to a non-resident corporate partner occurs via income allocation and is instead subject to the Part XIV branch tax of 25% on the partner's after-tax Canadian-source business income. Treaty reductions may lower the branch tax to 5% or 15% depending on residence and treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (aggregate fair market value of all reportable non-arm's length transactions with non-residents, triggering mandatory Form T106 and contemporaneous Local File/Master File documentation under subsection 247(4) of the Income Tax Act)

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime applicable to partnerships. However, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act requires genuine economic substance. The LP must possess physical premises, staff, operational decision-making, and commercial risk in BC. A shell LP without substance risks reassessment under GAAR, imposition of a 50% penalty on avoided tax, and denial of treaty benefits for the non-resident partner.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Criminal fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil reassessment with gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner are personally liable for the partnership's tax debts under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act; the CRA may seek civil restitution and travel restrictions through court orders for non-compliance.
Shell Company or Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment disallowing the profit allocation; 50% penalty on the tax avoided; possible dissolution of the LP under the British Columbia Limited Partnership Act for acting contrary to public policy.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner may be held jointly and severally liable for the penalty equal to 50% of the tax avoided; they may also be barred from acting as directors under the Canada Business Corporations Act.
Money Laundering and Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative penalties up to CAD 500,000 for corporations and CAD 100,000 for individuals per violation; criminal money laundering offences carry up to 10 years imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors are subject to financial penalties, prohibitions on managing financial services, and freezing orders on personal assets; persistent violations may trigger passport flags and prosecution.
Transfer Pricing and Withholding Tax Violations

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act. For withholding failures, the payer is liable for the full amount that should have been withheld, plus interest and penalties; criminal prosecution applies if committed with intent to evade.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted Part XIII withholding tax; personal bank accounts may be garnished and the CRA may obtain a court order restricting travel for directors of the general partner.
