Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/business.html); British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch — eTaxBC (https://etax.gov.bc.ca/)

E-government filing portal

For a British Columbia limited partnership (LP) with online retail business: (1) apply to CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN), which serves as the Canadian tax identification number, by filing Form RC1 or using Business Registration Online; select 'Partnership' as entity type and 'GST/HST' as required account. (2) A non-resident partner or startup with no Canadian address may register through BRO with foreign residency information and a care-of address for service; the CRA may assign a BN and a GST/HST account (RT) without requiring a local physical establishment, but a Canadian representative for service of notices is recommended. (3) After receiving the BN, register for the BC PST vendor number, which is the same 9-digit BN plus a PST program suffix, in eTaxBC; if eTaxBC rejects a foreign mailing address, submit the BC Regional PST Registration form (FIN-140) with copies of the partnership agreement and identification. (4) If the LP directly imports or exports goods, it must also obtain a CBSA RM (import/export) account from the Canada Border Services Agency. (5) For e-commerce payment processors such as Stripe/PayPal, evidence of the BN/GST-HST registration is generally required to verify VAT/TIN in Canada.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Canada. A BC LP is per se fiscally transparent for income tax: the partnership files an information return (T5013) but each partner is taxed on the allocated share (Income Tax Act s. 96). For GST/HST purposes, the partnership is a separate legal person and must register in its own name; the partners may elect one individual member to remit and file all GST/HST returns as a designated member under Excise Tax Act s. 273 and associated CRA forms. A partnership may also elect, in eligible circumstances, to adopt a non-calendar fiscal period for its information return. Canada has no elective hybrid-mismatch regime; the Income Tax Act anti-hybrid provisions apply mandatorily to disallow hybrid-structured deductions, so the LP should rely on transactional structure rather than tax election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD $30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters ending in the current quarter; once exceeded, registration is mandatory. BC PST: no monetary threshold; any vendor making taxable sales of tangible personal property or taxable services in British Columbia, including remote/online vendors selling to BC consumers, must register and remit PST unless an exemption applies (e.g., occasional sales).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate supplies of outdoor camping equipment sold for export, provided the property is exported as part of the supply—either physically delivered by the vendor to a foreign destination, placed in the hands of a carrier for export, or exported by the recipient under a sale conditioned on export within a reasonable period. Services ancillary to exported goods, such as freight and handling, and services supplied to non-residents for use wholly outside Canada also qualify for zero-rating (Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V). No PST is chargeable when the vendor ships goods out of British Columbia or the consumer takes delivery outside BC at the time of sale; the exemption is documented by shipment to a foreign address or a signed export declaration. Zero-rating generates input tax credits for GST paid on procurement, preserving a refund mechanism for the LP's export business.

  • CBSA Form B13 Canada Customs Export Declaration (required when prescribed value or regulated goods threshold is met)
  • Commercial invoice and international purchase order evidencing a sale to a non-resident buyer at an export address
  • Original Bill of Lading (ocean bill, air waybill, or truck carrier certificate / CMR document)
  • Packing list and Incoterms export documents evidencing physical removal from Canada
  • Bank wire receipt, currency remittance confirmation, or export payment reconciliation ledger (inward-remittance-equivalent evidence of proceeds)

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API tax-stamping, or government-side invoice clearance. Electronic invoices are permitted for GST/HST and PST so long as they contain required information and are retained in a secure, accessible format. GST/HST returns are transmitted using CRA's GST/HST NETFILE; PST returns and payments are filed via eTaxBC. No federal PEPPOL or clearance obligation applies to BC businesses in the current fiscal year.

For fraudulent or negligent zero-rating claims, CRA may reassess within 4 years (unlimited for misrepresentation or fraud), claw back the zero-rating, and assess the full GST that would have applied had the goods been taxable domestically, plus interest. The Excise Tax Act imposes a penalty of the greater of CAD $250 and 25% of the understated net tax for false statements; the Income Tax Act may impose a 50% gross-negligence penalty if the same documentation affects partners' income allocations. Knowingly forging export documents can lead to criminal prosecution under sections 327 (ETA) and 239 (ITA), fines of up to 200% of the evaded amount, and imprisonment. The BC Ministry of Finance may additionally recover uncollected PST, apply civil penalties under the PST Act, and cancel the vendor's PST registration if export substantiation is deficient.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The former Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and neither the Currency Act nor the Bank of Canada Act imposes exchange controls. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely and transfers of funds into and out of Canada for trade, capital injections, and profit remission are unrestricted. Profit remittance barrier is not FX-related: distributions of Canadian-source net income to non-resident limited partners in a BC LP may attract Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory, treaty-reduced), while returns of capital are not subject to withholding. Non-resident ownership is permissible and does not require ministerial approval for an e-commerce retail operation below Investment Canada Act review thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender rules. Canada does not impose a statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. All export and e-commerce receipts may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency or CAD accounts, converted at the exporter's discretion, and used freely for working capital, refinancing, or repatriation. For clarity, outdoor equipment retailers selling remotely into Canada should distinguish GST/HST collectible from the revenue stream but face no FX surrender obligation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Bank supervision is conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering oversight is enforced by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium with legal caveats. Offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., Airwallex, Wise Business, Revolut Business) may offer multi-currency rails and faster onboarding but do not themselves provide a Canadian deposit-taking licence. If the EMI routes funds through a Schedule I bank partner, the BC LP should verify that the final CAD settlement account carries a valid Canadian institution number, transit number, and the LP's legal name; otherwise the LP risks operating outside the clearing system. A pure offshore EMI arrangement is not recommended as the primary treasury rail; it may be used only as a supplementary foreign-exchange sub-ledger. The LP must also confirm the EMI's FINTRAC registration as a money services business (MSB) where services are provided into Canada.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections should be routed by SWIFT MT103 from the offshore parent treasury to the LP's CAD or USD account at a Schedule I bank. Remittance instructions must identify the beneficiary as the BC limited partnership with its full legal name, the account number, the bank's SWIFT/BIC, and the reference 'Capital Contribution – [Capital Call No.] [Partner Name]'. Include a clear purpose-of-payment narrative in the wire text (e.g., 'non-resident direct investment, capital contribution to BC limited partnership'); engage the receipt bank's compliance desk in advance if the amount exceeds CAD 100,000. For amounts above CAD 1,000,000 or involving multiple jurisdictions, a pre-onboarding beneficial-ownership disclosure and source-of-funds confirmation should be provided to the bank to avoid a transaction hold. Canada applies the FATF Travel Rule through FINTRAC reporting; the remitter should transmit full originator and beneficiary data (LEI where available) to comply with Payment Card and EFT reporting thresholds of CAD 10,000 or more.Supplier and trade settlements should be executed via: (1) SWIFT MT103 for standard supplier payments in USD, EUR, or CNY, cleared through the bank's correspondent network; (2) Commercial letters of credit (UCP 600 rules) issued by the Schedule I bank for large purchase orders of camping equipment (e.g., containers exceeding USD 50,000) with full compliance with UCP 600 and eUCP for electronic presentation; (3) domestic CAD clearing via Lynx (RTGS, high value) or ACSS (Automated Clearing Settlement System) for Canadian GST/HST, payroll, and 3PL payments; (4) merchant-batch settlement from Stripe and PayPal directly to the operational account. Clearing of USD transactions may be completed through the bank's US branch or correspondent (e.g., RBC Bank, TD Bank US, BMO Harris), enabling same-day ACH and Fedwire settlement for US-origin marketplace payouts. Outbound remittances to supplier banks must include the beneficiary's IBAN/correspondent routing, the underlying purchase contract number, and a purpose code consistent with the commercial invoice; CAD payments are not subject to exchange-control approval.FX forward contracts (deliverable CAD/USD, EUR/CAD, CNY/CAD) up to 12 months tenor with a Schedule I bank under an ISDA/CSA governing agreement, used exclusively for hedging underlying purchase orders and settlement exposure.Cross-currency swaps (CAD/USD and CAD/EUR) for longer-dated procurement contracts and capital-reduction planning, requiring ISDA 2002 Master Agreement and credit support annex.Vanilla FX options (CAD call/put) to protect discrete cash flows where the supplier quotes in a foreign currency.Spot FX trades (T+2) for routine monthly conversion of USD revenue into CAD operating liquidity.NDFs are not relevant or permissible for CAD because CAD is freely deliverable; NDFs may only be considered for settlement of residual non-convertible currency payables (e.g., certain APAC supplier balances), and even then they should be avoided in favour of onshore deliverable forwards.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent; no entity-level Canadian income tax applies. Income is allocated to partners. Benchmark combined federal/BC general corporate rate is 27% (federal 15% plus BC 12%); the combined federal/BC small business rate is 10% for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations (federal 9% plus BC 1%). Non-resident partners' allocated Canadian-source business income is subject to 25% Part XIII withholding unless reduced by an income tax treaty.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Dividends are not a typical distribution mechanism for a Limited Partnership. Instead, Canadian-source income allocated to non-resident partners is subject to Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory 25% rate under ITA s. 212(1)(c) read with s. 215. Treaty rates: nil or reduced where the partner has no Canadian permanent establishment under the Canada-US business-profits article; the US treaty dividend rates of 5% (10%+ corporate shareholder) and 15% (portfolio) become relevant only if the LP is reorganized into a Canadian corporation.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required under ITA s. 247(4) where a Canadian taxpayer participates in non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with a foreign affiliate and the aggregate fair market value of those transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in the fiscal year; the accompanying T106 Information Return must be filed for such transactions; Country-by-Country reporting is triggered only where the multinational group has consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: British Columbia does not apply a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Instead, Canada relies on the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245) and statutory 'substance-over-form' principles: a transaction or series of transactions lacking genuine economic substance and carried out primarily to obtain a tax benefit may be re-characterized by the CRA. A purported LP that performs no real commercial activity in BC (no inventory, no employees, no decision-making, no warehouse or fulfillment process) may be disregarded as a shell, with income reattributed to a Canadian deemed permanent establishment and full Canadian corporate tax exposure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion — deliberate misrepresentation of revenue

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under ITA s. 239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment for up to 5 years (indictable offence) or up to 2 years (summary conviction); civil gross-negligence penalty under s. 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax, plus daily compound arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the corporate general partner can be assessed personally under ITA s. 227.1 for unpaid withholding taxes; the CRA may pierce the LP veil where the partnership is a facade used to conceal taxable income; willful non-payment can trigger B-18 travel restriction certificates and criminal restitution orders; personal bankruptcy does not discharge penalty liabilities for fraudulent misrepresentation.
Shell company arrangement and GAAR abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA applies the GAAR at ITA s. 245 to deny the tax benefit, recompute tax liability on a substance basis, and impose a penalty of 15% of the tax benefit (increased to 25% for transactions entered into after 2024 under the Budget 2023 amendments enacted in Bill C-59); CRA may apply penalty interest and reassess beyond the normal reassessment period where misrepresentation is proven.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false declarations, authorize artificial inter-affiliate contracts, or knowingly issue misleading partnership allocations are exposed to civil restitution, director-liability assessments under s. 227.1 for unremitted amounts, and criminal exposure under s. 239 for counselling false returns.
Failure to withhold and remit Part XIII tax on non-resident partner income

Statutory sanction: Penalty under ITA s. 227(8) equal to 10% of the amount that should have been withheld and remitted, increasing to 20% for repeated failures within a five-year period, plus arrears interest on the unremitted amount; the principal tax debt remains enforceable against the partnership and the non-resident partner.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted withholding under ITA s. 227.1 unless they exercised reasonable diligence; the Canada Revenue Agency may issue director liability assessments, file garnishments, and obtain a writ of seizure against personal and family assets; willful disregard supports travel-ban applications under the Income Tax Act.
AML/FINTRAC registration and reporting failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for a corporation (first offence) and up to CAD 2,000,000 on subsequent violations; criminal fines of up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years for senior officers who knowingly contravene the Act.

Director personal liability: FINTRAC compliance orders extend to corporate officers who directed, authorized, or acquiesced in the violation; directors may be subject to criminal liability as parties to the offence; the BC Partnership Act's limited-liability shield for limited partners is lost when a partner participates in the management or control of the business, exposing that partner to the full suite of sanctions.
Transfer pricing non-compliance and documentation failure

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 247(2) permits the CRA to adjust the transfer prices to arm's-length amounts; failure to provide documentation triggers penalties under s. 247(3) calculated as CAD 500 to CAD 2,400 per month plus 10% of the net tax adjustment resulting from the price correction; gross negligence penalties under s. 163(2) may also apply where documentation is fabricated.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign financial statements containing fictitious transfer prices or who instruct the controller to suppress documentation risk civil fraud proceedings, director liability for the corporation's unpaid penalties under applicable corporate statutes, professional misconduct referrals, and, in egregious cases, criminal prosecution for obstruction of the CRA.
