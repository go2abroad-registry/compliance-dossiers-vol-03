Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal GST/HST and corporate income tax administration through Business Registration Online and My Business Account (portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); British Columbia Ministry of Finance — provincial PST and Employer Health Tax administration (portal: https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes)

E-government filing portal

A BC Business Corporation (incorporated under the Business Corporations Act (British Columbia)) must obtain a CRA 9-digit Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or Form RC1, opening program accounts RC0001 (corporate income tax), RT0001 (GST/HST) and RP0001 (payroll if applicable); register with the BC Ministry of Finance for PST through BC Registries eServices or Form FIN 416 if taxable sales exceed the small-seller threshold; and file corporate income tax returns under ITA s. 150. Foreign-controlled and non-resident-owned corporations may register directly under the CRA non-resident GST/HST program; there is no minimum paid-up capital requirement and 100% non-resident shareholding is permitted.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a Canadian-resident corporation subject to Part I corporate income tax on worldwide income, with foreign active business income sheltered through the foreign affiliate exempt-surplus and dividends-received regimes; no flow-through treatment (an SBC is not an eligible pass-through); no tax-exempt zone applies in British Columbia.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists under Canadian law. Available statutory elections include: (a) ITA s. 85 rollover election for tax-deferred asset transfers on incorporation or reorganizations; (b) CCPC status and deemed-control elections under ITA s. 256; (c) non-resident corporation elections under ITA s. 216(1) for Canadian-source property income filing and ITA s. 219(2) for branch tax computed as a deemed subsidiary; (d) Excise Tax Act s. 156 election permitting closely related corporations to file GST/HST as a single group; and (e) hybrid mismatch (BEPS Action 2) ordering rules under ITA ss. 18.4–18.7, which operate automatically with disclosure obligations rather than elective check-the-box treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST small-supplier threshold: CAD 30,000 of annual worldwide taxable supplies, including Canadian supplies; a non-resident supplier with Canadian-source taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30,000 must register. BC PST small-seller threshold: CAD 10,000 of prior 12 months' BC taxable sales; registration is required once exceeded, and suppliers of taxable software or telecommunications services must register regardless of the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: (i) goods exported by the supplier (s. 1) within 60 days of invoice, or longer where export circumstances reasonably justify it; (ii) services supplied to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST (s. 7), unless the service is rendered to an individual present in Canada or relates to real property in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed. Multilingual translation, subtitling, and media dubbing performed in BC for offshore studios and platforms qualify as zero-rated offshore services; the supplier recovers input tax credits through its GST/HST net-filing calculation and must maintain evidence of the non-resident client relationship and contractual scope.

  • Customs export declaration: Canada Border Services Agency Form B13A for goods or ACI eManifest/cargo report
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill for physical deliverables, or service delivery records, client acceptance, and proof of digital hand-off for offshore services
  • Commercial invoice showing 0% GST, scope of localization work, and non-resident client details
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank payment report (Wise B2B, Payoneer payout statements) evidencing foreign-currency settlement with the non-resident client

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, no API-based clearance platform, and no digital stamping. E-invoicing and EDI are voluntary; GST/HST invoices may be issued in electronic form if they contain the prescribed information under section 3 of the Input Tax Credit Information Regulations. All electronic records must be retained and made accessible to CRA on audit.

CRA may reassess within 4 years (indefinitely where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established), claw back denied zero-rating with compound daily interest, and impose civil penalties: failure-to-file or failure-to-remit penalties of 5% of the net tax plus 1% per month up to 17%, gross-negligence or false-statement penalties up to 25% of the net tax that would have been payable, and criminal prosecution for GST/HST evasion under section 328 of the Excise Tax Act (summary conviction: up to CAD 25,000 fine and/or 12 months' imprisonment; indictment: up to CAD 100,000 fine and/or 5 years' imprisonment), with parallel exposure under ITA section 239 for income-tax fraud. Inadequate export documentation results in reversal of the zero-rating to 5% GST, denial of input tax credits, and interest and penalties running from the original filing date.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined Federal (15% net) and British Columbia provincial (12%) general corporate income tax rate; the gross Federal rate is 38% reduced by the 10% provincial abatement and the 13% general rate reduction (net 15%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) on dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File, Master File, and Country-by-Country Report where applicable) must be prepared and maintained if the aggregate fair-market-value of cross-border transactions with non-resident related parties in a taxation year exceeds CAD 1,000,000 per transaction category; documentation is also required for all significant transactions regardless of volume under CRA guidance IC-87-2R. CbC reporting threshold is CAD 750 million consolidated group revenue.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to OFC structures, but the Income Tax Act, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR, section 245), the 'substance over form' doctrine, and CRA administrative policies require that a corporate entity have real economic substance (officers, premises, business purpose, administrative functions, and risk-bearing capacity). A BC-registered multilingual localization company that is merely a conduit, lacking employees, premises, or management decisions, will be challenged as having no genuine business purpose; the CRA may recharacterize transactions, apply GAAR to deny deductions and treaty benefits, and impose misrepresentation penalties. For non-resident parent and related-party payments, the transfer-pricing rules (section 247) also require the transaction to be documented with a view to ensuring that the Canadian entity bears the actual economic risks it purportedly assumed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to two years; under section 327, fines of up to CAD 50,000 and/or up to two years' imprisonment. Civil reassessments and gross-negligence penalties under subsection 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax may also be imposed. Criminal liability extends to the corporation under section 380 of the Criminal Code with unlimited fines and up to 14 years' imprisonment for fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions (CPP, EI, and federal/provincial payroll taxes) under subsection 227.1(1) by way of a director's liability assessment, subject to the due-diligence defence. For evasion, directors who participated or acquiesced may be prosecuted as parties to the offence; civil restitution orders can freeze personal assets and bank accounts, and the Canada–US and Canada–BC enforcement arrangements permit travel bans and cross-border asset seizures.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may disallow all deductions attributable to the sham arrangement, reassess the taxable income at the full statutory 27% rate, impose transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the net adjustment (section 247(3)), and levy 50% gross-negligence penalties. Under GAAR, benefits realized via treaty shopping or aggressive TP planning can be wholly eliminated to the extent that the principal purpose is misuse or abuse of the Act. Non-compliance with the corporate registry (BC Business Corporations Act) is punishable by fines of up to CAD 50,000 against the corporation and CAD 10,000 against officers.

Director personal liability: Directors may be Personally assessed under section 247(4) for the 10% transfer-pricing penalty if they knowingly participated in the mispricing. In a sham finding, the corporate veil can be pierced when the corporation is a mere agent or devise; directors who authorize the payment of undisclosed funds may be liable for breach of fiduciary duty, equitable compensation, and in egregious cases, criminal fraud prosecutions with imprisonment.
Money Laundering / Anti-Money Laundering (AML)

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act imposes administrative monetary penalties on corporations: up to CAD 250,000 for failure to comply with record-keeping, verification, and reporting obligations; criminal penalties include imprisonment for up to five years and a fine at the court's discretion. Seizure of assets, freezing of bank/payment-rail accounts, and mandatory forfeiture are ancillary consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who manage or participate in a transaction reasonably known to involve unlawful proceeds are liable to criminal prosecution as principals or conspirators. Personal penalties include fines of up to CAD 100,000 per offence and up to five years' imprisonment. Civil suits by regulatory bodies may impose disgorgement, accountfreezes, and travel restrictions pending trial, and a criminal conviction triggers immigration and cross-border mobility bans under Canadian immigration law.
