Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — central administrator of the Business Number (BN), GST/HST accounts, and corporate income tax accounts; primary portal: My Business Account / GST/HST NETFILE (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html). British Columbia imposes a separate 7% Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administered by the BC Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch, through the eTaxBC portal; BC PST is duplicative to GST and applies to goods and specified PST-liable services, not to financial factoring services.

E-government filing portal

A federal or BC-registered Business Corporation (CA_CORP) obtains a 9-digit CRA Business Number automatically upon incorporation through the corporate registry (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada federal registry or BC Corporate Registry), with CRA program accounts assigned for corporate income tax (RT) and GST/HST (RP); the corporation must update active program accounts via Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or CRA Business Registration Online. A foreign non-resident entity not incorporated in Canada must file Form RC171 (Non-Resident Registration and Application for a Business Number) with the CRA International and Large Business Directorate, attaching certified foreign incorporation documents, evidence of a Canadian permanent establishment or other carrying-on-business indicators, and a completed statement of Canadian business activity; the CRA then issues a non-resident Business Number enabling T2 corporate income tax filing and GST/HST registration. All GST/HST registrants must designate a Canadian contact address or authorized representative for CRA correspondence.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (ITA) and the Excise Tax Act (ETA), with no US-style check-the-box flow-through for corporations. The trade-finance factoring corporation is resident in Canada (incorporated in Canada, central management and control in British Columbia) and is taxed at the corporate level on worldwide income, with foreign tax credits available for foreign-source income (ITA s. 113/126). It is a 'financial institution' under ETA s. 149(1) and, if its principal business is providing financial services and it has a permanent establishment in Canada, a mandatorily registered 'selected financial institution' (SFI) for GST/HST purposes; its factoring and reverse-factoring services are exempt financial supplies under ETA Schedule V, Part VII and the financial-services definition in ETA s. 123(1). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (ITA) and the Excise Tax Act (ETA), with no US-style check-the-box flow-through for corporations. The trade-finance factoring corporation is resident in Canada (incorporated in Canada, central management and control in British Columbia) and is taxed at the corporate level on worldwide income, with foreign tax credits available for foreign-source income (ITA s. 113/126). It is a 'financial institution' under ETA s. 149(1) and, if its principal business is providing financial services and it has a permanent establishment in Canada, a mandatorily registered 'selected financial institution' (SFI) for GST/HST purposes; its factoring and reverse-factoring services are exempt financial supplies under ETA Schedule V, Part VII and the financial-services definition in ETA s. 123(1). Territorial scope: BC and federal corporate income tax apply to the full taxable income of the resident corporation (BC general corporate rate approximately 12%; federal general rate 15%; combined federal/BC small business rates approximately 9% federal and 2% provincial on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income).

Available election forms

Canada does not provide check-the-box entity classification elections. Applicable statutory elections include: (i) ETA ss. 156/157 elections for closely related registrants to simplify net tax (no-tax or single-group treatment) on intercompany supplies; (ii) ETA s. 225.2 special attribution method (SAM) election for selected financial institutions, with optional simplified net-tax methods for smaller financial institutions; (iii) ITA s. 216(1) election for non-residents to report Canadian-source rental/timber property income on a net basis; (iv) ITA s. 85 rollover election on transfers of eligible property to a corporation; (v) ITA s. 93(1) election for non-resident shareholders of foreign affiliates; and (vi) hybrid-mismatch elections under ITA s. 18.2(5) (and related hybrid/foreign-tax-credit-generator rules) to designate amounts as subject to Canadian tax so as to avoid denial or redetermination of deductions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

General GST/HST registration threshold: CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA s. 240(1)). A trade-accounts-receivable factoring corporation whose principal business is providing financial services is a financial institution and selected financial institution (SFI) under ETA ss. 149 and 240(1.1), and is mandatorily required to register for GST/HST regardless of the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, because its factoring services are exempt financial supplies; no minimum revenue registration threshold applies to SFIs. BC PST registration through eTaxBC is required only if the entity makes taxable retail sales of goods or PST-liable services in BC; pure factoring fees are not PST-liable and therefore do not trigger BC PST registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under ETA Schedule VI, Part V applies to goods exported by a registrant (property exported within 60 days of the supply and supported by prescribed proof-of-export evidence) and to certain services supplied to non-residents (Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7), such as advisory or non-financial services. However, factoring and cross-border reverse-factoring services are exempt financial services under ETA Schedule V, Part VII and ETA s. 123(1), and are NOT zero-rated irrespective of the non-resident status of the debtor or the assignor of receivables. Consequently, the trade-finance corporation cannot charge 0% GST on cross-border factoring fees and generally cannot claim input tax credits on expenses attributable to those exempt supplies (ETA s. 169(1) limitation), although the selected financial institution special attribution method may permit a limited ITC recovery based on taxable supply proportions. If the corporation separately sells goods or provides non-financial services to non-residents, such supplies may qualify for zero-rating with the standard export evidence package. There is no statutory Canadian foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark is a commercial underwriting standard for export-factoring facilities, not a CRA requirement.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) transmitted declaration) or the low-value shipment automated customs declaration
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order referencing the factored receivables and the underlying exported goods
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / truck waybill showing Canadian port of exit and final foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT confirmation evidencing collection of foreign export proceeds
  • CRA-prescribed proof-of-export documentation under ETA s. 169(2) and Schedule VI, Part V (e.g., carrier certificates, customs notations, foreign customs entry evidence)
  • Factoring and assignment agreement schedules to substantiate the exempt financial-services classification and to delineate the non-financial service fees (if any) for zero-rating

Canada does not impose any real-time fiscal clearance, government-API e-invoicing clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction-control mandate. GST/HST returns and remittances are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or the CRA GST/HST API, with ordinary statutory filing deadlines (generally one month after each reporting period). Commercial invoices for taxable supplies must be issued or received and contain prescribed information under the Excise Tax Information Regulations (ETA s. 169 / s. 169.1), including supplier registration number, transaction date, description, tax status, and net tax. Because the factoring entity makes exempt financial supplies, its invoices do not bear GST; however, as a selected financial institution it must file annual GST/HST returns using the special attribution method and maintain supporting schedules on operating and non-operating revenues. BC PST compliance is handled through eTaxBC with no real-time clearance obligation for exempt financial transactions.

CRA audit focus includes (i) disallowance of input tax credits claimed by voluntarily registered financial institutions where expenses are attributable to exempt financial services; (ii) reversal of improper export zero-rating claims when the export documentation bundle is incomplete, triggering reassessments beyond the normal four-year limitation period in cases of misrepresentation (ETA s. 298(4); ITA s. 152(4)(a)(i)); (iii) civil penalties under ETA s. 280 (greater of CAD 250 and 25% of misstated or unremitted net tax), gross-negligence penalties, and interest accruals under ETA s. 296; (iv) criminal tax-evasion exposure under ETA s. 327 and ITA s. 239, including fines up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years; and (v) for selected financial institutions, reassessment under the special attribution method with recapture of deemed taxable supplies (ETA s. 225.2) and penalties for failure to file annual GST/HST returns. Zero-rating claims unsupported by the above bundle are subject to clawback with interest and referral to the CRA Criminal Investigations Program where false or forged export documentation is involved.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada adheres to IMF Article VIII obligations and maintains full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions. There is no prior exchange-control permit, no surrender requirement, and no restriction on profit remittance. Dividends, interest, royalties, and service fees may be paid by ordinary wire transfers, subject to Canada Revenue Agency Part XIII non-resident withholding tax and any applicable Canada-U.S./Canada-foreign income tax treaty relief, plus CRS/FATCA certification. Inbound equity subscription for the BC factoring entity is freely permitted; where foreign-controlled acquisitions exceed prescribed statutory thresholds, the Investment Canada Act may impose notification/review, but this is not an FX control and would not inhibit a passive trade-finance subsidiary's capital injection.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed timeline for retaining or repatriating foreign currency export receipts. Canadian export proceeds may remain in original currency. Compliance obligations are limited to: (i) filing any applicable B13 export declaration with the Canada Border Services Agency; (ii) maintaining six-year records under the Income Tax Act; and (iii) reporting every cross-border electronic funds transfer of C$10,000 or more to FINTRAC. For the factoring entity, remittance of export-receivable proceeds to the operating account does not constitute a surrender event and is not subject to conversion.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as central monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator and bank licensor; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the AML/ATF and cross-border EFT reporting authority; Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) for retail payment service provider oversight under the Retail Payment Activities Act. Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign exchange control administration; the regime is a liberalized floating-currency system administered through banking, AML, and reporting rules.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low as a primary treasury or capital-escrow rail; Medium as a supplemental, low-value supplier-payment channel. An offshore digital EMI is not a licensed Canadian bank and cannot accept member-insured deposits or provide CDIC protection. It may conduct payment activities only to the extent it is registered with FINTRAC as a Money Service Business and, where applicable, registered with FCAC under the Retail Payment Activities Act. To preserve legal certainty, incoming capital and trade-receivables collections must not be concentrated in an offshore EMI; it should be limited to disbursement-only use to verified beneficiaries, with full SWIFT-compatible remittance data and rigorous sanctions screening.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Complex ownership chain with a foreign parent, BC holding vehicle, and nominee or trust participants.

Root cause: Complex ownership chain with a foreign parent, BC holding vehicle, and nominee or trust participants.

Defense evidence: Current Certificate of Good Standing from the BC Corporate Registry; full corporate organization chart showing every individual with 25% or more direct/indirect control; signed beneficial-ownership declaration; notarized passport copies; proof of residential address for ultimate beneficial owners; source-of-wealth statement supported by audited financial statements, bank references, tax returns, and prior business records; board resolution authorizing the account opening and capital injection; confirmation that no undisclosed nominees or trustees exist.
Factoring portfolio showing rapid assignment of high-volume receivables from multiple unrelated suppliers, raising double-pledge or money-laundering concerns.

Root cause: Factoring portfolio showing rapid assignment of high-volume receivables from multiple unrelated suppliers, raising double-pledge or money-laundering concerns.

Defense evidence: Master factoring and reverse-factoring agreements; receivables ledger with unique invoice numbers and aging schedules; signed notice of assignment and debtor acknowledgments; underlying purchase orders, contracts, bills of lading, customs export declarations, and proof of delivery; independent verification of debtor payor status; BC Personal Property Registry search report evidencing no prior competing security interests; true-sale legal opinion for each facility; internal audit trail in the treasury/ERP system.
Cross-border flows to or from jurisdictions on the FATF watchlist, Canadian Consolidated Sanctions List, or UN Security Council sanctions list.

Payments to escrow agents or third-party disbursement accounts where the named beneficiary does not match the underlying supplier invoice.

Root cause: Payments to escrow agents or third-party disbursement accounts where the named beneficiary does not match the underlying supplier invoice.

Defense evidence: Executed three-party escrow agreement identifying all roles; written escrow release instruction signed by the account party; invoice referencing the escrow beneficiary; bank-call-back verification record; assignment-of-proceeds agreement if factoring an L/C; board resolution approving third-party disbursement; proof that the third party is the supplier's designated collection agent; complete chain of remittance instructions and confirmations.
Repeated inbound remittances narrowly below the C$10,000 FINTRAC EFT reporting threshold, or same-day aggregation of related-party payments, perceived as structuring or smurfing.

Root cause: Repeated inbound remittances narrowly below the C$10,000 FINTRAC EFT reporting threshold, or same-day aggregation of related-party payments, perceived as structuring or smurfing.

Defense evidence: Automated transaction-monitoring and aggregation reports; invoice-by-invoice reconciliation proving each remittance corresponds to a discrete commercial receivable; no-splitting policy in the treasury manual; minimum invoice amount and single-payment contractual specifications; audit trail in the accounting system; management representation letter; escalation record confirming no suspicious transaction report is required unless the totality of facts indicates concealment.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound capital should route by SWIFT MT103 from the foreign parent's own maintained bank account to the BC entity's named Tier-1 Canadian account. Before the first injection, the bank must receive: the board resolution approving the capital subscription, the BC Notice of Articles, share subscription agreement, source-of-funds declaration, and beneficial-ownership schedule. The beneficiary legal name must match the registered account name exactly. Use a remittance narrative such as 'CAPITAL SUBSCRIPTION - CA_CORP - COMMON SHARES - REF [subscription date]' and avoid invoice references. Canada does not prescribe a mandatory SWIFT purpose code; the bank may require a narrative purpose and will execute currency conversion only on explicit instruction. Escrow release requires dual-approval, authenticated originator information, and a post-closing statement reconciling funds to subscribed shares.Trade disbursements to suppliers and exporters should clear via SWIFT MT103, referencing invoice, purchase order, and 'TRADE PAYMENT' in field 70. High-value domestic CAD obligations should settle through Payments Canada Lynx; USD obligations should clear through the Canadian bank's US correspondent. For documentary letters of credit, issue via SWIFT MT700 (or MT710 for deferred-payment/acceptance credit), amend via MT707, and present documents including bill of lading, commercial invoice, packing list, and certificate of inspection. Where the factoring entity finances L/C proceeds, hold an executed assignment of proceeds/forfaiting agreement, register any security interest under the BC Personal Property Security Act, and obtain bank-commercial-credit approval before releasing funds. Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined (Federal Part I corporate income tax 15% + British Columbia provincial corporate income tax 12%) for a general active-business taxpayer resident in BC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on any dividend paid by a BC-resident company to a non-resident shareholder (Income Tax Act s. 212(2)); the rate is 15% for a participating dividend paid to a preferred shareholder unless a treaty provides a lower rate.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

File no later than six months after the taxation year-end (a calendar-year-end corporation files by 30 June); the balance of tax payable is due two months after year-end (three months where the taxpayer is a CCPC eligible for the Small Business Deduction). A non-resident-owned corporation earning Canadian-source business income through a permanent establishment files a T2 within six months of that PE year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation plus Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) applies when the aggregate value of transactions with each non-resident related person exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in the taxation year (T106 due on or before the T2 filing date). A Master File and Local File are expected for multinational groups with global gross revenue above CAD 50,000,000 and cross-border intercompany financing or trade flows above CAD 1,000,000; the CRA audit manual additionally identifies thin capitalisation and financing policy of factoring vehicles as high-risk documentation areas.

Economic substance scope: No standalone Economic Substance Regulations exist in Canadian federal law; the CRA applies an OECD-aligned substance requirement through (i) Income Tax Act s. 245 general anti-avoidance rule against avoidance transactions lacking economic substance; (ii) s. 67 'reasonable in the circumstances' standard; (iii) s. 18(1)(e)/18(4) interest deductibility impediments where funding is back-to-back with a non-resident parent; and (iv) the central-management-and-control test for Canadian tax residence. For a BC factoring SPV, the CRA will examine physical premises in BC, payroll of credit and collection officers, substantive board decisions, and real assumption of default and collection risk; a shell SPV with nominal employees and automatic pass-through funding is a prime target for GAAR recharacterisation and T106 penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Manipulation of T2

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s. 239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to 2 years on summary conviction or up to 5 years on indictment. Civil gross-negligence penalty under s. 163(2): 50% of the understated tax; the CRA may reassess beyond the normal three-year period (six years where negligence is shown) and add daily interest.

Director personal liability: A director who directed, assented to, or participated in the evasion is jointly and severally liable with the corporation for the unpaid tax attributable to their involvement (s. 227.1 for unremitted employee withholdings and Part XIII tax; s. 160 for non-arm's-length property transfers). Guilty directors face personal restitution orders, criminal conviction, disqualification as officers, and CRA-initiated restriction orders or travel bans pending enforcement of the judgement.
Transfer-Pricing Mispricing / Thin Capitalisation / Repeated T106 Deficiencies

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing adjustment to the arm's-length result plus a discretionary 10% penalty on the net adjustment (s. 247(3), minimum CAD 5,000); T106 late-filing penalty of CAD 100 per day (maximum CAD 2,500); disallowance of a proportional interest deduction under the thin-capitalisation rules; and a six-year reassessment window.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the penalty amount if the corporation fails to satisfy it and the directors knowingly manoeuvred the related-party pricing (s. 227.1 covers unremitted source deductions; the CRA may proceed in Federal Court to enforce the 2:1 interest disallowance against the shareholders' guarantee). For severe deliberate mispricing, personal criminal exposure arises under s. 239(1)(d) where the director participated in a false or deceptive statement.
AML / Shell Company / FINTRAC Non-Registration

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalty up to CAD 1,000,000 per breach under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; CRA and BC Financial Services Authority administrative orders may suspend or strike the corporate registry record; corporate dissolution and asset forfeiture proceedings; in structuring cases the CRA may add a 50% civil penalty on the tax implicated plus applicable fines.

Director personal liability: Under the BC Business Corporations Act, directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's obligations arising from their knowing approval of unlawful distributions; courts will pierce the corporate veil where the SPV was a façade, imposing personal restitution to creditors, attaching personal assets via freezing orders, and imposing travel bans or confiscation orders where funds were moved offshore.
Related-Party Asset Stripping (s. 160) & Dividend Withholding Default

Statutory sanction: s. 160(1) joint and several liability imposed automatically: the CRA may garnishee receivables without judicial action and register a lien on the transferee's assets for the full tax liability up to the transferred fair-market value; unpaid Part XIII withholdings incur a 10% late-remittance penalty plus daily interest, and a 10% additional penalty where the payer executed false certificates under the Canada-US treaty.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorised a dividend without ensuring statutory withholding are jointly and severally liable for the entire unremitted amount under s. 227.1 (with the due-diligence defence available only if the director exercised the care of a reasonably prudent person); CRA may seek civil restitution, property liens, and a court-imposed export travel ban while a criminal tax-evasion inquiry is outstanding.
