Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA) — federal corporate income tax, Part I tax, GST/HST, payroll and excise tax; CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html). For British Columbia: BC Ministry of Finance / BC Taxpayer Gateway for provincial income tax, PST, employer health tax and other BC taxes.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated EMI that carries on business in BC must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from CRA. The standard pathway is: (1) incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or BC Business Corporations Act if not already incorporated; (2) file Form RC1 with CRA to open program accounts (RC — corporate income tax; RT — GST/HST; RP — payroll); (3) provide constitutive documents, beneficial-ownership details and a BC registered office; (4) if a non-resident corporation carries on business in Canada, complete CRA non-resident registration requirements and consider appointment of a Canadian agent for service; (5) obtain any FINTRAC money services business (MSB) registration separately. The BN is the statutory TIN; there is no separate VAT ID, but the RT program account functions as the GST/HST registration number.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian-incorporated corporation is a separate taxpayer; a resident corporation is taxed on worldwide income under Income Tax Act Part I and BC corporate income tax is allocated to the BC permanent establishment. It is not a pass-through entity and has no separate territorial exemption for foreign branch income (foreign tax credits apply).

Available election forms

Not applicable. Canada does not use the U.S. check-the-box election; CRA classifies an entity by its actual legal form and the Income Tax Act. For hybrid mismatch arrangements, s. 18.2 disallows deductions unless a corresponding inclusion is made, and s. 18.3 deals with imported losses; there is no elective 'tax transparency' override for a corporation. Provincial filing elections concern allocation of income and capital among provinces.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (annual worldwide taxable supplies, GST/HST small-supplier threshold). For BC PST, there is no analogous small-supplier threshold; registration is required if the person makes taxable sales in BC, although exempt financial services do not require PST registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act s. 123 and Schedule VI, zero-rating is available only if the supply is listed in Schedule VI. For physical goods exported from Canada: (i) the supplier or an agent exports the goods in the course of a continuous journey to a destination outside Canada; (ii) title and possession pass to a non-resident outside Canada; (iii) the goods are exported within the time and conditions in CRA administrative policy; and (iv) the supplier retains contemporaneous export evidence. For services supplied to non-residents under Part VI: (v) the service is supplied to a non-resident who is not registered under GST/HST; (vi) the service is not in respect of real property located in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of supply, and is not a personal service reasonably expected to be performed in Canada. Financial services rendered by an EMI are generally exempt under Schedule V, Part VII rather than zero-rated; where a financial service qualifies as an exported financial service in a zero-rated category, the same evidentiary criteria apply.

  • Customs Export Declaration / CBSA Electronic Export Report (e.g., CADEX or export declaration filed through a customs broker)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill or other contract of carriage showing the goods and destination outside Canada
  • Commercial invoice and packing list with the identity and address of the non-resident purchaser
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT MT103/MT202 payment evidence establishing that settlement came from outside Canada
  • Proof of physical export: customs broker confirmation, marine/air freight manifest, foreign port handling/warehouse receipt
  • GST/HST Schedule VI analysis memo and CRA export documentation record (kept with the entity's books for six years)

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, no mandatory e-invoice stamping, and no government API pre-clearance for GST/HST. CRA requires invoices and supporting records to be kept in an accessible format; businesses may voluntarily use PEPPOL/PDF/electronic data interchange. If a large taxpayer or a supplier to a provincial agency uses electronic invoicing, that is contractual, not tax-mandated. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically in certain cases based on filing thresholds.

CRA can reassess zero-rated supplies within four years (unlimited in cases of misrepresentation, neglect or fraud) if export documentation is incomplete; the resulting GST/HST becomes payable with arrears interest and, in strict liability cases, penalties. Excise Tax Act s. 280.1 imposes a gross-negligence penalty of up to 50% of the false refund/rebate claimed; s. 281 et seq. permits civil recovery and prosecution. Individuals can face additional criminal fraud penalties under the Criminal Code, and corporate directors/officers can face liability for withheld amounts under Excise Tax Act s. 323. CRA's unsubstantiated-export audits frequently revoke zero-rating and deny input tax credits retrospectively, creating a full cash clawback with interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a floating-rate, fully convertible currency regime compliant with IMF Article VIII; neither current-account nor capital-account transactions require FX approval or surrender authorization, and profit remittance faces no administrative currency barrier. Outbound dividend frictions are exclusively tax-driven under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25% non-resident withholding tax, treaty-reduced) and corporate-law-driven under the solvency test provisions of the British Columbia Business Corporations Act. A BC-incorporated electronic money institution may freely denominate settlement positions in CAD, USD, EUR, or GBP without Bank of Canada conversion authorization; however, FINTRAC cross-border electronic funds transfer reporting applies to international wires of CAD 10,000 or more, and retail payment funds must be safeguarded in accordance with RPAA and applicable BC consumer-protection statutes.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory repatriation timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds, cross-border service receipts, or digital wallet inflow balances; the mandatory conversion ratio is 0% and no surrender certificate or central bank filing is required. Export-oriented EMIs may retain foreign currency treasury balances offshore indefinitely, subject only to Income Tax Act functional-currency translation rules, Part XIII source-withholding implications on foreign payments, and Statistics Canada International Survey of Direct Investment reporting thresholds where inward or outward direct investment positions are established.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting jointly with the Department of Finance Canada on exchange-control policy and as supervisory authority for retail payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); systemic clearing oversight is delegated to Payments Canada for LVTS, ACSS, and the staged launch of the Real-Time Rail.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Conditioned-High when the EMI is structured through the licensed BC-incorporated corporation with a resident Canadian chief compliance officer, FINTRAC Money Services Business registration, and Bank of Canada RPAA payment service provider registration; Conditioned-Low for a purely offshore digital EMI with no Canadian nexus. Legal constraints: an offshore digital EMI directing services to Canadian residents or maintaining a place of business in Canada must register as a foreign MSB with FINTRAC; deposit-taking without a Bank Act banking licence is prohibited; restricted financial terms such as bank, trust, and insurance are protected under the Bank Act; virtual IBAN issuance must be executed through a sponsor-bank sub-IBAN or segregated account tenancy without marketing the EMI as a chartered bank; PIPEDA applies to cross-border processing of Canadian personal information; and the Bank of Canada superscription of the EMI under RPAA governs safeguarding, end-user disclosure, incident response, and clearing-rail participation conditions.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal and 12% British Columbia. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access an 11% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable federal and British Columbia business limits; financial institutions and certain specified corporations are excluded from relevant preferential treatment.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Under the Canada-China income tax treaty, dividends are generally limited to 10% where the beneficial owner is a company that owns at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other cases. The precise rate depends on treaty eligibility, beneficial ownership, voting rights, and anti-abuse rules.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of each taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for certain Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting statutory conditions. Tax installments are generally due monthly; eligible corporations may pay quarterly.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Electronic filing is generally mandatory for corporations, subject to statutory exceptions. The taxation-year return remains due six months after year-end regardless of the corporation's installment profile.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold that eliminates the arm's-length requirement or universally triggers a Canadian Local File or Master File in the OECD terminology. Contemporaneous documentation is required where non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents are subject to section 247 of the Income Tax Act. Form T106 reporting generally applies where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold. CRA may demand records, and documentation should normally be prepared by the filing deadline.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone OECD-style Economic Substance Regulations regime applicable to all companies. Substance is assessed through the transfer-pricing arm's-length principle, treaty residence and beneficial-ownership analysis, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, foreign-affiliate rules, thin-capitalization rules, interest-limitation rules, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and factual or statutory residence. A Canadian fintech should maintain Canadian decision-making, risk control, personnel, technology oversight, treasury governance, customer and transaction records, and commercially supportable intercompany arrangements. Contractual labels alone do not establish substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, interest, civil penalties, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and criminal prosecution. Tax evasion under the Income Tax Act may result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years; other offences may carry higher statutory penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution, personal civil penalties, liability for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under statutory director-liability provisions, restitution, asset seizure, prohibition orders, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions may arise through arrest warrants, bail conditions, immigration consequences, or proceeds-of-crime proceedings, but are not an automatic tax penalty.
Unremitted Payroll, Part XIII, or GST/HST Amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unpaid amount, prescribed interest, late-remittance penalties, failure-to-deduct penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and potential criminal charges for wilful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under applicable statutory provisions where the corporation failed to remit and the statutory due-diligence defense is unavailable. Personal assets may be pursued; imprisonment is possible for criminal conduct.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary transfer-pricing adjustment, denial or reduction of deductions, withholding-tax exposure, interest, penalty of up to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, and potential secondary tax consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil and criminal exposure for knowingly participating in false records or evasion. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessment does not automatically create personal liability, but statutory source-deduction liabilities and fraud-based claims can.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding at 25%, reassessment, interest, penalties, GAAR application, and possible reporting or promoter penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur liability for knowing misrepresentations, withheld-tax failures, and fraud. Criminal sanctions may apply where the arrangement involves wilful tax evasion.
Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, publication of violations, criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, asset seizure or forfeiture, and sanctions-related penalties under Canadian law. Serious money-laundering offences can carry imprisonment of up to ten years; terrorist-financing offences can carry imprisonment of up to ten years or life imprisonment depending on the offence.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, compliance officers, and responsible managers may face personal criminal liability, fines, forfeiture, director or officer disqualification consequences, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions can result from warrants, bail, sanctions, or forfeiture proceedings.
Unlicensed Payment or Retail Payment Activity

Statutory sanction: Registration refusal or revocation, compliance orders, administrative monetary penalties, prohibition from performing retail payment activities, and prosecution for contraventions. Additional licensing exposure may arise under provincial money-services-business rules and federal financial-sector legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be liable for knowing authorization of prohibited conduct, false filings, obstruction, or AML failures. Civil veil piercing is exceptional, but statutory offences and fraud can produce personal fines, restitution, forfeiture, and imprisonment.
Corporate Insolvency and Asset-Mixing

Statutory sanction: Civil recovery, transaction reversal, oppression or breach-of-duty claims, creditor remedies, bankruptcy proceedings, regulatory intervention, and possible criminal charges for fraud or fraudulent conveyance.

Director personal liability: Directors may face claims for breach of fiduciary duty, oppression, negligence, fraudulent preference, unlawful distributions, and statutory director liability. Personal restitution, asset seizure, bankruptcy restrictions, and imprisonment may follow proven fraud.
Books, Records, and Audit Obstruction

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-provide-information penalties, compliance orders, adverse reassessments, document-preservation sanctions, administrative penalties, and criminal prosecution for obstruction or destruction of evidence.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may be personally prosecuted and fined; imprisonment is possible for obstruction, perjury, destruction of evidence, or related tax and AML offences.
Prohibited Corporate Distributions and Capital Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Repayment orders, creditor claims, tax reassessment, denial of deductions, withholding-tax exposure, regulatory sanctions, and possible oppression or fraudulent-transfer remedies.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize unlawful distributions may be jointly liable to restore amounts and may face breach-of-duty claims, personal restitution, asset seizure, and criminal sanctions where fraud or intentional misconduct is established.
