For a non-resident limited partnership carrying on business in Canada, the partnership and each required partner must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) through the CRA Business Registration Online service. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box election for income tax purposes; a limited partnership is fiscally transparent by statutory rule. For U.S. federal tax purposes only, an eligible Canadian LP may file U.S. Form 8832 for check-the-box classification. For GST/HST purposes, the partnership may elect to have a non-resident partner accounted for under simplified registration or use the no-revenue claim election where applicable, but entity-level taxation is not available.

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under section 23 of the Excise Tax Act for supplies of tangible personal property (including wine and spirits) that are exported from Canada by the supplier or delivered outside Canada, provided the supplier obtains and retains documentary evidence of export within prescribed time limits. For bonded-warehouse wholesale trade, zero-rating also applies where the spirits are removed from a customs bonded warehouse for export, the customs entry is properly coded as an export, and the goods are shipped to a destination outside Canada. Services rendered in respect of exported goods or services supplied to non-residents for use wholly outside Canada may also qualify for zero-rating.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA export reporting code and control number)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or truck waybill evidencing dispatch from Canada)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank-verified evidence of consideration received from the foreign buyer)
  • Bonded warehouse removal/transfer records showing the goods left the licensed facility in export status

No real-time fiscal clearance, API-based government clearance, or digital stamping regime is mandated under Canadian GST/HST law. Businesses must issue and retain valid invoices or equivalent documents, and electronic invoicing (EDI or PDF-based) is permitted. CBSA and CRA may require digital transmission of customs declaration data for bonded-warehouse removals and exports through the Canadian Integrated Import Declaration / Export Reporting system.

The CRA may reassess and claw back zero-rated status where documentary evidence is incomplete or where the supplier fails to prove export. Penalties include: income tax/GST reassessment with interest, penalties for false statements and omissions under the Excise Tax Act, gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of the net understated tax (for 2024), prosecution for wilful evasion and fraudulent zero-rating claims, seizure of the bonded-warehouse license, and director/partner liability for tax and penalties. For customs excise matters, the Excise Act, 2001 imposes additional fines up to twice the amount of excise duty evaded, with potential imprisonment on indictment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility under IMF Article VIII. Both current account and capital account transactions are freely convertible; there is no exchange-control licensing, no parallel currency market, and no restriction on profit remittance or non-resident partner distributions. Canadian-resident LPs may contract and settle in CAD, USD, EUR, GBP, and any freely convertible currency. The only substantive hurdles are administrative: Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on income distributions (generally 25%, reduced by tax treaty), source-of-funds documentation for large wires, and FINTRAC reporting in increments above CAD 10,000. Capital contributions may enter Canada without prior central bank approval, but the receiving bank must conduct a source-of-funds review and may apply enhanced due diligence because the LP is in the medium-risk bonded wine and spirits sector.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export proceeds surrender obligation, no statutory conversion timeline, and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters may retain and use 100% of foreign-currency export proceeds indefinitely. For bonded wine/spirits sales, the exporter must declare proceeds through customs clearance records using Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) documentation, and any export payment routed through a Canadian financial institution in excess of CAD 10,000 requires an EFT report to FINTRAC. There is no export exchange certificate requirement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and Global Affairs Canada - Trade Controls Bureau.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low-to-Medium. Fintech EMIs such as Wise, Revolut Business, and Payoneer can be used as secondary settlement channels for trade disbursements, but they cannot serve as the primary operating bank for a BC limited partnership engaged in bonded wine and spirits wholesale trade. The bonded warehouse regime and excise duty deferral require a Canadian-regulated bank account capable of paying CBSA, Canada Revenue Agency, and provincial liquor authorities directly. Non-bank EMIs are not authorised to provide Canadian deposit-taking services under the Bank Act; if the EMI acts as a money services business providing FX conversion, it must be registered with FINTRAC. Most Canadian Schedule I banks will not fund a fully offshore EMI structure as the principal treasury node, and any EMI that intermediates high-value spirits settlements will be treated as an enhanced due diligence counterparty.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant capital injections should be routed through SWIFT MT103 or ISO 20022 pacs.009/pacs.008 with a clear purpose code: 'INTC' for intra-group equity contribution or 'TRAD' for a shareholder loan financing bonded inventory. The receiving rail for CAD is Lynx RTGS, and for USD it is Fedwire/CHIPS through the bank's New York correspondent; EUR/GBP flows arrive via TARGET2/SEPA or CHAPS. The bank will require a pre-funding file containing the executed LP agreement, capital call resolution, partnership registry search, good standing certificate, wire instruction summary, and a FINTRAC-compliant EFT report if the amount exceeds CAD 10,000. A source-of-funds letter is mandatory for any capital injection exceeding CAD 1 million. No central bank approval is required, but OSFI-regulated banks will not release escrowed capital until the partnership's ownership structure and the origin of funds are fully verified.For bonded trade settlements, the primary rail is SWIFT MT103 for high-value supplier payments, accompanied by commercial bank draft instructions. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27% (15% federal general corporate rate + 12% British Columbia provincial general corporate rate). A Canadian Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent; the 27% headline applies to corporate general and limited partners on their allocable share of partnership business income, unless the partner is eligible for a preferential rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.), as amended, on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. An LP is fiscally transparent; partnership income allocations and distributions to non-resident partners are not subject to withholding as dividends. If the LP is subsequently reorganized into a Canadian corporation, dividends paid to non-resident shareholders (including the LP's foreign partners) will attract the 25% statutory rate, reduced by treaty to commonly 15% (non-corporate) or 5% (corporate shareholder owning at least 10% voting shares).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 return is due no later than March 31 of the year following the fiscal period-end for a fiscal period ending December 31; for a non-calendar fiscal period, the return is due no later than five months after the end of the fiscal period, whichever is later.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 is due no later than six months after the end of the corporation's taxation year; for a December 31 year-end, the return is due by June 30. The balance of tax payable is due within two months after year-end for most corporations, or within three months for Canadian-controlled private corporations eligible for the small business deduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000. A Canadian taxpayer is required to file Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length's International Transactions) and maintain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation when the aggregate value of transactions or series of transactions with each non-resident non-arm's length person exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. The documentation must present the taxpayer's transfer pricing methodology, comparable searches, and arm's length conclusion and must be prepared before the relevant T2 due date.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulation; instead, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under s. 245, the sham transaction doctrine, and the Supreme Court's substance-over-form jurisprudence apply. The BC corporate general partner must demonstrate real economic substance in British Columbia: a physical office, local employees, substantive purchasing, storage, and distribution decisions, and an active bank account. Lack of substance in a related-party bonded trade structure will trigger GAAR reassessments, transfer pricing adjustments, and potentially criminal tax evasion charges if documentation is fabricated. Regulatory substance is also required under the Excise Act, 2001 and BC Liquor Control and Licensing Act for bonded storage.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Income Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under s. 239 of the Income Tax Act: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years. The CRA may assess a civil gross negligence penalty under s. 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax; the CRA may also seize business records and inventory under s. 231.1 and apply an additional 10% late-filing penalty for repeated failures.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 for unpaid amounts withheld or deducted (e.g., excise duties collected and source deductions remitted). A director who authorized, accepted, or acquiesced in the evasion is personally subject to prosecution, criminal fines, travel bans, and forfeiture orders; the BC Business Corporations Act permits lifting the corporate veil where the limited partnership is used to defraud.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment under s. 247(2) to the arm's length amount; a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment if the taxpayer failed to make or provide documentation and the adjustment exceeds CAD 1 million; a Form T106 late-filing penalty of CAD 100 per month up to CAD 2,500; GAAR penalties of up to 50% of the tax benefit if the arrangement is found to be abusive. The Excise Duty on imported spirits may be reassessed upward.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed or caused to be signed a false T106 or T2 can be held personally liable for the corporate partner's tax indebtedness; intentional transfer pricing manipulation supports criminal prosecution for tax evasion, including fines and imprisonment; the court may order restitution and a personal travel ban pending resolution of the assessment.
Money Laundering / Beneficial Ownership Concealment

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), failure to report a cross-border electronic funds transfer exceeding CAD 10,000, failure to maintain beneficial ownership records, and non-compliance with FINTRAC can result in administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for corporations and CAD 500,000 for individuals; criminal money laundering under s. 462.31 of the Criminal Code is an indictable offence punishable by up to ten years' imprisonment; the BC Land Owner Transparency Act and federal corporate beneficial ownership rules carry additional penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner who knowingly participate in laundering proceeds or concealing the true owner are criminally liable as principals or conspirators, with penalties including fines of CAD 1 million in serious cases and imprisonment up to 14 years for fraud under s. 380 Criminal Code; personal assets can be frozen under a restraint order and civil forfeiture may be pursued by law enforcement.
Shell Company and Sham Substance Structure

Statutory sanction: Under s. 245 GAAR, a tax benefit may be denied with a penalty of 50% of the benefit; sham transaction doctrine may invalidate the entire contractual arrangement; unlicensed bonded activity attracts regulatory fines under the Excise Act, 2001 and BC Liquor Control and Licensing Act; CRA may also impose a 10% penalty on the aggregate unreported partnership income under s. 162(7).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a sham general partner are jointly liable for the partnership's unpaid tax and excise duty through the general partner; the court may 'pierce the corporate veil' to hold directors personally liable for knowing facilitation of an unlawful scheme; travel bans and extradition may apply where the tax fraud involves international transactions.
