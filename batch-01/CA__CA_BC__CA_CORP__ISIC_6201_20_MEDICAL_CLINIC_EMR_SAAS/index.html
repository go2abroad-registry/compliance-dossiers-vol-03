Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) – My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); provincial counterpart for British Columbia: BC Ministry of Finance, eTaxBC and BC Corporate Online (https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes).

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (federal) or BC Business Corporations Act; obtain articles and certificate of incorporation from Corporations Canada or BC Registry via BC Corporate Online. 2) Apply for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) using CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1; the BN must be activated for the RC (corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), and RP (payroll) program accounts. 3) A non-resident foreign corporation registers by filing Form RC1 with a designated non-resident officer, providing the foreign registration number and home jurisdiction tax ID, and may simultaneously register for GST/HST under the simplified non-resident framework. 4) Register for BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) with the BC Ministry of Finance via eTaxBC or the BC OneStop Business Registry if making taxable sales in BC (remote seller threshold: CAD 10,000 annual gross revenue from BC taxable sales). 5) Open a CRA payroll (RP) account for BC employees; monitor the BC Employer Health Tax (EHT) registration threshold of CAD 500,000 annual BC remuneration. 6) File an annual return disclosing directors and significant individuals with the applicable registry; maintain the BN active to avoid CRA account suspension.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation; a Canadian business corporation is a separate taxpayer liable to federal Part I tax (12% general rate after federal abatement; 9% small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income within an associated group) and BC provincial corporate tax (12% general; 2% small business rate), producing combined effective headline rates of approximately 27% general and 11% small business. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; the BC allocation follows the permanent establishment (PE) formula under the federal income tax regulations. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a Canadian business corporation is a separate taxpayer liable to federal Part I tax (12% general rate after federal abatement; 9% small business rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income within an associated group) and BC provincial corporate tax (12% general; 2% small business rate), producing combined effective headline rates of approximately 27% general and 11% small business. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income; the BC allocation follows the permanent establishment (PE) formula under the federal income tax regulations. Non-resident corporations are taxed on Canadian-source business income only if a PE exists in Canada or BC, plus Part XIII withholding on passive payments.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a 'check-the-box' entity classification regime; corporations are conclusively taxed at entity level. Available elections include: (i) Income Tax Act s. 216(1) net-basis election for non-resident rental and timber royalty income; (ii) s. 85(1) rollover election to transfer property to a corporation on a tax-deferred basis; (iii) s. 256(2) associated-corporation designation allocating the small business deduction limit; (iv) GST/HST Quick Method election (Excise Tax Act s. 227) and alternative method elections for financial institutions; (v) non-resident exporter election under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 23 for establishing zero-rating of services to non-registered non-residents; and (vi) hybrid mismatch arrangements are addressed through the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL) rules in Part XVIII of the Income Tax Act (ss. 18.2, 18.21–18.23) and branch-benefit anti-hybrid provisions of ss. 18.4–18.5, rather than elective regimes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 – GST/HST small supplier registration threshold, measured on worldwide taxable supplies in Canada over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, registration is mandatory for the person. Non-resident digital suppliers (including SaaS vendors) must register when Canadian-sourced taxable revenue exceeds CAD 30,000 in a calendar quarter or across the four preceding quarters; no threshold applies to providers of 'specified digital supplies' once the election to use the simplified framework is made. BC PST remote seller threshold: CAD 10,000 of gross annual revenue from taxable sales of software/tangible property delivered into BC.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of a computer program accessed remotely (SaaS) or other digital service is zero-rated for GST/HST under Part V, Schedule VI, s. 23 and s. 23.1 of the Excise Tax Act if the recipient is a non-resident person who is not registered under s. 240(1) of the ETA, and the supply is not in respect of real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property located in Canada at the time the service is performed. For medical-clinic EMR SaaS, the export zero-rating applies to (i) subscriptions to foreign clinics, (ii) data-hosting or support services delivered from Canadian servers where the beneficial use occurs outside Canada, and (iii) ancillary professional services to foreign medical practices, provided no direct nexus exists with Canadian property. BC PST likewise does not apply where the software is delivered outside BC for use wholly outside the province; evidence of the destination country, end-user location, and foreign payment should be recorded.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A / CBSA 'A' export declaration for tangible media or hardware; for pure SaaS, a commercial export invoice referencing the software license and digital delivery method)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (for goods movement; for digital exports, server-delivery logs and IP/geo-location evidence of the non-resident customer's access point)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT/FX settlement advice, or bank statement evidencing receipt of payment from the non-resident customer
  • Non-resident status certificate (e.g., foreign VAT registration extract, W-8BEN-E equivalent, or signed declaration of non-residency and non-registration under ETA s. 240)
  • Executed SaaS subscription or license agreement identifying the foreign clinic as the consumer and specifying the export nature of the service
  • Reconciliation worksheet mapping zero-rated export invoices to the GST/HST return (RT) and supporting general ledger extracts

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal e-invoicing clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction controls (CTC). The CRA accepts electronic GST/HST invoices provided they contain the prescribed information (supplier name and BN/RT account, date of supply, invoice number, tax amount and rate, total payable) under ETA s. 286 and CRA administrative policy; the Pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing pilot (Peppol / EN 16931 framework) is voluntary and not an API clearance gateway. BC PST does not impose invoice stamping requirements; registrants must retain sales invoices for audit but no immediate government clearance is required.

The Excise Tax Act contains no codified 180-day FX repatriation deadline; however, CRA custom and practice treat consistent settlement of export receivables within 180 days as confirmatory of a genuine export sale. Longer collection cycles or late repatriation trigger audit scrutiny, potential FAPI attribution to foreign affiliates, transfer-pricing adjustments under s. 247, and denial of zero-rating if the transaction is recharacterized as a supply to a Canadian person. Records must be retained for 6 years (ETA s. 286; ITA s. 230). The CRA may reassess GST/HST within 4 years (ETA s. 298(1)) and impose: (i) gross negligence penalty of up to 50% of the unremitted tax under ETA s. 285.1(1) and ITA s. 163(2); (ii) daily compound interest under ETA s. 280; (iii) retroactive clawback of input tax credits and uncollected GST/HST with interest; (iv) director liability under ETA s. 323 for unremitted amounts; and (v) criminal prosecution for evasion under ETA s. 327 and ITA s. 239, carrying fines and imprisonment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. Profit remittance is unrestricted: dividends, royalties, interest, and management fees may be paid offshore subject only to Part XIII withholding tax of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, typically reduced to 5%-15% under tax treaties). No exchange-control permit, central-bank approval, or repatriation license is required for payments made from a Canadian bank account held by a British Columbia incorporated EMR SaaS treasury entity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or foreign-currency repatriation quota on software/SaaS export receipts. Export earnings may be retained in CAD or USD onshore or offshore. The only statutory obligations are FINTRAC cross-border electronic funds transfer reporting for single transactions of CAD 10,000 or more, CRS/FATCA account disclosures, and normal corporate income tax declarations of foreign-currency gains or losses.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), constituted under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2). Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange administration equivalent to a centralized capital-control agency; cross-border transaction surveillance is performed by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary collections/disbursement rail only; Low as a primary treasury vehicle. Canada does not recognize the EU/UK Electronic Money Institution (EMI) license; an offshore EMI cannot accept deposits or open a domestic payment account without a Canadian bank partner. Any payment service provider serving Canadian customers must register under the Retail Payment Activities Act with the Bank of Canada (phased registration effective September 2025) and, if engaged in foreign-exchange dealing or money remittance, register as a Money Services Business with FINTRAC. Recommended structure: Schedule I bank account as the legal treasury layer, with Stripe Billing and bank-transfer rails used exclusively as the customer acceptance layer.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions in the taxation year triggers the requirement for contemporaneous, complete, and accurate Local File documentation; a Master File and Country-by-Country Report are expected for multinational enterprise groups with consolidated annual revenue of C$750,000,000 or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations; however, the Income Tax Act's General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and the Limitation on Benefits provisions in newer DTAs require genuine commercial rationale and real economic activity. A BC-resident EMR SaaS corporation must maintain a substantive presence, including Canadian directors or officers with management authority, a physical registered office, local bank accounts, books and records, and an operational substance framework matching its intercompany risk allocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (False Reporting / Concealment)

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, summary conviction carries a fine of up to C$25,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 12 months; on indictment, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax may also apply under section 163(2).

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or participate in evasion are personally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax, penalties, and interest; the CRA may issue director liability assessments, garnish personal bank accounts, register liens against personal property, and request travel bans or passport restrictions.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing (Non-Arm's Length Services)

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess on an arm's length basis under section 247, resulting in additional tax, interest, and a transfer pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment when complete documentation is not provided within 90 days. Part XIII withholding tax may also be reimposed on mischaracterized management fees or royalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve sham or non-arm's length intercompany charges may face personal civil liability for the tax shortfall under anti-avoidance regimes, and the corporate veil may be pierced where the arrangement is a pure conduit or false document is filed.
Shell Company / Artificial Economic Substance (GAAR / Treaty Abuse)

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) to deny the tax benefit, reassess the unpaid Part XIII tax, impose interest, and levy a GAAR penalty of 20% (30% for abusive transactions) of the tax benefit realized. The corporation may lose treaty benefits and be treated as fiscally transparent in certain cross-border tax rulings.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil to impose personal liability on directors and controlling shareholders if the entity is a facade or sham; CRA can issue personal assessments, freeze assets, and pursue extradition or travel bans for significant international tax evasion.
Part XIII Withholding Tax Evasion on Dividends, Interest, or Royalties

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent is personally liable for the full 25% unremitted tax plus compound interest; the CRA may also impose penalties under section 227(9) equal to 10% of the amount not remitted, or 20% for a second or subsequent failure, and may prosecute for failure to comply with Part XIII obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII tax under section 227.1, and failure to enforce collection can lead to direct assessments, seizure of personal assets, and in egregious cross-border cases, criminal sanctions and travel restrictions.
