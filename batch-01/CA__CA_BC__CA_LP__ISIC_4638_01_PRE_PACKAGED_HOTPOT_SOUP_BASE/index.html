Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — business registration portal: My Business Account at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; GST/HST registration administered federally by CRA (Form RC1 or online); BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) administered separately by the British Columbia Ministry of Finance, Consumer Taxation Branch via eTaxBC (https://etax.gov.bc.ca).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Confirm Canadian nexus — a BC-resident limited partnership is registrable, while a non-resident LP carrying on business in Canada must register once taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in the trailing four calendar quarters (ETA s. 240(1)). Step 2: Ensure the LP is legally registered with the BC Corporate Registry (BC Online) under the BC Limited Partnership Act, with a registered office and, if non-resident, an agent for service in BC. Step 3: Obtain the federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and/or GST/HST Account) or by applying online through CRA My Business Account; a non-resident applicant may be required to post security (bank guarantee or bond) under ETA s. 240(3). Step 4: Open program accounts in the LP legal name — RT (GST/HST) for indirect tax and RC (income tax information return) for the T5013 partnership return; register for BC PST vendor/reseller status with the BC Ministry of Finance only to acquire goods for resale free of PST. Step 5: Issue GST/HST invoices bearing the BN; zero-rated export invoices must state the zero-rated status under Schedule VI Part V of the Excise Tax Act. Step 6: File the annual T5013 Summary/Slips and allocate partnership income to the general and limited partners, who report the allocated Canadian-source income on their own T1 or T2 returns; non-resident partners' share is taxable in Canada only if the partnership carries on business through a Canadian permanent establishment and the income is not treaty-exempt.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — For Canadian income tax the BC LP is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96: the partnership itself pays no income tax, files only an information return (T5013), and each partner is taxed on its allocated share. For GST/HST the LP is a separate person and registrant. The public-offering SIFT rules do not apply to a private export-distribution LP. The Canada-US income tax convention fiscally transparent entity rules apply so US partners are taxed currently on allocated income and obtain treaty benefits.

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box election; LP classification is governed by ITA s. 96 and the BC Partnership Act. Available elections and rules: (i) voluntary GST/HST registration below the small-supplier threshold (ETA s. 240(2)); (ii) GST/HST group registration election for closely related registrants (ETA s. 156); (iii) joint election on partnership property transfers (ITA s. 96(3)); (iv) elective deemed partnership fiscal period (ITA s. 99(2)); (v) a US partner may cause the LP to file IRS Form 8832 classification election in the US, but a resulting mismatch with Canadian pass-through treatment activates the hybrid-mismatch rules in ITA ss. 12.7, 18.2 and 18.3, which permit specific elective designations to neutralize deduction and withholding asymmetries.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total taxable supplies (including zero-rated exports) made in Canada or from a place in Canada in the last four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA s. 148(1)(a) and s. 240(1)); once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. For BC PST there is no filing threshold for making taxable retail sales in BC, but a PST vendor/reseller number is needed to acquire goods for resale without PST; export and wholesale-for-resale supplies are excluded from PST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST-registered LP may zero-rate exports of pre-packaged hotpot soup base under ETA Schedule VI Part V (supply of goods) where: (i) the supply is a sale from a Canadian place of business to a non-resident buyer; (ii) the goods are actually exported from Canada within a reasonable time (commercially expected within 60 days) by the vendor or by a common carrier or the buyer; (iii) title and risk of loss pass outside Canada; (iv) the goods are reported through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or a CBSA B13A export declaration where required; and (v) the LP holds the prescribed documentary evidence of export. Ancillary offshore services (for example, ocean freight arranged outside Canada) to a non-resident who is not a GST/HST registrant are zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V s. 9 provided the service is performed wholly outside Canada. Domestic sales of the soup base within Canada remain taxable at 5% GST; if also sold at retail in BC, 7% PST applies. Refund mechanics: the LP claims input tax credits on its GST/HST return and receives a refund of any net overpayment of GST within normal CRA processing timelines.

  • Customs Export Declaration — CBSA Form B13A or equivalent filed electronically in the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for export shipments, required generally for goods valued above CAD 2,000 or for controlled/prohibited goods.
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill issued by the common carrier, naming the exporting LP as shipper, the overseas buyer as consignee, and a foreign port/place of delivery outside Canada.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank SWIFT/TT credit advice or letter-of-credit settlement statement) evidencing receipt of the foreign-currency sale proceeds from the non-resident buyer into the LP's Canadian bank account.

Canada imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory API stamping, or digital stamping regime. E-invoicing is voluntary; the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and PEPPOL interoperability are used mainly for business-to-government procurement. Paper invoices, PDF, XML and EDI are all acceptable, provided they include the supplier name and BN, registrant status, total consideration and tax payable as required by ETA s. 221. Zero-rated export invoices should expressly state the zero-rated character (Schedule VI Part V) to preserve audit clarity; underlying records, including the CERS export declaration, bills of lading, purchase orders and remittance advices, must be retained for six years (ETA s. 286).

CRA export audits reconcile each zero-rated invoice against the CERS export declaration, bill of lading, purchase order, and the 180-day inward remittance evidence; missing or inconsistent documents cause denial of zero-rating, reassessment of uncollected GST at 5% on the full consideration, disallowance of input tax credits, arrears interest, and penalties. Statutory exposures: 25% civil penalty for false statements (ETA s. 280); 50% gross-negligence penalty (ETA s. 285.1); and criminal fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus possible imprisonment (ETA ss. 283 to 285). CRA may also reassess beyond the normal four-year limitation period where misrepresentation, neglect or fraud occurred (ETA s. 298), and claw back refunds through the normal assessment and appeal process, with interest running from the original refund date.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible currency regime with unrestricted current account transactions and liberalized capital account rules under the Foreign Investment Policy. Profit remittances face no barriers. All cross-border flows are subject to PCMLTFA reporting and FINTRAC oversight; no capital controls apply to legitimate corporate activity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement for export proceeds. Exporters retain full control over foreign currency receipts and may convert at prevailing market rates. Mandatory reporting to Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for tax compliance only; no conversion ratios or surrender timelines exist.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax under s.212(2) of the Income Tax Act on Canada-source dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident. This rate is not directly triggered by distributions from a BC Limited Partnership, because an LP is not a corporation and does not pay dividends. Instead, a non-resident corporate partner repatriating profits from the LP is subject to Part I tax on its allocable Canadian business income and, where applicable, a 25% Part XIV branch profits tax on the after-tax amount repatriated to its home jurisdiction, reduced under the Canada-US Treaty to 5% for qualified corporations or 15% for other non-resident corporations.

Any balance of tax due is generally payable within two months after year-end (or three months for a CCPC eligible for the small business limit).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 in aggregate reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions in a taxation year triggers the mandatory filing of Form T106. Canada does not statutorily prescribe separate Master File and Local File tiers; however, a taxpayer above this threshold must prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation sufficient to demonstrate arm's-length pricing on request. CRA may require the documentation within three months of a notice and may impose a 10% penalty under s.247(3) where reasonable efforts were not made.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone Economic Substances Regulation (ESR) regime comparable to the UAE, but economic substance is enforced through the common-law substance-over-form doctrine, the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule under s.245 of the Income Tax Act, and OECD treaty anti-abuse provisions. A BC Limited Partnership must carry on genuine business activity in Canada, maintain a Canadian registered office and physical facilities, employ personnel with operating authority, and perform substantive distribution functions. Otherwise the CRA may disregard the partnership, reallocate Canadian-source profits to a foreign partner, or impose the 25% branch tax and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Concealment

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under s.239(1) of the Income Tax Act carries, on summary conviction, a fine of 50% to 100% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to two years; on indictment, a fine of 100% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to five years. CRA may also assess a gross negligence penalty under s.163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners who participated in or knowingly allowed the evasion are jointly and severally liable. A general partner's personal assets are exposed under BC partnership law; civil restitution, asset freezes, travel bans, and imprisonment can be imposed.
Failure to File / Non-Remittance of Withholdings

Statutory sanction: Failure to file penalties are prescribed under s.162(1) (5% plus 1% per month) and s.162(6) (C$100 plus C$25 per day up to C$2,500). Failure to withhold under s.227(8.2) makes the LP liable to remit the unwithheld amount plus interest and penalties of up to 10% of the amount.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner can be personally assessed under s.227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII withholdings. A general partner's unlimited liability converts the partnership obligation into a personal debt, and foreign travel bans may be issued pending resolution of the outstanding liability.
Transfer Pricing Misconduct

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the transfer price under s.247(2), impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty under s.247(3), and charge a separate late-filing penalty under s.162(2.01) of C$100 per day up to C$2,500 for failure to file T106. Interest accrues from the original taxation year.

Director personal liability: Corporate officers and directors may be subject to due diligence liability and can be assessed for the unpaid corporate amount under provincial director liability rules where they authorized the pricing and failed to ensure reasonable documentation standards.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement / GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may disregard the partnership as a sham, allocate the business profits to the non-resident partner as carrying on business in Canada, apply the GAAR to deny the tax benefit, and impose a 50% gross negligence penalty. The BC registrar may also strike the LP for non-compliance with the Limited Partnership Act.

Director personal liability: Where the limited partner has participated in control of the LP, the statutory limitation on liability is lost under BC law, and the limited partner is personally liable for partnership debts. Courts may pierce the corporate veil of a foreign shell general partner and impose liability on the ultimate beneficial owner.
Anti-Money Laundering / Beneficial Ownership Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the BC Business Corporations Act, failure to file or update the individuals with significant control register carries fines up to C$100,000 for the entity and C$50,000 for directors. Under Canada's Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties can reach C$2,000,000; criminal liability under s.462.31 of the Criminal Code carries imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers who knowingly or recklessly facilitate money laundering or fail to file required beneficial ownership reports may be prosecuted personally, face imprisonment, and be subject to professional disqualification. General partners are personally liable for the LP's obligations to the full extent of their personal assets.
Permanent Establishment Exposure / Foreign Reporting Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The non-resident corporation becomes subject to Part I tax on all Canadian-source business income at the combined 27% rate and, on remittance, a 25% Part XIV branch tax. Failure to file a T2 return triggers penalties of 5% plus 1% per month, a jeopardy assessment, and potential denial of treaty benefits.

Director personal liability: Directors of the non-resident corporation and its Canadian branch are jointly liable for unremitted source deductions and can be personally assessed. A finding of a Canadian PE may extend Canadian tax collection powers to the foreign parent's worldwide assets in enforcement actions by the CRA.
