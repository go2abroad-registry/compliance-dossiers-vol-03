Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; primary portal: CRA My Business Account (canada.ca/en/revenue-agency) for Business Number (BN), GST/HST (RT program account), corporate income tax (RC program account) and payroll (RP program account). Provincial indirect-tax authority: British Columbia Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC portal) for BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) registration and remittance.

E-government filing portal

For a federally or provincially incorporated Business Corporation, CRA issues the 9-digit Business Number (BN) automatically upon incorporation. The GST/HST account is procured by enrolling the RT program account through CRA online registration or Form RC1. Foreign entities without Canadian incorporation must obtain a statutory TIN by filing Form RC1 with CRA; non-resident vendors supplying digital services to Canadian customers must register for GST/HST from the first Canadian dollar of revenue (no small-supplier threshold) and may designate a Canadian representative or agent. BC PST registration via eTaxBC is required only for taxable BC sales; remote access SaaS (including cybersecurity vulnerability scanning) is generally outside the scope of BC PST.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation: the Business Corporation is a resident of Canada taxed as a separate entity on worldwide net income under ITA s. 9, with no pass-through election available. ISIC 6201 SaaS revenue constitutes taxable 'services' for GST/HST purposes (ETA s. 123) and active business income for corporate income tax purposes; if the CA_CORP is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), the active business income qualifies for the federal small business deduction (9% federal rate on the first CAD 500,000) and the BC provincial small business rate (2%). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: the Business Corporation is a resident of Canada taxed as a separate entity on worldwide net income under ITA s. 9, with no pass-through election available. ISIC 6201 SaaS revenue constitutes taxable 'services' for GST/HST purposes (ETA s. 123) and active business income for corporate income tax purposes; if the CA_CORP is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), the active business income qualifies for the federal small business deduction (9% federal rate on the first CAD 500,000) and the BC provincial small business rate (2%). No territorial scope exclusion and no tax-exempt-zone status apply.

Available election forms

No 'check-the-box' regime exists in Canada; entity-level classification is statutory and cannot be waived. Relevant elections applicable to CA_CORP: (1) ETA s. 156 election for closely related corporations (Form GST25) deeming intercompany supplies to be made for nil consideration; (2) ETA s. 221 Quick Method of accounting (Form GST74) for small suppliers with taxable revenue below CAD 400,000 (excluding zero-rated export supplies); (3) ITA s. 256(2) election to deem associated corporations to be non-associated for CCPC small business deduction purposes; (4) hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2 and 18.3) apply statutorily with limited recharacterization adjustments for dual-inclusion income and carry no discretionary check-the-box election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies during the current and preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold, ETA s. 148(1)); registration becomes mandatory within 30 days after the threshold is exceeded. Voluntary registration is available under ETA s. 240 before the threshold is reached. A non-resident supplier of 'specified digital services' must register without any threshold (ETA s. 240(2.1)); a Canadian-resident CA_CORP remains eligible for the small-supplier exemption until the CAD 30,000 benchmark is exceeded or the corporation voluntarily registers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exported cybersecurity SaaS is zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 where all conditions are met: (i) the supply is made by a GST/HST registrant; (ii) the recipient is a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST under ETA Part IX and does not carry on business in Canada at the time the service is performed; (iii) the service is not supplied in respect of real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada; and (iv) the consideration is payable to the Canadian exporter. Zero-rating preserves full input tax credits on cloud infrastructure and labour inputs (ETA ss. 169 and 170). A supply made to a Canadian-resident intermediary or for use in Canada is taxable at 5% GST; contract allocation must identify the offshore end-user to substantiate the 0% rate.

  • Executed service agreement (MSA/SOW) naming the non-resident customer with foreign place of performance and governing law; services exports do not require a B13A customs export declaration, so the commercial contract functions as the customs-substitute export document
  • Tax invoice satisfying ETA s. 3 content requirements showing 0% GST, foreign billing address, and the customer's non-resident status; if the customer has a Canadian presence, the customer's GST/HST registration number (or confirmation of non-registration) is attached to evidence the Part V s. 7 test
  • Inward remittance evidence: SWIFT MT103 or international wire transfer advice from the customer's foreign bank account to the Canadian entity's account, functioning as an inward remittance certificate and demonstrating settlement within the 180-day commercial repatriation window
  • Non-resident status proof: W-8BEN-E for US counterparties, foreign corporate registry extract, or foreign tax identification documentation evidencing that the recipient had no Canadian establishment and was not a GST/HST registrant at the time of supply

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) mandate exists in Canada. E-invoicing is voluntary and interoperable through the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL framework. Mandatory electronic filing applies to GST/HST returns where annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,000,000 (ETA s. 273.1); T2 corporate tax returns are also subject to mandatory e-filing for corporations with gross revenue above CAD 1,000,000. Registrants must retain all prescribed books and records, including electronic invoices, in a readable and accessible format for six years (ETA s. 286); no invoice-level data transmission to CRA occurs before the periodic return.

CRA may reassess zero-rated export claims within four years, extended to seven years in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, with no time limit for fraud. A failed zero-rating claim triggers: (i) reassessment of 5% GST on the full consideration plus compounding arrears interest at the prescribed rate plus 4%; (ii) the ETA s. 285 false-declaration penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 or 25% of the understated net tax; (iii) the ETA s. 285.1 gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the net tax understated; (iv) late-filing and late-remittance penalties under ETA s. 280 of 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per month for up to 12 months; (v) equivalent income tax penalties under ITA s. 163(2) for gross negligence; and (vi) criminal prosecution for evasion under ETA s. 327, punishable by fines up to 200% of the evaded amount and imprisonment of up to five years. Refunds and input tax credits are clawed back with retroactive interest, and CRA audit practice tests export pricing reasonableness against transfer-pricing benchmarks and requires the complete substantiation file to be retained for six years under ETA s. 286.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations; the Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts with no administrative approval requirement for profit remittance, dividend distribution, or capital repatriation. Non-resident shareholders face Canadian non-resident dividend withholding tax (normally 25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, recoverable or reduced under applicable tax treaties). There are no foreign-exchange surrender, quota, central-bank approval, or currency-matching barriers for a low-risk cybersecurity SaaS entity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender regime exists in Canada. There is no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts, no prescribed banking timeline for repatriation, and no requirement to convert foreign currency into Canadian dollars. Export/service receipts may be retained in any freely convertible currency, but all profits and taxable income must be reported to the Canada Revenue Agency in Canadian-dollar equivalents at applicable Bank of Canada exchange rates.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) with prudential oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange-control authority or central-planning FX licensing regime, and cross-border monetary flows are monitored by FINTRAC and the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium feasibility. Offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) are not authorized to hold Canadian deposit accounts under the Bank Act. To operate in British Columbia, a foreign EMI must either partner with a Canadian Schedule I bank or register with FINTRAC as a money services business; after the Retail Payment Activities Act comes fully into force, such entities must also register with the Bank of Canada as a retail payment service provider. An unlicensed offshore EMI is legally unsuitable as the primary treasury rail for inbound capital or outbound vendor settlements.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.0% combined Federal (15%) and British Columbia Provincial (12%) general corporate income tax rate on taxable income allocated to BC

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on all dividend payments to non-resident shareholders under subsection 212(2) of the Income Tax Act (Canada), unless the rate is reduced by an applicable income tax treaty; for a Canadian subsidiary paying substantial dividends to a U.S. parent, the Canada-U.S. Treaty (Article X) reduces the rate to 5% where the beneficial owner is a corporation that owns directly at least 10% of the voting shares, 15% in portfolio cases; with a China-Canada Treaty, the dividend rate is 10% where beneficial owner owns at least 10% of the capital, 15% otherwise; with a Hong Kong-Canada treaty, 5% where the beneficial owner controls at least 10% of the voting power, 15% otherwise; no treaty relief is available to shareholders in zero-corporate-tax offshore jurisdictions without a comprehensive agreement (e.g., BVI or Cayman Islands, absent substance and entitlement)

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within six months after the end of the corporation’s fiscal period; for a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following year; any balance of tax payable is due within two months after the fiscal year-end (three months for qualifying CCPCs claiming the small business deduction, subject to the eligible grace period limits); instalment payments are required quarterly if estimated tax payable exceeds CAD 3,000, with due dates on the last day of each month following the corporation’s fiscal quarter

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in the value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions for which a Local File (Form T106) must be completed and maintained; the Master File (Form T106 Part II with the T2) is required where aggregate revenue exceeds CAD 30 million and the value of cross-border related-party transactions with each non-resident related party exceeds CAD 5 million, or where total assets exceed CAD 100 million, or where total gross revenue exceeds CAD 50 million; separate Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) is required for Canadian ultimate parent entities of multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million (approximately CAD 1.1 billion), and a Canadian subsidiary of a foreign parent group may be required to file CBCR in Canada if the ultimate parent does not file in its own jurisdiction; transfer-pricing documentation must be contemporaneous and filed within six months of the fiscal year-end or be subject to penalty

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) statute of the kind found in the UAE or BVI; however, the doctrine of economic substance is robustly enforced through the statutory general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under subsection 245(2) of the Income Tax Act, which denies a tax benefit arising from a transaction that is considered an avoidance transaction unless the transaction is primarily for a bona fide purpose other than to obtain a tax benefit, and where the transactions are not consistent with the object, spirit, or purpose of the statute; for treaty claims, the OECD BEPS 2023 Multilateral Instrument (MLI) Minimum Standard and the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) apply to deny treaty benefits unless the person claiming benefits has genuine economic activity, substance, staff, premises, and business purpose in the treaty jurisdiction; for Canadian non-resident withholding and dividend stripping, subsection 212(4) and the 'dividend on connected shares' rules require ownership of more than 10% of the issued shares and genuine business integration, otherwise the reduction is denied; Canadian courts have consistently applied a purposive analysis that requires real economic operations, commercial risk, and business purpose — paper conduit entities in tax havens interposed between a Canadian operating entity and a treaty-resident parent may trigger a full 25% withholding plus reassessments with gross negligence penalties; where a Canadian-held intellectual property migration to an offshore low-tax entity is followed by a licensing stream, the Canada Revenue Agency will scrutinize the transfer for arm's-length royalty value, the absence of local personnel, and the application of Canada's functional analysis, commonly resulting in recharacterization under the OECD's 'DEMPE' (Development, Enhancement, Maintenance, Protection, and Exploitation) principles and GAAR

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Non-Filing of Corporate Income Tax

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess within the normal four-year period (or indefinitely where misrepresentation due to neglect, fraud, or wilful default) and impose a civil penalty under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax attributable to the false statement; interest accrues daily at the prescribed rate; criminal prosecution for tax evasion under section 327 of the Income Tax Act attracts, upon summary conviction, a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years; for a BC corporation, the BC Income Tax Act also imposes penalties of 50% of the amount of tax evaded plus fines; the Director of the BC Securities Commission may also impose administrative monetary penalties if evasion intersects with capital raising or continuous disclosure obligations

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of the BC corporation are jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for the corporation’s unpaid tax, interest, and penalties where assets have been distributed, and for payroll source deductions withheld and unremitted; BC Business Corporations Act permits the court to pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is used to commit fraud or where the directors knowingly finance evasion; a finding of wilful tax evasion may result in directors’ personal bankruptcy on CRA priority claims, Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) travel bans under the Cross-Border Currency Reporting Program where assets are moved internationally, and imprisonment of up to five years for the directing mind; the BC civil forfeiture office may seek forfeiture of property obtained through tax evasion; the CRA publishes willful default prosecutions, causing professional licensing and business licence damage for cybersecurity principals.
Failure to File Mandatory Transfer Pricing Documentation / Penalty Under Subsection 247(3)

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the taxpayer’s cross-border transaction prices to arm's-length prices and apply the transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act equal to 10% of the understated tax, greater than CAD 5,000, where the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts to determine and use arm's-length prices; in addition, late-filed Form T106 penalties of CAD 2,500 per month to a maximum of CAD 6,000 per form, and false statements penalties of CAD 24,000 or more apply; the assessment can be issued for a taxation year beyond the normal reassessment period where the taxpayer has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default; interest accumulates at the prescribed rate compounding daily; CRA may also impose gross negligence penalties of 50% of the underestimated amount when misrepresentation is deliberate

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in a transfer pricing misrepresentation may be personally liable for the 10% penalty to the extent they caused the understated tax; the Ontario and BC courts have extended personal liability to officers who directed the transfer pricing policy; directors can be held personally for civil fraud and unjust enrichment claims brought by the CRA under the Crown Liability and Proceedings Act; a criminal referral under section 239 of the Income Tax Act for making false statements in transfer pricing documentation may lead to imprisonment for the principals; foreign in-bound tax directors should obtain an internal separation of duties and independent expert opinions to avoid individual exposure.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance in Treaty Jurisdiction

Statutory sanction: Under Canada's GAAR, CRA will deny the treaty reduction and assess the full 25% Part XIII withholding plus interest and penalties; with the OECD MLI, derivative benefits and limitation on benefits provisions may block treaty access outright; Canadian courts under Alta Energy Luxembourg S.à r.l. v. Canada have upheld the GAAR for circular shareholding structures where the purpose of the entity is obtaining treaty benefits; penalties include the 50% gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) and, where artificial transactions are used to avoid tax, penalties under section 162 up to CAD 25,000; the Minister may also apply a 10% penalty on the arm's-length adjustment; foreign shareholders may be denied refunds under NR7 where there is no good-faith treaty claim; permanent establishment exposure for the non-resident parent may also be found, resulting in full Canadian branch tax under subsections 219(1) and 219.2 of the Income Tax Act (25% branch tax)

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of both the Canadian entity and the foreign shell are personally liable for induced breach of the Income Tax Act; the CRA has the power to issue a requirement to pay under subsection 316(1) to directors for unremitted Part XIII withholding; directors who sign false NR301 declarations or knowingly establish a sham entity are subject to penalties for making false statements — fines up to CAD 50,000 and imprisonment of up to five years under section 239; Canadian financial reporting officers and tax partners who advise on conduit structures may face professional negligence claims; travel bans and seizure of personal assets can occur where the shell's sole asset is the Canadian operating company's equity and the director attempts to liquidate or strip value
Money Laundering / Anti-Money Laundering Failure

Statutory sanction: Violations of the PCMLTFA carry administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for corporations and, on indictment, fines up to CAD 750,000 and imprisonment of up to 10 years for laundering proceeds of crime under the Criminal Code (Canada), section 462.31; FINTRAC can impose penalties of up to CAD 1,500,000 for first-time violations arising from 2022 amendments; the BC Civil Forfeiture Office may freeze and forfeit assets involved in money laundering; failure to report a suspicious transaction may lead to a CRA audit of unreported income and penalties of 50%; senior officers may face imprisonment for participating in laundering cybercrime ransom proceeds

Director personal liability: Dir qua directors may be subject to director liability under the PCMLTFA for failure to implement a compliance program (administrative penalties up to CAD 1,000,000); where directors are found to have knowingly participated in laundering cybercrime proceeds, criminal liability attaches to the directing mind, with sentences up to 10 years in a federal penitentiary; the BC Civil Forfeiture Office may pursue personal assets disproportionate to the company interest; FINTRAC can disclose to law enforcement and CRA, resulting in additional tax evasion prosecutions and travel bans under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act for foreign directors when fraud or laundering is established.
Failure to Report Foreign Subsidiary / Cross-Border Disclosure (Form T1134 / T1142)

Statutory sanction: Late or failure to file T1134 attracts a CAD 2,500 penalty per form; where the failure is for more than 24 months, the penalty is CAD 6,000 per form; continued non-compliance for more than 24 months for each offshore affiliate triggers CDN 1,000 per month with a maximum of CDN 24,000; failure to file T1135 for foreign property attracts a maximum penalty of CAD 12,000 per year, and continued failure attracts an additional CAD 2,000 per month; CRA may also reassess the foreign affiliate's dividend income as taxable in Canada where no election was filed, retroactively applying the 25% rate; false statements in foreign affiliate disclosure may attract 50% gross negligence penalties

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate failures to file T1134, but where the director signs a false certification of the T1134 they may be personally assessed for false statement penalties; directors who divert income to an unaffiliated offshore entity receive a personal benefit and are subject to director liability for unremitted amounts and equitable claims; the CRA has authority under subsection 289(1) to prosecute directors for false statements, and the courts have held that an officer who treats corporate assets as personal funds loses the protection of the corporate veil.
Unpaid Source Withholdings and Payroll / GST/HST Remittance Evasion

Statutory sanction: The CRA assesses a 10% penalty on the amount of unremitted source deductions if failure to remit was made knowingly or under circumstances of gross negligence, plus daily interest at the prescribed rate (currently approximately 5% per annum compounded daily, but variable); GST/HST penalties are equal to 20% of the net amount of GST/HST that was not remitted, plus interest; for Part XIII under-withholding, the corporation is liable under subsection 215(1) for the full 25% of unwithheld amounts and may be assessed a 10% penalty under section 227(8) and interest; in egregious cases, the Minister may issue a director liability assessment and a requirement to pay to third-party debtors; criminal conviction for failure to remit can result in a fine of up to 200% of the amounts and imprisonment of up to five years

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 for the full amount of unremitted source deductions, interest, and penalties, regardless of whether the corporation remains active; a director can be personally assessed under the Due Diligence Defence only if they can prove they exercised reasonable care and attempted to ensure the corporation’s compliance; personal liability is also extended to GST/HST unremitted amounts under Part IX; in a BC liquidation, the CRA has a super-priority for source deduction claims before secured creditors; directors who knowingly remit corporate funds out of Canada to foreign parents after failing to remit source deductions commit a criminal breach and may face extradition to Canada.
