Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — national tax administration and GST/HST registry, accessible via the CRA My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); British Columbia administers the separate provincial sales tax (PST) through the BC Ministry of Finance via BC eTax (https://www.bcetax.gov.bc.ca).

E-government filing portal

Apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Form RC1, by telephone, or through the CRA's Business Registration Online service; the BN is the single statutory tax identifier, and the GST/HST account is created by adding the RT program code to the BN once taxable supplies in Canada exceed the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold (registration is also mandatory for importers and certain non-resident operators). For a British Columbia limited partnership, first register the LP under the BC Partnership Act via the BC Corporate Registry so that the CRA application is made in the partnership's legal name; non-resident partners must provide the partnership's Canadian business address and an authorized representative to allow electronic filing of T5013 partnership returns and GST/HST returns; register separately for BC PST through BC eTax if the partnership has a place of business, inventory, or taxable sales in BC.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — the BC limited partnership is fiscally transparent for federal income tax purposes under ITA s. 96: the partnership computes income at the partnership level and files a T5013 information return, while each partner is taxed on the allocated share; however, for GST/HST the partnership itself is a separate registrable person, and BC PST applies to the partnership as a vendor.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Canadian law. Useful elections and mandatory rules include: (i) allocation of partnership income/losses on a reasonable basis agreed by partners under ITA ss. 96 and 103; (ii) election under ETA s. 156 to treat taxable supplies between closely related registrants as made for nil consideration; (iii) simplified accounting elections for small GST/HST registrants, including the Quick Method and permitted annual reporting; (iv) Canada's hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.3–18.6 and related anti-avoidance provisions), which apply mandatorily to payments whose characterisation differs between Canada and the payee's jurisdiction and do not offer an elective entity-classification mechanism.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are freely convertible; no exchange-control permits are required for partnership capital contributions, trade settlements, profit distribution, or capital repatriation. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely; Canadian-resident entities may hold foreign-currency accounts (FCN) domestically or abroad without restriction. For this BC Limited Partnership, remittances to non-resident limited partners take one of two forms: (i) allocable business income, taxable under Part I of the Income Tax Act on a net basis and protected for U.S. partners by the Canada-U.S. tax treaty where no permanent establishment exists; or (ii) return of capital, which is non-taxable and freely remittable. No CRA or OSFI clearance is required for any outward remittance; only bank-level AML diligence and FINTRAC electronic funds transfer (EFT) reporting at the CAD 10,000 threshold apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender ratio, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no repatriation requirement for export proceeds of dehydrated vegetables or spices. Exporters may lawfully: (i) retain proceeds indefinitely in foreign-currency accounts at Canadian or offshore banks; (ii) convert to CAD at any time at market rates; and (iii) net export receipts against related import payments, subject to transfer-pricing (section 247 of the Income Tax Act) compliance where related parties are involved. FINTRAC EFT reporting triggers at CAD 10,000 per transaction, but this is a reporting obligation of the financial intermediary, not a government surrender or approval mechanism.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) — prudential regulation; FINTRAC — AML/ATF administrator. No separate foreign-exchange control authority exists; FX operations are carried out by Schedule I banks under the Bank Act (Canada) and the Currency Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Low-Medium as a primary treasury rail; Medium as an ancillary collection/disbursement node. Wise (Wise Payments Ltd.), Airwallex (Airwallex (Canada) Ltd.), and Payoneer operate in Canada as FINTRAC-registered Money Service Businesses (MSBs), not as banks under the Bank Act (Canada). They offer multi-currency virtual accounts, competitive mid-market FX, and sub-CAD 50,000 cross-border transfers within 1-2 business days. Legal constraints: (i) MSBs cannot originate documentary letters of credit (SWIFT MT700) nor access the Lynx large-value CAD settlement system; (ii) funds held are not CDIC-insured and must be segregated in settlement accounts at a Schedule I sponsor bank; (iii) complex partnership/trust/LLC ownership structures are frequently rejected by EMI compliance; and (iv) EMIs are not authorized to execute or confirm FX hedge transactions. Conclusion: use Schedule I banks for capital escrow, commercial LC, and FX hedging; use EMIs only for low-value receivables and small overhead disbursements.

25% for dividends paid by a Canadian company to a non-resident recipient; reduced to 15% under most comprehensive income tax treaties (e.g., Canada-US treaty), and to 5% where the beneficial owner is a corporate shareholder holding at least 10% of voting shares and meeting treaty conditions. Not directly applicable to a limited partnership because partnership distributions are not legally dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Threshold under s.247(4) Income Tax Act: mandatory contemporaneous documentation required if the aggregate fair market value of all cross-border non-arm's-length transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. For services, the threshold may be considered at a lower values based on CRA's administrative guidance (e.g., for services performed, the test is over CAD 2,000,000 in certain circumstances).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain formal Economic Substance Regulations (such as those in the UAE) but applies the OECD transfer pricing guidelines, the Anti-Avoidance provisions (GAAR), and the purpose-based substance requirements under tax treaties. A limited partnership engaged as a wholesale exporter of dehydrated vegetables and spices must demonstrate commercial substance through substantive employees, physical inventory ownership, decision-making functions, and real business premises in British Columbia, to avoid re-characterization as a tax shell or perceived artificial arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Failure to Report Income

Statutory sanction: Penalties under s.162 and s.163 of the Income Tax Act: gross negligence penalties equal to 100% of the understated tax, plus 50% of any related failure-to-file penalty. Criminal prosecution under s.239 may result in fines up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporation that is a general partner may be held personally liable for unpaid taxes, interest, and penalties of the corporation under s.227.1 of the Income Tax Act if they failed to exercise due diligence. For a limited partnership, a general partner (and its directors) can face civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment if found to have directed or acquiesced in tax evasion.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: Adjustment under s.247(2) of the Income Tax Act; penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment (if adjustment exceeds PHP? CAD 1,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue, and reasonable efforts were not made). The CRA may also levy interest on the additional tax assessed.

Director personal liability: Transfer-pricing penalties do not directly trigger director liability; however, directors may be liable for the corporate taxpayer's unpaid tax, penalties, and interest if the corporation fails to withhold or remit Part XIII tax on cross-border payments. Repeated violations of transfer pricing without a sustainability strategy may lead to criminal tax evasion charges against responsible directors, along with travel restrictions and imprisonment.
Shell Company Artificial Arrangement

Statutory sanction: CRA can invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under s.245 to deny tax benefits. Moreover, the partnership may be re-characterized as a trust or corporate entity under common law if it fails to meet the partnership definition (e.g., insufficient operations, no co-ownership of business, etc.), leading to additional corporate income tax, penalties, and interest. Alberta and BC courts have upheld reassessments that disregard artificial partnerships for tax fraud.

Director personal liability: Shareholders or partners who knowingly participate in a sham or shell scheme can be personally liable for the taxes owed. Directors of a corporate general partner can face civil action for restitution to the CRA, orders to be personally assessed for corporate tax debts, and, in serious cases, criminal prosecution and imprisonment under s.239 of the Income Tax Act.
Anti-Money Laundering / Terrorism Financing Compliance

Statutory sanction: Violations of the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) are subject to administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 100,000 per violation for individuals and up to CAD 500,000 for entities. Criminal prosecution for money laundering under the Criminal Code may lead to imprisonment up to 10 years and substantial fines.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and designated persons of a corporate general partner may be personally liable for non-compliance with PCMLTFA regulations, including penalties, and may be subject to travel bans, asset freezes, and criminal prosecution if they knowingly facilitated money laundering. The CRA cooperates with FINTRAC to expose tax evasion linked to illicit funds, and directors may be personally assessed for unpaid taxes and related penalties.
