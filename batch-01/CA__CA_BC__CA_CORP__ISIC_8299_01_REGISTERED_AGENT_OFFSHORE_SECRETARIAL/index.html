Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); CRA My Business Account portal (www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); British Columbia Ministry of Finance for Provincial Sales Tax (PST) via eTaxBC (etax.gov.bc.ca); Corporate registry via BC Corporate Online and Corporations Canada.

E-government filing portal

1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or BC Business Corporations Act; the certificate of incorporation generates a unique incorporation number. 2) The CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN) through the Business Registration Online (BRO) system; a non-resident parent may act as incorporator and shareholder of the BC corporation. 3) Register the required CRA Program Accounts: RT (GST/HST) for the corporation and RC (payroll) if employees are in Canada; the GST/HST registration is mandatory once taxable supplies made in Canada exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold over four consecutive calendar quarters, but voluntary registration is advisable for a zero-rated export business to claim input tax credits. 4) Register separately with the BC Ministry of Finance for PST where the corporation carries on business in BC and makes taxable supplies (e.g., legal services, storage, certain enumerated taxable services); no PST registration threshold applies to a person carrying on business in BC. 5) Appoint a Canadian resident director or use the BC extraprovincial non-resident director rules; file initial and annual reports, and maintain a registered and records office in BC; provide the BN to the financial institution for payment rails, and complete the CRA 'Authorizing or cancelling a representative' (Form RC59) for a tax preparer.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election; a federal/BC business corporation is a per se taxable entity (equivalent to a C corporation), not eligible for pass-through status. Available elections for the registered-agent group include: (1) GST/HST Elections under ETA s.156 and s.256(1) to treat supplies between closely related or associated corporations as made for nil consideration, with no joint election permitted where one party is a non-resident unless conditions in s.256(2)-(2.4) are met; (2) Income Tax Act rollover elections under s.85, s.87 (amalgamation), and s.88 (winding-up) for internal restructuring; (3) optional registration under ETA s.240 for zero-rated exporters to recover input tax credits; (4) an election under ITA s.216 for a non-resident to file a Canadian return on net rental income, not relevant to this service scope; hybrid mismatch countermeasures operate automatically under Part XIII and the foreign affiliate regime, not by elective check-the-box.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — GST/HST registration is mandatory once worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA s.123 'small supplier'); BC PST carries no monetary registration threshold for businesses carrying on business in BC, and the PST rate is 7%.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of commercial registered-agent and offshore secretarial services to a non-resident recipient qualify for the 0% GST rate under Schedule VI, Part V, s.7 of the Excise Tax Act, provided the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada and is not rendered to an individual while the individual is in Canada; the provider must obtain and retain evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient (e.g., certificate of foreign incorporation, foreign address, service agreement with foreign governing law) and documentation that the payment is wired from an offshore account; BC PST does not apply to services supplied to non-residents for use outside BC.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) — not applicable to services; required only for tangible goods
  • CBSA proof of export documentation (carrier receipt, bill of lading / airway bill) for goods
  • Commercial invoice or service agreement expressly identifying the non-resident recipient
  • GST zero-rated supply statement and invoice showing 0% GST with the supplier's BN/RT account
  • Inward remittance certificate / SWIFT bank wire confirmation showing foreign-source funds

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval API mandate exists in Canada. CRA accepts paper and electronic invoices as books and records, but ETA s.169 and s.286(1) require the invoice to contain the supplier's name and GST/HST registration number, date, tax amount per rate, and the recipient's name; the supplier must retain supporting documentation for six years, and the BC PST invoice rules require separate identification of PST where applicable.

Canada imposes no explicit statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for exported services; the 180-day marker represents standard export documentation retention and intercompany settlement practice (transfer pricing arm's-length settlement), as CRA may disregard transactions where no payment is received in a reasonable period. The CRA may deny zero-rating and claw back input tax credits where records fail to establish the non-resident recipient and the true export character of the service; civil liability includes arrears interest (ITA s.161 and ETA s.280), a 50% gross-negligence penalty (ITA s.163.2), penalties under ETA s.285.1 for false statements of up to CAD 25,000, ETA s.323 director liability for unpaid corporate GST, and criminal prosecution under ITA s.239 and ETA s.327 for evasion with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; BC PST false-declaration penalties can reach 25% of the tax plus interest, with a statutory four-year assessment window.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under both current and capital account transactions. There are no restrictions on the repatriation of dividends, profits, or invested capital for non-resident shareholders of British Columbia companies. Corporate payments abroad are unrestricted subject to AML verification and large-transaction reporting thresholds. Canada imposes no exchange licensing, no advance approval for inward capital injections, and no surrender requirements on export proceeds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law imposes no mandatory conversion, repatriation timeline, or surrender ratio for export proceeds. CAD is a freely floating currency; corporate entities may retain foreign currency receivables in offshore or domestic foreign-currency accounts and use them for ongoing operational settlements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (monetary authority); Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) (prudential regulation of banks); Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) (AML/ATF and cross-border transaction reporting); no dedicated foreign exchange control authority

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

High feasibility for payment-only service providers duly registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Such fintech EMIs can execute cross-border MT103-equivalent transfers, issue virtual IBANs, and provide multi-currency settlement, but are not licensed to take deposits or extend Canadian bank clearances. For corporate secretarial and registered agent undertakings, a full Schedule I bank relationship remains primary; fintech EMIs serve as secondary liquidity accelerators for low-value disbursements and peer-to-peer jurisdictional settlements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocol: all shareholder equity injections must be wired via SWIFT MT103 from the shareholder account to the BC company's operating account held at a Canadian Schedule I bank or an authorized MSB partner. Beneficiary account name must match the exact corporate legal name in the BC corporate register. Remitter must be the registered shareholder (institution or individual); third-party remitters are only accepted with a countersigned equity subscription resolution and source-of-funds declaration. Transactions must be annotated with purpose code 'S010 – Equity Investment' or the bank-specific code for inward direct investment, plus the B.C. incorporation number and a reference to the resolution date. For transactions at or above CAD $10,000, FINTRAC suspicious transaction / large cash threshold reporting applies; for wire remittances above CAD $10,000 the bank initiates cross-border wire transaction reporting. Canadian banks must also complete EWIR (Electronic Funds Transfer report) for SWIFT transactions if they follow FINTRAC EFT rules. Capital is freely disposable without exchange control approval, but evidence of source of funds (signed subscription agreement, corporate authorization, audited financials) must remain in the treasury file for at least seven years.Clearing rails for trade settlements and operational disbursements: (1) SWIFT MT103 for high-value service payments, professional fees, intercompany distributions, taxes, and trade settlement up to CAD equivalents; (2) Commercial Letter of Credit (MT700/MT707) for documentary trade settlements where secure payment against transport or delivery documents is required, typically used by the registered-agent clientele for cross-border intellectual property or legal services; (3) Electronic funds transfer (EFT) via Canada’s Retail/Systemic Clearing Rails (ACSS/LVTS) for domestic operational payments in CAD; (4) Web-based bank transfer (wire) in USD via BMO/CIBC US cross-border accounts for targets in North America. All outbound disbursements must be executed under segregated dual-authorization and reconciled monthly against service contracts and underlying commercial invoices. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal and British Columbia general corporate income tax rate of 27% (15% federal Part I general rate plus 12% BC provincial general rate); eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations pay a combined rate of 11% on active business income up to C$500,000 (9% federal plus 2% BC provincial).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the federal Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner. The rate is reduced under Canada’s income tax treaties, commonly to 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for a 10%-or-more corporate shareholder under the Canada-US treaty; other treaties may apply rates of 10% to 15% depending on ownership thresholds.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the end of the taxation year. As a separate obligation, corporate income tax balance-due is payable by the general due date of two months after fiscal year-end, except that a qualified CCPC eligible for the small business deduction has a balance-due deadline of three months after year-end. BC annual corporate registry reports are due under the BC Business Corporations Act and are not the same as the T2 deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate annual value of specified non-arm’s-length cross-border transactions with non-residents, above which the Canadian corporation must file T106 and maintain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. Country-by-country reporting obligations apply to corporate groups with consolidated annual revenue exceeding approximately C$750 million (or the threshold set out in CRA’s CbC reporting rules).

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted Cayman-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245 of the Income Tax Act and the common-law sham doctrine to disregard transactions that lack real economic substance and are undertaken mainly to avoid Canadian tax. For an offshore registered-agent and secretarial-services company, actual employees, an operating office, board meetings, underlying contracts, bank accounts, and commercially rational fee-setting are required to demonstrate substance. A BC registered office without any business activity may be treated as a shell, and the CRA may reallocate profits to the place of effective management or to the non-resident beneficiary of the services. The OECD transfer-pricing guidelines inform CRA’s choice of method; for routine administrative services, the Cost Plus Method or TNMM is commonly accepted where the Canadian entity assumes limited risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Income Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under section 239 of the Income Tax Act with fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment. The CRA may also impose a gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax and assess interest compounded daily on the full reassessment.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced in fraud or dishonest-conduct cases. Directors and officers who knowingly participated in tax evasion can be jointly assessed under director-liability provisions, ordered to make restitution, subjected to civil freezing orders, and, upon criminal conviction, receive imprisonment. Canadian enforcement authorities may restrict international travel where there is a flight risk.
Abusive Transfer Pricing

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess the transaction to the arm’s-length price and impose tax, interest, and, under subsection 247(3), a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the amount by which the transfer price was understated. Failure to file the T106 information return can result in additional penalties under section 162, and the loss of a deduction for expenses connected with non-arm’s-length services if no service was actually rendered.

Director personal liability: A director who authorizes or signs false transfer-pricing documentation is not immune by reason of corporate separate personality. In civil assessments, the CRA can sue directors for unremitted withholding taxes, and in criminal proceedings, individuals who participate in a false pricing arrangement can be prosecuted for counselling or participating in tax evasion.
Shell Company and Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: The CRA can invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under section 245 to deny the tax benefit and restore the economic transaction; the BC Director may dissolve a company for non-compliance with corporate registry filings, and a sham transaction may be treated as void for tax purposes. CRA can also levy late-filing and information-return penalties and reallocate the income to the true earner.

Director personal liability: Courts will disregard the separate legal personality of a shell company used as a cloak for fraud. Directors and beneficial owners who use the company to shield personal assets from creditors, or to facilitate tax evasion, may be held personally liable for the corporation’s tax liabilities and subjected to freezing orders, bans on acting as directors, and criminal sanctions.
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The Canadian paying corporation is liable for the full unwithheld Part XIII amount plus the failure-to-remit penalty, which is 10% of the amount not remitted, together with compound daily interest. The CRA may also assess the corporation for late or missing Part XIII remittances and deny treaty relief retroactively where documentation is defective.

Director personal liability: Every director of a Canadian corporation is jointly and severally liable, together with the corporation and other directors, for amounts the corporation failed to deduct and remit under the Income Tax Act. The CRA can issue a Director’s Liability Assessment and enforce collection against the director’s personal assets without first exhausting all corporate remedies.
Anti-Money Laundering and Concealment of Beneficial Ownership

Statutory sanction: Criminal money-laundering facilitation is punishable under the Criminal Code of Canada; penalties include imprisonment and substantial fines. In the FINTRAC compliance context, administrative monetary penalties can be imposed on reporting entities for deficient compliance programs. A registered agent engaged in false filings can also be prosecuted for forgery, false statements, or fraud upon the public registrar.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in, or turn a blind eye to, nominee-entity concealment face criminal liability, personal fines, prohibition orders, and confiscation of proceeds. The corporate veil will not shield an individual who uses a company as the instrumentality of money laundering or fraud.
