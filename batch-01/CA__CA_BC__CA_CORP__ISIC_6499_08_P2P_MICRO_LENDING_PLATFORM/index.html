Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada); portal: CRA My Business Account — https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; BC provincial income tax and PST administration coordinated through CRA and the BC Ministry of Finance

E-government filing portal

Non-resident and foreign-owned corporations carrying on business in Canada must register for a federal Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO). Registration includes obtaining the BN, GST/HST account, import/export account, and payroll deductions account if applicable. A foreign entity with no Canadian permanent establishment but earning Canadian-source income must still obtain a BN and file a T2 corporation return or Part XIII withholding return, as applicable. For British Columbia, the BC corporate registry number is obtained at incorporation; income tax registration is through CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Canadian-incorporated Business Corporation is a separate taxable entity subject to federal Part I corporate income tax and BC corporate income tax. Non-resident corporations are taxable in Canada only on Canada-source business income, certain Canadian-source passive income, and gains from dispositions of taxable Canadian property; BC tax is allocated when a permanent establishment exists in British Columbia. No pass-through tax treatment is available to corporations.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists under Canadian tax law; entity classification follows the legal form. Relevant elections include: choice of fiscal period, Section 216 election for non-resident rental income, Section 217 election for non-resident social assistance or pension income, hybrid-mismatch designation/election under subsection 18.4(6), and various GST/HST elections (e.g., quick method, quarterly filing, financial institution elections) available to registrants. Cross-border structures must also analyze treaty permanent-establishment relief and the Canada–US entity mismatch rules under sections 18.4 and 18.5.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

12%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in worldwide annual taxable supplies (small supplier threshold); BC PST: CAD 30,000 in worldwide annual taxable sales (small seller threshold). A non-resident seller must register once thresholds are exceeded or when making taxable supplies in Canada that are not zero-rated; marketplace and platform rules may deem operators to make the underlying supply in certain circumstances.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST/HST registrant may zero-rate exported goods when the supplier exports the goods or the goods are delivered outside Canada to a non-resident. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated when the service is supplied for use exclusively outside Canada and meets the Schedule VI, Part II criteria. For the P2P micro-lending platform, pure financial services are generally exempt from GST/HST rather than zero-rated; separately identifiable administrative, loan-application-processing, and back-office management services supplied to non-resident lenders may qualify for zero-rating only if the supply is not a financial service and is destined for foreign consumption. BC PST does not apply to exempt financial services and does not typically tax exported services or intangibles delivered outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA export reporting form A8/A6 or B13),
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent evidence of export (e.g., air waybill, cargo manifest)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank remittance / settlement evidence proving Canadian dollar or foreign currency receipt from the non-resident customer
  • Commercial invoice identifying the zero-rated supply and its foreign destination
  • Original loan, underwriting, and platform agreements demonstrating the foreign service recipient and foreign use
  • Non-resident status evidence, including a treaty residence certificate where applicable

Canada does not operate a national real-time fiscal clearance or continuous transaction control regime. There is no mandatory e-invoicing clearance, digital stamping, or government API pre-approval requirement. CRA mandates electronic GST/HST return filing for most registrants and supports GST/HST NETFILE and EDI/XML interfaces. Invoices must contain the required disclosure information under section 169 of the Excise Tax Act and must be retained for six years.

CRA may deny zero-rating if the exporter cannot produce contemporaneous documentation proving export or foreign use. Retrospective reassessment normally extends four years, but can extend to eight years for gross negligence or fraud. False statements or misleading export claims attract penalties under section 280 and 285 of the Excise Tax Act (including penalties up to 50% of the net tax sought), the Income Tax Act section 163.2 gross-negligence penalty, and BC PST penalties. Interest runs from the original return date. Export documents must be retained for six years; failure to repatriate consideration or use artificial intermediary invoicing will increase the risk of simulated-transaction recharacterization and criminal tax evasion prosecution.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The Canadian dollar is freely floating without exchange-control licensing or rationing. Profit remittance to non-resident investors is unrestricted; however, Part XIII withholding tax applies to dividends (generally 25%, reduced to 5–15% under tax treaties) and certain interest payments. The Investment Canada Act may review foreign acquisitions exceeding statutory thresholds but does not impede ordinary treasury or loan-funding flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds surrender requirements are statutorily non-existent in Canada. Exporters may hold foreign currency receipts in any freely convertible account with no mandated conversion timeline or ratio. The mandatory conversion ratio is 0%; no exporter is required to repatriate proceeds to the Bank of Canada. Only Statistics Canada's balance-of-payments survey may require aggregate export-value reporting for statistical use.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the monetary authority; the Department of Finance Canada supervises foreign exchange policy, while OSFI supervises prudential bank conduct and FINTRAC administers anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing obligations for FX-related banking flows.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Low without local structure; Medium if combined with a British Columbia-regulated lending entity and FINTRAC-registered money services business. Canada does not operate an EU-style electronic money institution (EMI) passport. A foreign EMI is not authorised to take deposits under the Bank Act; it must use a Schedule I Canadian bank as settlement partner and must comply with provincial consumer-lending licensing under British Columbia's Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.00% combined for a general non-CCPC corporation (15.00% federal Part I general rate + 12.00% British Columbia provincial corporate rate); a qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may reduce effective federal/BC rates to 11.00% on the first C$500,000 of active business income (9.00% federal + 2.00% BC small business rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000; a Canadian resident (or non-resident with a permanent establishment in Canada) must prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for all transactions with non-resident non-arm’s length parties where the aggregate fair market value of such transactions exceeds C$1,000,000 in the taxation year; multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least C$750,000,000 must file a Country-by-Country Report

Economic substance scope: Canada does not enact separate British Columbia Economic Substance Regulations; the CRA enforces substance through the common law sham doctrine, the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule in section 245, and treaty abuse rules. A P2P micro-lending platform must demonstrate real economic presence in BC by maintaining staff, credit underwriting decision-making, customer service, risk-management infrastructure, and physical/IT operational capability; transactions that lack realistic economic substance and confer a tax benefit may be recharacterized, with penalties and adverse treaty-risk findings

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Returns

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment with interest; gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of any understated tax; criminal conviction punishable by up to C$500,000 in fines and imprisonment for up to five years; compulsory disclosure orders and seizure of corporate records

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly participate or fail to prevent false returns may be jointly and severally liable for corporate tax arrears under statutory director-liability provisions; CRA may freeze personal bank accounts, obtain court orders for civil restitution, and a conviction can trigger travel restrictions through identity flags in law-enforcement systems
Shell Company and Artificial Interposition

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization under section 245, denial of treaty benefits, disallowance of expense deductions, reassessed taxes plus interest, and a 25% GAAR penalty on the tax benefit; provincial corporate capital/minimum tax reassessment may also follow

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a mere agent or sham, holding directors accountable for misrepresentations to lenders and creditors; civil restitution of wrongfully extracted loan proceeds, equitable damages, or disgorgement may be ordered, and fraudulent-conveyance orders can restrict international travel and personal asset transfers
Anti-Money Laundering (AML/CFT) Breach

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties of up to C$2,000,000 per violation for corporations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; possible suspension or revocation of the statutory provincial licence in BC and exclusion from banking/payment-rail integrations; referral to the BC Securities Commission or privacy regulator in data-breach cases

Director personal liability: Directors and named officers can personally receive monetary penalties of up to C$500,000 if they knowingly or recklessly fail to implement the AML compliance program; serious money-laundering predicate offences may result in criminal charges, travel bans, and freezing of personal assets pursuant to civil forfeiture and terrorist-financing statutes
Non-Resident Withholding and Information Return Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess the payer for 25% of the gross payment as withholding tax, plus interest and late-filing penalties; the payer remains liable even if funds were never withheld; Form NR4 and T106 penalties accumulate per slip or transaction; double-counting may occur inside branch tax computation

Director personal liability: Directors are specifically liable under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted source withholding amounts; CRA may issue director liability proceedings, depart-permit denials under the Income Tax Act, garnishment of directors’ bank and pension accounts, and travel bans where the directors attempt to leave Canada with unpaid statutory debts
Thin Capitalization and Excessive Interest Shifting

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions are disallowed under the Canadian thin capitalization rules in subsection 18(4), compounded by transfer-pricing adjustments, gross negligence penalties of 50% on unreported allocations, and application of the excessive interest and financing expenses limitation (EIFEL) regime to deny EBITDA-based interest deductibility; reassessed taxes carry compound interest

Director personal liability: Although the tax adjustment itself is against the corporation, directors who sign false certificates, backdate loan agreements, or represent that thin-capitalization computations are correct may be liable to CRA for unpaid corporate tax if the company is dissolved or becomes insolvent during the reassessment period; BC insolvency laws permit recovery against directors who authorize preferential payments to foreign shareholders ahead of tax debt
