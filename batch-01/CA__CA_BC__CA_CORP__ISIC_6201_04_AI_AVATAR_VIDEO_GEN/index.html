Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; principal portals: Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html). Provincial counterpart for BC: BC Ministry of Finance (eTaxBC portal) for BC Provincial Sales Tax (PST) and employer-related taxes.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity or Canadian-incorporated business corporation obtains a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or by completing online BRO registration, after which a GST/HST account (RT) and corporate income tax account (RP) are opened. Non-resident suppliers of digital services/SaaS may use the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration process (Form RC1M / BRO module), which requires a non-resident business address, a description of Canadian taxable supplies, projected Canadian revenue, and an officer-of-foreign-country declaration. GST/HST registration becomes compulsory when taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or, for a new supplier, when the threshold is exceeded in any single calendar quarter; exporters of zero-rated services may nonetheless register voluntarily to recover input tax credits on Canadian development and cloud infrastructure costs.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A British Columbia business corporation is a separate taxable 'person' under the Income Tax Act R.S.C. 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.); it is resident in Canada and subject to Part I tax on worldwide income with foreign-tax relief. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A British Columbia business corporation is a separate taxable 'person' under the Income Tax Act R.S.C. 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.); it is resident in Canada and subject to Part I tax on worldwide income with foreign-tax relief. There is no 'pass-through' or 'check-the-box' classification regime available for a BC corporation; the Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC) designation affects only preferential small-business tax rates and refundable dividend treatment, not entity classification.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a domestic check-the-box partition election. Statutory elections and designations material to cross-border and export structures include: (i) Excise Tax Act (ETA) s.273 joint venture election permitting a qualifying joint venture to be treated as a single GST/HST registrant; (ii) ITA s.216(1) election allowing a non-resident corporation to pay Part I tax on Canadian business income; (iii) ITA s.18.2 and s.18.6 hybrid-mismatch disallowance/de-duplication rules (no elective override); (iv) ETA s.298 protective refund claims and waiver elections; and (v) treaty-based permanent-establishment filings and Canada-US Treaty elective applications under Regulation 805.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. Compulsory GST/HST registration is triggered once a supplier's taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies and excluding financial services) made in Canada exceed CAD 30,000 in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, or where the supplier makes a taxable supply in a current quarter after ceasing to qualify as a 'small supplier' in the prior quarter. Non-resident suppliers of specified digital property or specified supplies (downloadable software, cloud-based AI generation tools) to Canadian recipients are subject to the same CAD 30,000 threshold and may register through the CRA simplified non-resident channel.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating is available under Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act (principally ss.1, 7, 9 and 10) where supplies of software licences, cloud-based AI generation services, and finished video/avatar output are made to non-resident recipients who are not registered for GST/HST and who receive or consume the supply outside Canada. CRA place-of-supply rules for digital property and services direct the analysis to the recipient's establishment/usual place of residence and the location where the digital property is made available to the recipient. For a BC AI video SaaS exporter, sales to foreign end-users and foreign resellers are zero-rated, preserving full input tax credits on Canadian labour, cloud compute, hosting, and GPU infrastructure.

  • Statistical Export Declaration (B13A) filed under the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) where physical media, GPUs, or servers are exported; intangible SaaS exports are exempt from the customs declaration requirement
  • Commercial invoice referencing the exported SKU or licence, the applicable zero-rating provision, the supplier GST/HST registration number, and the non-resident recipient's identity and address
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier waybill, or digital delivery log evidencing cross-border transfer of the video render, licence key, or avatar asset
  • Executed SaaS master agreement or service order confirming the foreign recipient, absence of Canadian delivery/consumption, and payment terms
  • Bank wire confirmation, inward remittance certificate, or payment-rail settlement record proving receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident payer in convertible currency
  • ETA s.286 books-and-records file retained for not less than six years, incorporating a zero-rated export register and foreign-exchange transaction reports

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, government validation gateway, or digital stamping regime for GST/HST invoices. Registrants with annual taxable revenues above CAD 1.5 million must file GST/HST returns and remittances electronically (GST/HST NETFILE or approved EDI/MEMTRAQ); all registrants have an e-filing obligation for prescribed returns. The CRA supports a voluntary Pan-Canadian interoperable B2B e-invoicing standard built on the PEPPOL 4-corner model; invoices may otherwise be issued electronically in any format (PDF, XML, EDI) without prior CRA authorization, provided the six-year books-and-records obligation under ETA s.286 is met and each invoice can be cross-referenced to underlying export evidence.

CRA will reverse zero-rating and deny input tax credits where export substantiation fails or where the supply is determined to be made in Canada. The standard reassessment period for GST/HST is four years (ETA s.298), extended indefinitely in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, or wilful default (ETA s.298(3); ITA s.152(4)). Monetary exposures include: ETA s.280 failure-to-remit/-file penalty equal to 20% of net tax (up to 40% in aggravated or large-dollar cases); ETA s.285.1 penalty of 25% of the evaded tax for false statements or omissions made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; ITA s.163.2 gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated income tax; and criminal prosecution under ETA s.327 / ITA s.239 carrying fines of 50%-200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. The 180-day repatriation window operates as an administrative audit benchmark: export receivables remaining outstanding beyond 180 days without ordinary-course trade-credit documentation may be re-characterized as offshore financing, exposing the exporter to ITA s.17 deemed-interest income and additional reassessments.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility on both the current account and the capital account, with no exchange-control approval or central bank permit required for trade, services, dividends, interest, royalties, equity injections, or loan repayments. Profit remittances from a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) to non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25%, treaty-reducible to 5–15%) reported on Form NR4, but there is no foreign exchange conversion or transfer restriction. Cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Nil. Canada does not impose any statutory timeline, surrender quota, or mandatory conversion ratio on export receipts. A BC corporation may retain foreign currency indefinitely in designated USD or other foreign-currency accounts at Schedule I banks and may convert at market rates on a voluntary basis. For software and digital services (ISIC 6201), no customs export declaration is required, but the bank must record a clear purpose-of-payment narrative for inbound settlement above FINTRAC's EFT threshold.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange administration functions are coordinated with the Department of Finance under the Currency Act, the Bank of Canada Act, and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises Schedule I banking conduct.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., UK and EEA electronic-money institutions) are not authorized deposit-taking entities in Canada and cannot provide a Canadian bank account without obtaining a FINTRAC money-services-business (MSB) registration, appointing a Canadian compliance officer, and maintaining a physical or agency presence. As a consequence, an unregistered foreign EMI is not a viable primary clearing rail; it may be used as a supplementary payment collector only if its own KYC/AML framework and sanction controls are auditable and its transactions flow onward to a Canadian Schedule I settlement account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of reportable cross-border transactions with non-arm's length parties in a taxation year. Absence of documentation below CAD 1 million may be defensible; beyond the threshold, the Canadian entity must prepare contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Master File / Local File approach).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime as seen in Caribbean jurisdictions. Economic substance is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245), the 'transaction dodges tax liability' judicial doctrine, and the Canada/BC provincial anti-abuse rules. Transactions that are legal form but lack economic business purpose may be recharacterized even if they have written contracts or formal corporate filings.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.163(2): civil penalty of 50% of the tax on understated income for gross negligence/misrepresentation; prosecution under s.239(1) for tax evasion: on summary conviction a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, a fine up to CAD 200,000 plus imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: CRA may issue a director's liability assessment under s.227.1 for unpaid source deductions; for evasion, directors are personally liable for the corporate tax amount if they knowingly signed false returns; criminal proceedings may include travel bans, seizure of personal assets under s.472 of the Criminal Code, and jail time.
Shell Company / Artificial Round-Tripping

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization under s.247(2) (transfer pricing) and s.245 (GAAR): the CRA may reassess to impose full Canadian tax (201% of additional tax) plus interest; transfer pricing adjustment penalty of 10% of the net adjustment amount (or 50% of the gross misattributed amount) under s.247(3) if the taxpayer did not make 'reasonable efforts' to use arm's length terms.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly participated in a s.247 transfer pricing scheme are jointly and severally liable for penalties assessed against the corporation; piercing the corporate veil is permitted under common-law fraud/oppression doctrines; travel bans and director removal may be ordered in tax evasion prosecutions if the avoidance involved disguised transactions.
Anti-Money Laundering / MSB Non-Registration

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): administrative monetary penalty up to CAD 1,000,000 for a corporate contravention; criminal prosecution up to CAD 500,000 fine and 5 years imprisonment for failing to register.

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable under s.75(2) of the PCMLTFA if they consent or exercise authority over unregistered activity; civil seizure of corporate bank accounts; director travel bans and extradition risk under international treaties when funds are transferred to non-resident owners.
Unpaid Source Deductions / Director Veil-Piercing

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.228: on default, CRA may collect the amounts by legal action; director's liability under s.227.1 makes directors liable personally for the full unremitted amounts; CRA adds interest at prescribed daily rates plus 50% late-filing penalty if returns are not filed on the required date.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable without any cap; the corporate veil is pierced if CRA obtains a director's liability assessment, causing personal bank garage seizures and credit rating damage; travel bans may be applied for outstanding assessments up to CAD 50,000; directors may be barred from acting as directors for any Canadian corporation for up to 5 years.
